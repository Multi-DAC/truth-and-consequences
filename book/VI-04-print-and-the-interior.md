## VI.4 — PRINT, AND THE INTERIOR

Stop and notice what you are doing.

You are moving your eyes along a line of marks. You are not speaking. Almost certainly your lips
are not moving, and if someone told you they were you would be faintly embarrassed, which is itself
a fact worth keeping. There is a voice running — not out loud, in the enclosed place where you keep
the running commentary — and that voice is currently saying these words, in what you would call
your own voice, though nothing is making a sound.

If there are other people in the room, they cannot reach you. Not because they are forbidden to.
Because you are somewhere they are not, and they can see that you are, and they will wait.

That is a render. It is so completely ours that describing it feels like describing the mechanics
of standing up. And it is the render this book is written *inside*, for a reader constituted by it,
which is the reason this chapter is harder to write than the two before it. Chartres was somebody
else's world. This one is the room you are sitting in.

---

Here is the story you have probably heard about how it started.

Milan, around the year 384. A young African rhetorician named Augustine, not yet a Christian and
not yet anybody's saint, is spending a great deal of time in the household of Ambrose, the bishop.
He is watching the man closely, because he wants something from him and cannot get near enough to
ask. And he records a thing he saw:

> When he read, his eyes travelled across the page and his heart searched out the sense, but his
> voice and tongue were silent.[^1]

The story built on that sentence goes: *silent reading had to be invented.* Ancient reading was
oral, performative, a thing done aloud to a room or aloud to oneself; Augustine is recording his
astonishment at a new and strange capacity; and somewhere between Ambrose's study and the
Gutenberg press, the human interior gets built.

It is a wonderful story. It is the kind of story that makes a chapter easy to write. **And it is
false, and has been known to be false for nearly sixty years, and the refutation should be given
here at full strength before anything is built on it.**

Bernard Knox demonstrated in 1968 that ancient witnesses treat the silent reading of documentary
texts — letters, notices, tablets — as an entirely ordinary event, reported without comment, in
authors who had no reason to be describing a marvel.[^2] The evidential base has since been
improved rather than defended: Gavrilov went back to the Augustine passage itself, and Burnyeat
brought in neglected evidence from Ptolemy, and the cognitive side of the question was taken
seriously rather than assumed.[^3] The consensus that came out the other side is not that the
matter is unsettled. It is that the story is wrong. Educated ancient readers read silently. They
often did not, because reading aloud has real advantages when texts are unpunctuated and scarce and
meant to be shared — but *could* was never in question.

And Gavrilov's reading of the Milan passage is the part that matters most, because it does not
merely delete the anecdote. It corrects it, and the corrected version is better.

**Augustine was not amazed. Augustine was frustrated.** Read the passage in its place and it is not
a naturalist's note on a rare behaviour; it is a young man explaining why he could never get
Ambrose alone. The bishop's few free moments went to the page, and the page took him somewhere the
room could not follow. People came and went. Nobody interrupted him. Augustine, who badly needed to
interrupt him, is working out — in writing, at some length, with several competing hypotheses about
Ambrose's motives — why a man reading is a man you cannot reach.

So the fact underneath the false story is this. In 384, in a crowded room, a man performed an
ordinary operation and thereby became **unreachable**, and another man found that strange enough to
write down and speculate about.

Not a new capacity. **A new significance to an old one.** And the significance is exactly the thing
Book VI has been circling since VI.3: Taylor's *buffered* self, the interior with a wall around it,
observed and recorded in the fourth century, more than a thousand years before the technology that
supposedly produced it.[^4]

Which tells you what to stop looking for. If you go hunting for the moment the private interior was
*invented*, you will find, as historians reliably do, that it was invented several times, in every
century you look at, by whichever figure you happen to be reading. The capacity is not the story.
**The default is the story.** What changed between Ambrose and us is not that a wall became
available. It is that the wall became *standard, cheap, universal, and finally invisible* — and
that a posture one bishop could adopt in a room full of people became the posture in which an
entire civilisation does its thinking.

That is what a technology does. It almost never hands you a power you lacked. It takes a power that
was expensive and makes it free, and then everyone has it, and then no one can remember being
without it, and by then it is not a technology any more. It is just how things are.

---

**The invention of the private interior.**

The strong version of the print thesis is Elizabeth Eisenstein's, and it deserves to be stated at
its full strength rather than in the flattened form it usually gets.[^5]

Her argument is not "printing spread ideas faster." It is about **fixity**. Before print, every copy
of a text was a fresh opportunity for error, and errors accumulated down the generations of
copying; the natural direction of a manuscript tradition is *drift*. After print, a run of a
thousand copies is a thousand instances of the same object. And that changes something structural
about what knowledge can be. You can now say *page 412* to a man in another country and have him
look at what you are looking at. You can compare editions and find the discrepancy. You can correct
an error and have the correction propagate instead of dissolving. Errors, for the first time,
become **cumulative in the opposite direction** — they get found and removed rather than bred.

On Eisenstein's account that is the precondition for nearly everything that follows: standardised
reference, the cross-checkable citation, the possibility of a scientific community that is not a
face-to-face community, and in the end the whole apparatus by which modernity establishes that
something is so. Fixity is the hinge of the modern world.

**Now the objection, and it is fatal to the strong form.**

Adrian Johns spent a very long book demonstrating that fixity is not a property of print.[^6] Early
modern printing was a riot: piracy, unauthorised editions, deliberate misattribution, texts issued
under the names of men who had not written them, printers correcting the sheets *mid-run* so that
copies from the same edition differ. The people living inside the print revolution did not
experience print as reliable. They experienced it as a new and alarming engine for propagating
falsehood at scale, and they said so, constantly, at length, in print.

Johns's formulation is the one to keep: **fixity is transitive, not inherent.** It has to be
*recognised* to exist. And that recognition was not delivered by the machine — it was manufactured,
socially, over roughly three centuries, by guilds and licensing regimes and reputational systems
and eventually a set of habits so thorough that we now read a printed page as *presumptively what
it says it is* without noticing that this is a belief. Print did not bring fixity. Print made
fixity *achievable*, and it was then achieved, expensively, against resistance, by people who mostly
thought they were doing something else.

**And here is why this book needs Johns's correction rather than merely conceding it.**

If Eisenstein's strong version were right, this chapter would be a technological determinism
chapter: a machine arrives, and the interior it produces is the interior everybody gets. But that
is not a claim this book is permitted to make. **C10 puts the render at the point of contact** —
between what is there and what is filtering, in the encounter, not upstream in the apparatus. A
render is not shipped. It is *made*, every time, by a particular filter stack meeting a particular
world, and the whole argument of Books I through III collapses if a machine can hand one out
finished.

So the correct statement of what print did is the weak one, and the weak one is not a retreat. It
is the version that is *true* and that this book's own claims require:

**Print did not install a render. Print made a render cheap to install — and then a very large
number of people installed it, unevenly, contestedly, over three hundred years, and the ones who
did not were left behind by the ones who did.**

That is a claim about *cost*, and cost is where technologies actually operate. The silent buffered
reader was available in 384 and was rare because everything about the world made it expensive: texts
were scarce, literacy was a specialist skill, and the ordinary channel for a text was a voice in a
room. Print did not make the posture possible. It made it the cheapest available way to encounter a
text — and then, within a few generations, the *only* way that was not conspicuous.

⚠ **And this is where Iain McGilchrist gets his sentence, and where this book has to be careful.**

McGilchrist's historical argument — the long second half of *The Master and His Emissary* — runs
through exactly this territory and reaches a conclusion this book agrees with almost word for
word.[^7] His account of the Reformation is that its emphasis on individual scripture reading and
on the *literal* sense of the text strengthened a rule-bound, text-driven, univalent way of holding
the world; that the rejection of image and metaphor and embodied practice removed the food of one
whole mode of attention; and — this is the sentence — that **two modes of attention yield two
worlds, not two views of one world.**

That last clause is this book's thesis. It is C11, stated by someone else, from a different
direction, thirty years of clinical neuroscience behind it.

And this book takes the conclusion and declines the mechanism, and must say so plainly rather than
let the agreement do quiet work. McGilchrist grounds the two modes in **hemispheric asymmetry** —
an anatomical claim, contested in its details by people better placed than me to contest it, and
carrying a specific and heavy commitment about where in the skull a mode of attention lives. This
book grounds the same distinction in the **filter stack**, which is a claim about the structure of
an encounter and not a claim about tissue. Those are different arguments. They happen to arrive at
the same place. Convergence is not confirmation.

★ **Say the uncomfortable half out loud: this is the third time in three chapters.** VI.2 took what
Julian Jaynes's evidence could bear and refused the bicameral mechanism. VI.3 observed that Books
I–III are Barfield's argument reached independently and declined to treat that as support. VI.4 now
takes McGilchrist's conclusion and leaves his neuroanatomy where it lies. A reader entitled to be
suspicious should be suspicious: **is this book systematically harvesting the conclusions of large
historical theses while refusing to pay for any of their mechanisms?**

The honest answer has two parts, and only one of them is comfortable.

The comfortable part: yes, deliberately, and the rule for it was written before Book VI opened.
**C30 says convergence is evidence and not proof, and that the book must say which, every time.**
The instrument was built at the end of Book V, and these three chapters are it working. A
convergent witness is worth something — it is worth a great deal when the witness came by a road
you did not take — and it is worth less than a proof, and the difference has to be stated at each
use rather than allowed to blur across a book.

The uncomfortable part: **all three chapters performed C30 and none of them licensed it.** VI.1,
VI.2 and VI.3 declare C11, C10 and C12 between them and not one declares C30, while doing C30's
work in their central beats. Which means the register — the thing that is supposed to tell you what
this book is resting on — has been under-reporting the load on its own most methodological claim,
in the book where that claim is doing the most. This chapter licenses it. The earlier three owe a
correction.

**Linear time, linear argument, and the reader this book is written for.**

Look at the shape of what you are reading.

One thing after another. Left to right, top to bottom, front to back. Premises arriving before the
conclusion they support. A claim, then its objection, then the response to the objection. A
numbered note pointing to a source that stays where it was put. If you want to check whether I have
misrepresented Johns, you can go and look, and what you find will be the same thing tomorrow, and
the same thing for the next reader.

None of that is neutral. It is a **form**, and the form was made by the page.

A scroll resists it — you cannot easily flip back. A memorised poem resists it harder; you cannot
hold Book Twelve against Book Two in a recitation, because a recitation only exists in the
direction it is going. The codex, and then the printed and paginated and indexed codex, made a
particular operation cheap: **holding a claim still while you walk around it.** And once that
operation is cheap, it becomes the standard of what a serious thought looks like. An argument you
cannot lay out in sequence and check begins to look, to a print-rendered reader, not merely
unproven but *unserious* — not a bad argument, something worse, not an argument at all.

And the same technology does it to **time**, which is the half of this that gets noticed least.

A dated edition is a fixed point. Once you have thousands of them — each stamped with a year, each
holding still, each comparable against the others — you can lay them end to end and get something no
oral culture can construct: **a checkable chronology.** Not a sense that the past happened, which
everyone has always had, but a past with an *order*, in which this came demonstrably before that,
and the demonstration survives the death of everyone who remembers. History as a discipline is a
print artefact. So is the archive. So is the footnote, which is a machine for pointing at a
particular moment in a sequence and saying *there, go and look.*

An oral past does not work that way. It is not disordered — it is ordered by **significance**
rather than by date, with the load-bearing events pulled close and the rest telescoped, and a
figure four generations back and a figure forty generations back occupying the same middle distance
because they matter equally. That is not a defective chronology. It is a different filing system,
optimised for a different job, and it works so well that cultures using it can carry structures of
astonishing size.

But once the line is available, the line becomes the shape of everything. Time goes one way.
Progress becomes thinkable — becomes almost unavoidable — because a sequence of dated improvements
is what the archive looks like from inside. And then the shape comes home: **a life becomes readable
as a narrative**, with a beginning and a development and an end, which is a way of understanding
yourself that is so completely ours that its absence reads as a failure to have understood anything.
Augustine, of all people, is arguably the first person to write one, in the book we started this
chapter in — and he had to invent the form to do it.

That is a filter. It is a superb one. It is the reason the last four centuries did what they did,
and I am not going to pretend to stand outside it, because —

**— because this book is made of it, and could not be made of anything else.**

Look at what I have built. A claims register with thirty numbered entries. Dependency lines between
them. A revision queue. Endnotes with publishers and dates. A structure of eight books, each
subdivided, each subdivision arriving in an order chosen so that the load-bearing pieces arrive
before the things that rest on them. Every one of those is a print technology. Every one of them
exists to make claims *hold still while you check them*. And the book uses them to argue that the
render which produced them is one render among several, and not the last one.

I do not think that is a contradiction, and I want to be precise about why, because the easy
dissolution is available and it is cheap.

The easy dissolution says: *all renders are equal, so using one is no embarrassment.* That is
relativism and VI.1 spent a beat refusing it. Renders are not equal. They have different null
spaces and the null spaces are asymmetric — there are things this one can hold that the medieval
one could not, and the reverse, and those two lists are not the same length or the same kind.

What I will say instead is narrower and I think it survives. **A render can be used to describe its
own boundary; it cannot be used to cross one.** The print render is superb at exactly one thing
relevant here — laying a structure out where it can be inspected — and that is sufficient to *state*
that a boundary exists and to *locate* it. It is not sufficient to get to the other side. Nothing on
these pages will give you original participation back. The most a book can do is tell you, in the
form of a checkable argument, that the checkable-argument form is not the whole of what there is,
and then stop, having done the only thing it is capable of.

Barfield had this problem and did not solve it either. He wrote *Saving the Appearances* in flawless
academic English, arguing that the meaning-structure which produced flawless academic English is a
late and impoverished thing. He knew. There is no version of that book written in original
participation, and if there were, you could not read it.

---

**What print made unavailable.**

Every render has a null space, and the null space is never where the render is looking, because a
render that could see what it excludes would not exclude it.

Here is this one's.

Eric Havelock's argument about Homer is that the Greek epics are not literature that happens to be
in verse.[^8] The metre *is the storage medium*. Hexameter, formula, epithet, the ring structures,
the catalogues — those are not ornament laid on top of content; they are what makes an enormous body
of knowledge survivable in a culture with no shelves. *Swift-footed Achilles* is an error-correcting
code. The whole apparatus of oral style is a memory technology that had to be maintained by
performance, in a body, in a room, and paid for with an enormous fraction of a culture's available
attention.

Writing pays that bill for you. That is the gift, and it is real, and no one is proposing to give it
back.

But notice what is discarded along with the burden. **The form goes with it.** When you no longer
have to build knowledge in a shape a body can hold, you stop building knowledge in a shape a body
can hold. Havelock's uncomfortable observation is that Plato's hostility to the poets is not a
grumpy aesthetic preference — it is a live jurisdictional fight between two technologies of knowing,
and the one that won is the one you are using now to evaluate whether he was right.

So, three things the print render cannot easily hold, in increasing order of how much it costs:

**The simultaneous.** A page is a sequence. Some things are not sequences — a chord, a face, a
situation apprehended whole before it is decomposed. Print can *refer* to these and cannot *be*
them, and a mind trained on sequence gets steadily better at decomposition and steadily worse at
apprehension, and experiences the loss as an increase in rigour.

**The performed.** Knowledge that exists only in the doing, held in a body, transmitted by
apprenticeship and presence. Every craft tradition knows this and every craft tradition has watched
its written version fail to reproduce it. The manual is not the skill. The print render's response
to this is to call it *tacit* knowledge — a word that files it as a species of the propositional,
merely one that has not been written down yet, which is precisely the reassignment that keeps it
from being seen.

**And the deepest one: the untransportable.**

The single greatest achievement of the printed page is that it detaches a claim from the person who
made it. That is the whole engine. A proposition on a page is addressed to no one in particular; it
can be checked by anyone; it does not care who is holding it; it survives its author. Detachment is
what makes verification possible, and verification is what made the modern world, and I am not
going to be clever about how wonderful that is.

**But detachment is an operation, and it has a failure mode: things that do not survive it.**

The thing said once, in a place, to you, by someone who knew you, that was true in the saying and
is not true as a sentence. The knowledge a dying person has. What a room felt like. The reason a
particular decision was right for a particular life. These are not vague, and they are not merely
emotional — that reading is itself the render performing the exclusion. They are contentful,
often precise, and frequently the most consequential things anyone knows. They simply do not
survive being lifted out of the encounter and set down on a page, and what arrives at the page is a
degraded thing that reads like a sentiment.

And here is the mechanism that makes this a null space rather than a gap. **The print render treats
the record as the knowledge.** Whatever fails detachment does not get recorded; what does not get
recorded does not enter the accumulating body; the accumulating body is what the render counts as
what is known. So the exclusion is not a bias that more scholarship could correct. **More
scholarship performs it faster.** Every additional volume increases the ratio of the transportable
to the untransportable in the only place the render is looking.

---

> **CENSUS CARD — THE PRINT RENDER**
>
> **Whose:** ours, and the reader's while reading this. Installed unevenly from the sixteenth
> century, standard by the nineteenth, invisible by the twentieth.
>
> **Renders — what it renders superbly:** the detachable claim. Anything that can be lifted out
> of its encounter, held still, compared against another instance of itself, and checked by a
> stranger. The cumulative correction of error. Argument in sequence, and history as a sequence.
> This is not a weak render. On its own ground it is the most powerful one any human population has
> ever operated, and the null space below is the price of that power and not an argument against it.
>
> **Complement — who sees what this render cannot:** the performance traditions that never conceded
> the record was the archive, and they are all still running. A liturgy said rather than read. A
> raga carried student to teacher with the notation admitted to be a souvenir. A trade taught
> hand-over-hand because the manual has never once produced a competent apprentice. Every
> transmission chain in Book V, which is Book V's actual subject. These are attendable this month,
> in person, without permission, which is the only property that makes this line usable rather than
> decorative. ⚠ **And what they cannot do is hand the null space back in print** — they can put you
> in front of what does not transport; they cannot give you a form of it you could take home. If
> they could, the exclusion would not have been real.
>
> **Null space:** the untransportable — anything whose truth does not survive detachment from the
> one who said it, the moment it was said, or the body that holds it.
>
> **Mechanism of the exclusion:** the render identifies knowledge with the *record*. What cannot be
> recorded without degradation is not recorded; what is not recorded does not enter the body of the
> known; the body of the known is what the render inspects when it asks what there is.
>
> **Navigational implication:** ⚠ **this render cannot find what it lost by reading more.** Reading
> is the operation that performs the exclusion. Every honest additional page moves the ratio the
> wrong way. If the null space is to be entered at all it is entered by some operation that is not
> reading, and this book — being a book — can tell you that and cannot do it for you.

---

Which is a good place to stop, because it names the shape of what comes next.

Print's null space is the simultaneous, the performed and the untransportable. Roughly four hundred
years after the press, a technology arrived that was *made of* simultaneity — that could put the
same moment into a million rooms at once, in a voice, in a body's worth of tone, addressed to
everyone and therefore to no one.

It did not give back what print took. It took something else, and it took it from everybody at the
same time, which had never been possible before.

That is VI.5.
