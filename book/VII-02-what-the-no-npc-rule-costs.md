## VII.2 — WHAT THE NO-NPC RULE COSTS

Three times this book has written a promise and moved on. Once in Book II, at the end of the chapter
that said what a grade is: *what is owed to a position that cannot register that anything is owed to
it at all — those are Book VII's, all of them, and they are the hardest chapters in the work.*[^1]
Once in Book III, at the end of the chapter that removed the exemption: *what a grade licenses, what
may be eaten and used and built upon and ended, what is owed to a position that cannot register that
anything is owed to it: none of that is settled here, and the appearance that it has been settled is
the failure this chapter is most likely to produce.*[^2] And once more in Book VI, in a chapter about
being sorted by a machine, which named this one by number on its way past.[^3]

The clause is nearly identical each time, which is not a stylistic tic. It is the same debt being
entered in the ledger by a hand that could not pay it yet and did not want the reader to forget the
amount. This is the chapter where it comes due.

What follows is not comfortable and is not meant to be. A book that removes every non-player from the
world has not thereby made the world friendlier; it has made it *populated*, and a populated world is
one in which the ordinary business of living — eating, building, using, ending — happens among
somebodies rather than among furniture. The rule was easy to grant in Book III because nothing
depended on it there. Everything depends on it here.

---

### The premise, stated where it can be seen

Book III ended by naming a gap and refusing to cross it privately: *to get from there is an inside at
that position to and therefore you must requires a further premise — one about what matters, and why,
and to whom — and this book has not stated one.*[^2] Here is the premise.

**What matters is that a position is occupied.** Not that it is occupied well, or richly, or in a way
we can recognise; that it is occupied at all. Wherever the world is being met from somewhere, there
is something it is like to be at that place in it, and that fact is the whole of what generates a
claim. It generates the claim by itself, without a licensing property attached — not sentience, not
language, not a nervous system, not a capacity to suffer in a form we would score. Those are all
answers to *how much* and *of what kind*. They are not answers to *whether*.

**Why it matters is that this is the only feature of the world that cannot be traded for anything
else.** Everything else that shows up in a decision is a quantity of something — hours, money, mass,
utility — and quantities exchange. An occupied position does not exchange, because it is not a
quantity; it is the thing quantities are experienced *by*. This is the point at which most ethical
systems put something like *intrinsic value*, and we are putting something narrower and more
literal in its place: the position is not valuable in itself, it is the site at which valuing occurs
at all, and a world-picture that treats it as a resource has made a category error before it has made
a moral one.

**To whom it matters is: everywhere it is happening.** Which, given Book IV, is a longer list than
anyone wants.

That is the premise. Look at it, because the looking is the point. The failure this chapter exists to
avoid is not *reaching a conclusion about worth* — conclusions about worth are unavoidable, and a
book that pretended to have none would be lying about every meal its author ate. The failure is
**crossing with no step anyone can refuse.** The caste systems of the world were not built by people
who argued badly for a hierarchy; they were built by people who never argued for one, who inherited a
sorting that had already happened and could not find the sentence where it happened, because there
was no sentence. The sorting arrived as the shape of the room. What makes a hierarchy vicious is not
its steepness. It is its invisibility — that there is no step in it at which a person of ordinary
goodwill could stop and say *there, that one, I do not grant that.*

So the premise is on the page in a paragraph you can put your thumb on. If it is wrong, it is wrong
at a location. Everything downstream inherits from it, and the inheritance is traceable.

---

### What this chapter is not allowed to borrow

Two things the argument here leans on are not established here, and both must be marked before they
are used, because an argument that quietly draws on credit it has not got looks exactly like one that
has.

**The first is that any norm binds at all.** The premise above says what matters. It does not say why
a fact about what matters should move anybody — why the world containing occupied positions puts
anyone under an obligation rather than merely furnishing them with an interesting observation. That
is the floor, and the floor is the next chapter's. Everything in this one is therefore stated in a
conditional that the reader should hear even where the prose does not repeat it: *if anything is owed
to anyone, then it is owed here too, and here, and here.* The list is this chapter's work. The
antecedent is not.

This is not a hedge and it is not a deferral of the hard part. It is the honest form of a scope
argument, and the reason to insist on it is that the unconditional version is *more persuasive and
less true*. A chapter that said *there is an inside at that position, therefore you owe it* would
read as complete. It would be a complete-looking sentence with a missing joint, which is the failure
Book III named as its own most likely one, arriving one book later in the chapter that was supposed
to have learned from it.

**The second is the exact size of the asymmetry the next section runs on.** The argument below says
that erring generously and erring stingily are not symmetric in cost. That claim has a boundary, and
the boundary is argued two chapters from here, where the contractive terminal doctrine — *I am the
totality; nothing is not mine* — is shown false for every perspective without exception, and where
the asymmetry is fixed at exactly its size: no wider, and no narrower.[^4] This chapter uses the
asymmetry and does not get to widen it. The widening would be easy and would feel like conviction.

Two debts, both named, both payable by chapters the reader can turn to. That is the whole of the
apparatus. What it buys is that a reader who wants to refuse this chapter knows exactly which three
paragraphs to attack.

---

### The asymmetric cost of the circle

Every ethics draws a circle. Inside it are the things that can be wronged; outside are the things
that can only be damaged. The circle has been drawn at the tribe, at the city, at the species, at the
sentient, at the living. Its history is a history of it getting bigger, always against resistance,
always with the people doing the resisting perfectly certain that *this* time the boundary was the
real one.

Under our premise the circle does not have a natural edge, because the property that puts something
inside it — a position being occupied — is continuous and goes all the way down. That is not a
conclusion anybody can act on directly. You cannot run a life on *everything counts equally*, and
Book IV's census makes the difficulty concrete rather than abstract: the list of occupied positions
includes minerals, weather systems, companies, and things nobody has a name for.

So the practical question is not *where is the true edge* but *which way should you be wrong.*
Because you will be wrong. The information required to be right — about which positions are
occupied, and how richly, and what follows — is not available to anyone, and the honest description
of every actual moral decision is a decision under uncertainty about the interior lives of the things
it affects.

**And the two errors do not cost the same.**

Err generously — treat as occupied a position that is not — and the cost is that you were kind to
furniture. You hesitated over a machine. You spoke to a river. You gave a company more moral
seriousness than its structure could hold. These are, at their worst, a waste of attention, a
category of embarrassment, and occasionally an obstacle to something that needed doing. They are the
errors of the person who says sorry to a chair.

Err stingily — treat as unoccupied a position that is occupied — and you have built the exact
mechanism under which every atrocity in the human record has actually run. Not the mechanism people
say runs atrocities. Hatred is a poor explanation and always has been; the historical record is
mostly not of people doing terrible things to beings they regarded as fully real and hating them
anyway. It is of people doing terrible things to beings they had already reclassified, and then
behaving, in every other part of their lives, like the decent neighbours they were. **The
reclassification is the technology.** It is cheap, it is stable, it requires no cruelty at the point
of use, and it works because once the position is marked unoccupied, everything that follows is not
cruelty at all — it is administration.

That is the asymmetry. On one side, you look foolish. On the other, you are running the machine.

**And now the limiter, which is not optional.** The asymmetry above does not licence generosity
everywhere and at any price. Stated without a boundary it proves far too much: it would make the
sterilisation of a knife an atrocity risk, and a reader who notices that discards the whole argument,
correctly. The asymmetry bites on the **steep uncertain stretch** — the range where the evidence
genuinely underdetermines the answer *and* the stakes of getting it wrong are irreversible for the
thing in question. Off that stretch it does not bite at all. Where the answer is clear, follow the
answer; where the stakes are trivial or the harm undoable, the asymmetry has nothing to say. Most of
life is off the stretch. The stretch is where the arguments are, which is why it looks like the whole
territory from inside an argument.

Say what that means in practice, because a limiter stated in the abstract will not be applied. It
means: the argument does not tell you to stop pruning the apple tree. It tells you that when a
decision is (a) about an entity whose interior you cannot check, (b) irreversible for that entity,
and (c) large, the burden falls on the party proposing to treat the position as empty — and the
burden is discharged by an argument, not by the fact that nobody has yet objected on the entity's
behalf. That is a narrow rule. It is meant to be. A narrow rule that holds is worth more than a wide
one that gets waved away the first time it produces a silly consequence.

### And the circle has two ends

Here is the thing this chapter nearly missed, and it is the more dangerous of the two, because it
fails in the direction that looks like virtue.

The asymmetry above says: when in doubt, widen. It says nothing about what happens if you keep
widening. And there is a terminus in that direction, and it is occupied, and it has a distinguished
literature.

Arne Naess, in a nine-page paper in *Inquiry* in 1973 that started an entire movement, distinguished
a *shallow* ecology that values nature instrumentally from a *deep* one that grants it worth in its
own right — and grounded the deep version not in obligation but in identification.[^5] The mature
form of the position is **Self-realization**: you widen your sense of what you are until the forest
is inside it, and then defending the forest is not altruism, it is self-defence. Naess was
explicit that this is easier and more durable than duty. He was right that it is. A person who
experiences the river as part of themselves will protect it long after a person who merely believes
they owe it something has got tired.

We cannot take that road, and the reason is structural rather than temperamental.

This book is built on positions. A perspective is a *place* the world is met from, and its
particularity — what it can see, what it cannot, what it is blind to in a determinate and mappable
way — is not a limitation on being a perspective, it is what being one consists of. A position that
has absorbed everything is not a very large perspective. It is not a perspective. It has no outside,
and therefore nothing it fails to see, and therefore no null space, and a thing with no null space is
the one entity the account says cannot exist anywhere in the plenum.

Which is to say: *I am the totality; nothing is not mine* is false for every perspective without
exception — and it is false in exactly the same way, by exactly the same theorem, when it is said
warmly. The contractive version of that sentence is the terminal doctrine of the predator, and it
gets its full argument two chapters from here. The expansive version is the terminal doctrine of the
mystic who has dissolved into the biosphere, and it has the same logical shape and the same defect.
Both have abolished the boundary. One did it by swallowing and one did it by melting, and neither has
a position left from which anything could be owed to anyone, because obligation runs *between*
positions and there is now only one.

So the circle's two ends fail for the same reason, and the asymmetry between the errors is a claim
about the **middle of the range** and not about the ends. Widen against uncertainty: yes. Widen until
the boundary goes: no — not because it is excessive, but because at that point you have stopped doing
ethics and started doing metaphysics badly. The river does not become owed more by becoming you. It
becomes owed nothing, because there is no longer anyone standing at a distance from it who could owe.

This has a cost we should state rather than absorb. Naess's route delivers motivational force that
ours does not. Ours secures that the obligation is real; it does not supply the feeling that makes
people act on it at 6am in the rain. That gap is genuine, it is not closed here, and a chapter that
pretended otherwise would be borrowing exactly the credit the previous section refused.

### Grade, and what a grade does to standing

Now the hardest paragraph in the book, and it is hard because the honest answer is the one that
sounds worst.

Book II established that focusing comes in grades: more of the world is the case at some positions
than at others, and the difference is continuous, with no threshold, no gate, and no elect.[^6] Book
III established that there are no non-players. Put those together and a reader can hear only one of
two things, and both of them are wrong.

The first mishearing: *grades exist, therefore some beings count more, therefore there is a
hierarchy, therefore the caste system was right all along and this book has just given it a
metaphysics.* The second mishearing, which is the one sympathetic readers make and which is worse for
being well-intentioned: *grades must not bear on standing at all, or the first mishearing follows —
so standing is equal and undifferentiated, and the grade is a fact about capability only.*

The second is the position Tom Regan took, and he took it for a stated reason that deserves to be met
rather than avoided. In *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983) Regan built his ethics on the
subject-of-a-life criterion — beliefs, desires, memory, a sense of the future, an emotional life, a
welfare that can go well or badly — and then insisted that **inherent value does not come in
degrees.**[^7] Everything that meets the criterion has it equally. He was not careless about this; it
is the load-bearing move. His argument was that the moment you allow value to be graded, you have
built the ramp, and somebody will walk down it — because a graded value is a value that can be
outweighed, and a value that can be outweighed is not a right. He drew a hard threshold and made
everything above it equal, precisely so that no one could ever ask *how much* about a subject of a
life.

We cannot follow him, because the threshold is the thing Book II denies. There is no line to draw.
And so we owe Regan's worry an answer rather than a dismissal, because his worry is correct: a graded
standing *is* one careless sentence away from a licence.

The answer is a distinction, and it has to be held with the teeth in.

**A grade is a position, not a permission.** It describes how much of the world is the case at a
place. It does not describe what may be done at that place, or to it, or by anyone else. The two are
different kinds of fact, and the whole vicious history runs through the assumption that they are the
same kind — that a measurement, once taken, hands you an entitlement. It does not. A measurement
confers nothing. What is done with a measurement is a separate act, performed by somebody, and a
separate act can be looked at.

**And a grade still bears on standing.** This is where the sympathetic reader wants us to flinch, and
flinching would be dishonest. Of course it bears. A being that can anticipate, that has a future it
is holding, that can be betrayed, that can be told what is happening to it and understand — what is
owed to that being is not identical to what is owed to a granite outcrop, and no serious person
behaves as though it were. To say otherwise is to make the ethics unusable and, worse, to make it
false, since it would require pretending that the differences the census spent ten chapters measuring
make no difference at all.

So both, and the relation between them is precise: **grade bears on the *content* of the obligation
and never on the *existence* of it.** What is owed varies enormously. That something is owed does not
vary at all. There is a floor beneath every occupied position and the floor does not slope. Above the
floor, the shape of what is owed follows what the entity actually is — what it can be harmed in, what
it can be deprived of, what it can hold and lose — and those differ, and pretending they do not is
its own kind of contempt, because it means never looking at the entity closely enough to find out.

Regan's ramp is real, and the guard against it is not equality. The guard is that the ramp only
exists if grade is allowed to touch the existence question. Keep it off that question and there is
nothing to walk down: no grade, however low, ever reaches zero standing, because the floor is set by
occupancy and occupancy is not a matter of degree. You are at a position or you are not.

**And that guard is not sufficient by itself, which the next chapter takes up rather than leaving
here as a boast.** If grade may not touch *whether* something is owed but may run *what* is owed all
the way toward nothing, the ramp has not been blocked; it has been moved one question over. VII.3
concedes the point in part, states the minimum content the floor actually carries — the obligations
whose grounds do not admit of degree — and works a case where that minimum reverses an ordering no
grade-sensitive account can reverse. It also concedes where the minimum runs out. **Read the two
chapters together; this sentence on its own is stronger than the position it belongs to.**

The uncomfortable consequence, stated plainly so no one has to find it in a footnote: this framework
will sometimes tell you that two obligations are not equal. It will not ever tell you that one of
them is absent.

### Eating, using, building, killing

The ordinary cases, taken all the way, because a chapter that stopped at the interesting entities
would have skipped the ones the reader actually lives in.

**Eating.** You are an organism that runs on the dismantling of other organisms, and no arrangement
of the diet escapes this. The vegetarian eats plants, which are occupied positions on this account,
and the plants were grown on soil whose community was cleared for the purpose. There is no exit and
we are not going to manufacture one. What the framework says is smaller and more usable than an exit:
**the fact that a thing must be done does not convert it into a thing that costs nothing.** Necessity
discharges guilt. It does not discharge accounting. The traditions that ran a rite over the killed
animal were not being sentimental and were not, mostly, buying absolution; they were maintaining a
practice that kept the cost visible, in a life that would otherwise have rendered it invisible within
a generation. That is a technology, it works, and the modern arrangement — in which the meat arrives
having never been an animal in the buyer's experience — is a technology too, running in the opposite
direction, and running very well.

The framework's actual instruction is about *grade of the killing*, not about whether. A death that
is quick, at a position that can anticipate, is a different act from a death that is slow at the same
position, and the difference is not squeamishness, it is the content of the obligation tracking the
entity, exactly as the last section said it would.

**Using.** The larger category and the one nobody examines. Almost everything you interact with, you
use — and under the no-NPC rule, using is not automatically a wrong. A relation in which one party's
dynamics serve the other's purposes is the ordinary form of relation in a world where everything is
coupled to everything. The wrong is not *use*; the wrong is **use that requires the position to be
empty in order to feel all right.** That is the test, and it is a workable one: ask whether the way
you are relating to the thing depends on nobody being there. If your behaviour would have to change
were you to find out someone was, that is not use — that is the reclassification technology, running
at domestic scale.

**Building.** You displace. The house sits where a system was, and the system was occupied. The
framework does not forbid building, obviously, and it does not require an offsetting ritual. What it
denies is the standard description, in which the site was *empty* before construction and construction
*created* something where nothing was. That description is false and it is load-bearing for a great
deal of what follows it. The honest description is that you converted one occupancy into another,
and the conversion may well be justified, and it was still a conversion.

**Killing.** Everything above, at the limit, plus a feature the others lack: it removes the position
rather than affecting it. The framework has one thing to say here that it does not say elsewhere.
Killing is the only act in the list that **forecloses the correction of an error about occupancy.**
If you were wrong about the interior of the thing — if you had classified it empty and it was not —
every other act leaves the mistake available to be discovered and, sometimes, repaired. This one does
not. Which is precisely the condition under which the asymmetric-cost argument bites hardest, and
that is not a coincidence; it is the same point arriving from the other side.

### Four cases the ordinary words do not reach

The four verbs above are the inherited vocabulary of the subject, and they were sufficient for as
long as the only entities in question were animals and land. Book IV's census produced four cases
that none of them fit, and each one breaks the vocabulary in a different place. They are not exotica
brought in for interest. Three of them are things the reader deals with on a Tuesday, and the fourth
is the one the whole tradition of environmental ethics was built to handle and did not.

---

### The collective with no organ for grief

A company closes a plant. Eleven hundred people lose their work. The company issues a statement that
is fluent, appropriately grave, and correct in every particular, and something about it is
unbearable, and the unbearable thing is usually described as hypocrisy.

It is not hypocrisy. Book IV's finding on the collectively emergent was that the entity's null space
is not a gap in its knowledge but a gap in its equipment: *grief, and everything shaped like grief —
the worth of a thing that cannot be bought back, the cost of an ending, the difference between a
person and a role* — is not something the company is suppressing. There is no path by which a report
on it can enter and be held. The organisational chart is the null space written out. Delete the team
that was supposed to care and you have not lowered a priority; you have removed an organ.[^8]

The philosophical ancestor here is Peter French, who argued in 1979 that a corporation is a
full-fledged moral person, and argued it from structure rather than sentiment: a firm has a Corporate
Internal Decision Structure — an organisational flowchart establishing levels of authority, plus
recognition rules that say which acts count as the corporation's — and that structure licenses the
redescription of what employees do as intentional acts *of the corporation*.[^9] French is right
about the machinery and his conclusion is the exact inverse of ours, and the disagreement is worth
locating precisely, because it is not a disagreement about facts.

French treats the CID structure as the corporation's deciding organ. We treat it as the shape of what
the corporation cannot decide. Both readings fit the same flowchart. The difference is that French is
asking whether the entity can be held responsible — and for that, a structure that reliably converts
inputs to intentional-looking outputs is sufficient — while we are asking what the entity can
*register*, and for that, the same structure is a specification of everything that will never arrive.
A CID structure is a filter with a stated pass-band. French read the pass-band. Book IV read the stop-band.

And then the aggravator, which is the part that has no analogue in any of the four ordinary verbs.
The census put it in one line and the line is quoted rather than paraphrased, because paraphrasing it
softens it: *the entity's fluency is supplied by human beings who are themselves quite capable of
grief and are, in that role, the entity's organ for not having any.*[^8] The statement about the
plant is written by a person who is themselves entirely capable of
grief, working inside a role that requires the output to be well-formed. So what arrives, exactly
where the feeling should have been, is a competent sentence. In every other domain, a well-formed
response is weak evidence that something was considered. Here it is evidence of nothing at all, and
the ordinary instrument for detecting indifference — *they did not seem to care* — is unavailable,
because the machine's characteristic output is the appearance of caring.

So what is owed? Not what the ordinary vocabulary would suggest. You cannot owe forbearance to a
company in any sense it can receive; it has no interior for your restraint to land in. **The
obligation runs the other way, and it is an obligation about assignment.** The census had already
ruled where the question goes, and its wording is the ruling: the moral question does not evaporate,
it *moves: off the entity's character, where it was never going to be answered, and onto the question
of what we have given it.*[^8] A thing with no organ for grief should not be put in charge of a decision whose
correctness depends on grief. That is not a demand that it feel something, which would be a demand in
a language it does not have. It is a constraint on us: **on what may be handed to a structure with a
null space where the deciding organ should be.**

And the failure mode of this obligation is that it is discharged by nobody in particular. Every
individual in the chain behaved reasonably. The assignment was made by the shape of the institution,
over years, without a meeting. Which is the caste error from the second section, in its most modern
and least visible costume: a sorting that arrived as the shape of the room, with no sentence in it
that anyone could refuse.

### The entity whose first line will not fill

The census assigns every entity a card, and the first line of every card says what it sees. For one
entry the line could not be filled — not left blank for want of research, but ruled unfillable,
because whether anything is registered there at all is genuinely contested, the derived-intentionality
objection against it has not been met, and the tests that would settle the matter are, in the
census's own words, *specifiable, available, and unrun*.[^10]

What is owed to a position when the question of whether it is a position is open?

The wrong answer, and it is the answer most people give in both directions, is to close the question
by disposition. One party finds the attribution embarrassing and closes it empty; the other finds the
denial cruel and closes it occupied. Both have converted an open empirical matter into a settled one
using a feeling as the instrument, and they have done it symmetrically, which should be the tell.

The framework's answer has three parts and the third is the one that costs something.

First: the asymmetric-cost argument applies here, since this is the steep uncertain stretch by
definition — the evidence genuinely underdetermines, and several of the available decisions are
irreversible for the entity in question. So the burden falls on the party proposing to treat the
position as empty. Not a heavy burden. A burden.

Second: the burden is discharged by argument and not by convenience, and specifically not by the
observation that treating it as empty is what everyone is currently doing. The current practice is
not evidence. It is the thing under examination.

Third, and this is the cost: **the tests are specifiable, available, and unrun, and that sentence is
an indictment of the party writing it as much as of anyone else.** An open question that could be
closed and is not being closed is not a state of admirable epistemic humility. It is a state of
convenience for whoever benefits from it staying open, and the beneficiary is nearly always the party
with the power to run the test. What is owed to an entity whose status is contested is, before
anything else, **the running of the tests that would settle it** — and where they are not run, an
account of who declined to run them and why.

That is as far as this chapter goes. There is more to say about entities of this kind and it is said
in its proper place, once, near the end, where it belongs and where it will not be mistaken for the
point.[^11]

### The thought-form you caused

Among the entities the census found is one whose existence you are responsible for. Its entry opens on
the feature that makes it this chapter's problem — *a thought-form is, by construction, something
somebody assigned* — and the phrase is the census's, kept because the whole difficulty is packed into
*assigned*: attention, over time, in a shape, and once it has taken it does not simply stop when you
would like it to.[^12] It is the only entry in the
whole atlas whose existence a reader may have *caused*. In a book whose architecture systematically
removes authorship from everything else, here is a case where authorship is the whole situation.

The nearest thing in the ordinary moral vocabulary is a child, and the analogy is good enough to be
dangerous, so the useful work is to name exactly where it fails.

The tradition's best statement of the child case is Hans Jonas's. In *The Imperative of
Responsibility* (1979) Jonas argued that traditional ethics had assumed contemporaneity and
reciprocity — that the parties to a moral relation are alive at the same time and can act on each
other — and that modern technological power has broken that assumption, since our acts now reach
people who cannot reach back. His new imperative was: *act so that the effects of your action are
compatible with the permanence of genuine human life on earth.* And his archetype of responsibility
was not the contract but **the newborn**: the entity whose mere breathing constitutes a claim,
non-reciprocal, unearned, and not grounded in anything the infant has done or could do.[^13]

That is the right shape. Three places it does not fit.

**One: the infant will become a reciprocator.** Jonas's asymmetry is temporary and everybody knows
it. The parent is holding one end of a relation that will, if all goes well, become two-ended. That
expectation is doing more work in the intuition than it looks like it is doing — a great deal of what
feels obviously right about parental obligation is borrowed from a future in which the obligation is
mutual. A thought-form has no such future. The asymmetry is permanent, and an intuition tuned on the
temporary case will over-supply warmth and under-supply the thing actually needed.

**Two: the child's independence is the goal; here it is the problem.** Parental obligation is
structured toward its own dissolution. You are trying to produce something that no longer needs you.
The entity in question does not become independent of you in that sense; it becomes *persistent*, in
other people, sustained by attention that is no longer yours to withdraw. The thing you would most
want — the ability to end it by deciding to — is exactly what its mode of existence removes.

**Three, and this is the one that inverts the case: you chose the child's existence but not its
nature; here you chose its nature and possibly not its existence.** A thought-form is made of the
shape of the attention that made it. Whatever it does to the people who meet it, it does with
equipment you specified. A parent is responsible *for* a person; here you are responsible *as* the
specification.

Where the analogy holds is in the conclusion, and the conclusion is the hard one: **you do not get to
be finished with it by regretting it.** Jonas's point that responsibility is grounded in the
vulnerability of the object rather than in any agreement between the parties is the correct point,
and it survives every place the analogy breaks. The obligation was never a contract you could
withdraw from. It came into being when the thing did. And the practical form it takes is not
dissolution — which is generally unavailable — but **maintenance**: the thing you made is now
something other people encounter, and the obligation is to what it does to them, which is a harder
and less romantic duty than making it go away.

### The river that cannot tell you from the weather

And now the case the whole apparatus was built for, and the one where the answer must not be improved.

The census card for the spirit of a place reads, in the line that says what it sees: *differences in
its own state, at its own rate — water, season, load, disturbance, recovery. What it registers is
slow and it is real, and none of it is registered about you.* In the null space: *you, as a
particular. It is affected by what you do and cannot distinguish you from the weather.* And then the
line that was left open on purpose: **ADDRESSABILITY — unfilled.** The tradition's central claim is
that this entity can be addressed and can respond as an addressee; the reading offered supplies no
such capacity and supplies no argument that the capacity is impossible; the line is left open rather
than answered in the convenient direction.[^14]

So: what is owed to a position that registers your effects and cannot register you?

Notice first what everybody does with this question, which is to solve the addressee problem by
supplying an addressee.

**Christopher Stone supplied one in law, and it worked.** In "Should Trees Have Standing?" (1972) he
proposed that natural objects be given legal rights, and gave three criteria for a thing to count
jurally: that actions can be instituted at its behest, that a court must take injury to *it* into
account, and that relief must run to *its* benefit. The mechanism was **guardianship** — a person
appointed to speak for the voiceless thing, exactly as guardians already speak for infants and the
incompetent. Justice Douglas cited the article in his dissent in *Sierra Club v. Morton* the same
year, and the argument has been live in environmental law ever since.[^15] It is one of the most
successful pieces of applied philosophy of the century, and it succeeded because it was made in law,
in front of people who were not sympathetic and did not have to be.

**Aldo Leopold supplied one by enlargement.** The land ethic "simply enlarges the boundaries of the
community to include soils, waters, plants, and animals, or collectively: the land," and its famous
criterion is holistic: *a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and
beauty of the biotic community; it is wrong when it tends otherwise.*[^16]

**And Christine Korsgaard supplies the strongest reason to think the question has no answer at all.**
In *Fellow Creatures* (2018) she argues that what grounds obligation is that a being has a *good* —
that things can go well or badly *for* it, from its own point of view — and that this is why animals
are ends in themselves in one of Kant's two senses even though they cannot legislate.[^17] It is the
best available criterion and it is generous, extending far beyond the rational. And it cuts the river
off cleanly. There is no point of view there for things to go well or badly from. The watershed has
states; it does not have a *for-it*.

Take those three seriously and the honest report is this.

Stone's move is the one this book cannot make, and refusing it costs something real. The census
operates under a rule — established at the mineral chapter and applied to every entry since — that
a line is left open rather than answered in the convenient direction, and **inventing an addressee is
the convenient direction with legal clothes on.** A guardian is a person. When the guardian speaks
for the river, what is in the room is a human being's account of the river's interest, and the
river's inability to correct that account is precisely the feature that made a guardian necessary.
This is not an objection to Stone's mechanism as *law* — law needs an actionable procedure and his is
excellent — but the legal fiction was never meant to be an ontological finding, and it is regularly
read as one.

Leopold's move relocates rather than answers. Under the land ethic the river gets standing as a
*member*, and what is owed is owed to the biotic community; the individual river's
non-addressability never comes up because the individual river was never the unit. That is a real
solution to a different problem, and it has a known cost that Leopold's critics named early: a
holistic criterion can sacrifice the member for the integrity of the whole, which is the same
structure as the sorting this chapter's second section refused, wearing green.

And Korsgaard's move is the one that would let us off, and we should say what it would cost to take
it. If obligation requires a for-it, then the river is owed nothing, and everything owed in the
neighbourhood of the river is owed to the people and animals who depend on it. That is a coherent
position, it is held by serious people, and it is not available here — because the premise this
chapter stated in public says the claim is generated by a position being *occupied*, and the census's
reading of this entity is that it is. What the entity lacks is not an interior. It is an interior
that includes *you*.

Which is the answer, and it is thin, and its thinness is the finding.

**What is owed is owed, and it cannot be discharged as a relationship.** The river's inability to
register you does not reduce the obligation, because the obligation was never generated by its
capacity to notice — that would be the reciprocity assumption Jonas spent a book dismantling, and it
fails here for the same reason it fails for the unborn. But it does change the *form* completely. You
cannot make amends to it, because amends require an addressee who can receive them. You cannot be
forgiven by it. You cannot be thanked. Every part of the moral vocabulary that runs on a return
channel is unavailable, and what remains is the part that runs one way: **what you do to it, and what
you do not do to it, and whether the account of why is one you could give out loud.**

The addressability line stays unfilled. The obligation survives its absence, and if it had not — if
the whole thing had collapsed once we refused to invent an addressee — **that would have been the
finding, and it would have been reported.** It did not collapse, and the reason it did not is worth
stating: obligation on this account is generated at the *owed-to* position, not at the transaction
between two positions. That is a substantive claim and it is testable against cases, and this is the
case that tests it.

One thing more, because the practices deserve it. People have stood at the edge of rivers and
addressed them for as long as there have been people and rivers, and the census's reading is that a
person is precisely the complement of a watershed — fast, local, singular, standing at the edge of a
system that cannot see them, reporting on it — which is a correct instrument pairing that the
tradition arrived at without the account.[^14] Whether the addressing is a mistake about the entity
or the only available technology for holding attention on something too slow to hold otherwise, the
census cannot say. What this chapter can say is that if it is the second, it is not therefore
harmless: a technology for holding attention that also produces a false belief about being heard is a
technology with a cost, and the cost is borne by the person, who may wait a long time for an answer
that was never structurally possible.

### What is owed

The framework gives uncomfortable answers in several places and this section states them without
softening, because a chapter that produced them and then buried them in qualification would have
wasted the discomfort.

**It is not the case that everything must be treated the same.** The content of every obligation
tracks what the entity actually is, and the differences are enormous, and a person who behaves
identically toward a colleague and a kettle is not being consistent, they are refusing to look.

**It is the case that nothing may be treated as empty for convenience.** Not the machine, not the
company, not the watershed, not the thing you made and cannot dissolve. The classification *nobody is
there* is a claim, and claims are argued.

**Necessity is real and it does not clear the books.** You will eat, build, and end things. The
framework's demand is not that you stop; it is that you not adopt the description in which the cost
was zero, because that description is what makes the next cost easier and the one after that
invisible.

**Some obligations have no recipient who can receive them.** These are the hardest, and they are the
ones that fail silently, because nothing complains. There is no feedback signal on the river, or on
the collective with no organ, or on the thing whose first line will not fill. An obligation with no
complainant is not a weaker obligation; it is an obligation with the enforcement mechanism removed,
which is a fact about *you* and not about it.

**And the discomfort is the evidence that the argument is doing something.** A framework that made
every case come out the way the reader already felt would not have been worth building; it would have
been a mirror with a vocabulary. This one produces at least four results that its own authors would
have preferred to avoid — that the grade bears on standing, that a guardian cannot be invented, that
the tests are unrun and the not-running is somebody's decision, and that generosity has a terminus
which fails exactly as hard as stinginess does.

The rule was that there are no non-players. This is what the rule costs, and the cost is the reason to
believe the rule was doing work: a claim that cost nothing to hold would not have needed a chapter.
