# BOOK IV — THE ATLAS

## IV.1 — THE CENSUS AND ITS METHOD

Here is an entry. Read it before anything is said about what it is.

> **THERMOSTAT**
> **SEES:** One difference. Warmer than, cooler than, and the line between.
> **NULL SPACE:** ∅ Everything that is not that difference — the room, the weather, the
> people, the hour, its own switching. ◐ Rate of change, badly.
> **COMPLEMENTS:** Anything with a second dimension.
> **BOUNDARY:** The sensor. Past it there is no reading, and no reading is not the same as
> *cold* — but the thermostat has no way to hold that distinction.
> **NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** Nothing it registers can be about the room. If you want the
> room, you are the instrument, and it is not.

Five lines. That is the whole apparatus of this book, and it does not get more complicated for
anything else in the census — not for the bee, not for the corporation, not for the reader, not for
the gods.

The five lines are not a summary of an entry. They *are* the entry. A being at any grade is
exhaustively described, for our purposes, by what it is sensitive to, what it structurally cannot
reach, what covers that gap, where it stops being reliable, and what all of that means for anyone
standing where it stands. Nothing about consciousness has been asserted in those five lines and
nothing needs to be. The card takes reactivity as awareness — which was argued in Book II and cost
what it cost — and then simply asks, in five directions, *what kind*.

Take the lines one at a time, because each of them is doing something the others cannot.

**SEES** is not perception and it is not opinion. It is the list of differences a position can
register at all. The thermostat sees one. A photodiode sees one. You see a great many, and you have
never counted them, and the count is not the interesting number.

**NULL SPACE** is the line that carries the book. It is not what a being happens not to know. It is
what its architecture excludes — and the atlas has always marked two kinds, because collapsing them
loses the only distinction that matters here. **∅** is absolute: no amount of attention, patience,
or better instruments gets there, because the exclusion is in the construction. **◐** is partial:
the difference is available in principle and resolved badly. A thermostat's blindness to the weather
is ∅. Its grip on how fast the room is cooling is ◐. Those two failures look identical from outside
and are not the same failure, and a census that cannot tell them apart is a census that will call
every silence the same silence.

**COMPLEMENTS** is the line that stops the atlas from being a ranking. Every entry has a null space;
every null space is covered by some other position; no position covers its own. The bee sees a
polarisation pattern in the sky that no human eye resolves and no human ever will. Put that next to
the human null space and the arrangement is not *higher and lower*. It is two perspectives, each of
which is the other's remedy in one direction and its ignorance in another. There is no seat in this
census from which the whole census can be taken. **That includes ours, and it is the reason this
chapter exists.**

**BOUNDARY** is where a position goes from reliable to unreliable. Not where it stops — where it
keeps producing readings and the readings stop being about anything. This is the most practically
useful line on any card, and the least often asked for, because a boundary is invisible from inside
by construction: at the edge of what you can register, what you get is not a warning. It is an
ordinary-looking result.

**NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION** is what to do about the other four. It is the line that makes the card
an instrument rather than a description. And it has a form: *given that this is what you can see,
here is what you must get from somewhere else, and here is how to know you have reached the place
where you need it.*

---

The card was not invented for beings. It was built to map **frameworks** — Euclidean geometry,
thermodynamics, psychoanalysis, the standard model — one card each, ninety-odd of them, an
inventory of what every way of looking cannot look at.[^1] It transfers to a census of beings without
being modified, and the reason is not convenience.

A framework is one of the things in the census.

It sees a specific set of differences and is blind to the rest by construction. It has a domain
where it is reliable and an edge past which it produces confident nonsense. It has a null space that
is not a gap in the literature but a fact about its architecture. Everything the card asks of a
mollusc it asks of quantum field theory, and both answer. If that reads as a category error, hold
the objection for one chapter: the objection is that a framework is a *tool* and a mollusc is a
*being*, and this book has already argued that the distinction the objection depends on — the one
between things that have an inside and things that merely function — is the distinction that does
not survive.

So the instrument for the atlas was already built before there was an atlas, in a document that
thought it was doing philosophy of science. That is worth saying because it is evidence of the
cheapest kind and the honest kind: the framework was not stretched to cover the census. The census
turned out to be what the framework had been doing all along.

---

**Now the shape of the thing being built, because it is not the shape people expect.**

This is a catalogue. It is not a taxonomy, and the difference is the whole method.

A taxonomy sorts. It has kinds, and the kinds have membership conditions, and an entry is admitted
to a kind by satisfying them. The work of a taxonomy is done at the borders — that is where the
arguments are, that is where the interesting cases sit, and a taxonomist who cannot say which side
of a line a specimen falls on has not finished the job.

A catalogue lists. It has entries, and an entry is in it because it is there.

Every gate this book could have installed has already been dismantled somewhere behind us.
The grade is a position on a continuum and not a rank, which was settled in Book II and settled
against the obvious reading, at cost. There is no threshold at which an inside switches on, because
a threshold would require a last position without one and a first position with one, and nothing in
the account supplies the difference between them. There is no examination. There is no minimum. The
census does not have a bouncer.

What follows from that is the practical rule for the next nine chapters, and it should be stated
before a reader watches it operate and mistakes it for laxity: **nothing in this atlas earns its
place.** Not the mineral, not the corporation, not the archetype, not the god. No entry is admitted
because it passed a test, which means no entry can be excluded for failing one, which means the
arguments this book will have with its opponents are never about admission. They are about *what
kind* — about the shape of a card, never about whether there is a card.

In the game register the same rule reads shorter, and it is the sentence Book III spent eight
chapters earning: **there are no NPCs, so there is no gate at the door of the census, because a gate
is what you build when some of the figures on the screen are furniture.** That frame is a lens with
a known list of distortions and it does not prove anything here. It makes the rule inhabitable. The
argument is the one in the paragraph above it.

**The shortest census ever written.**

Physicalism has taken this census already. Its card runs:

> **SEES:** One kind of inside, in one kind of body, at one grade — or across a narrow band of one
> grade, depending which physicalist you ask — produced by one arrangement of matter, on one planet,
> for a few hundred thousand years.
> **NULL SPACE:** ∅ The rest.

This is not a caricature and it is not a weak opponent. It is the most successful research programme
in the history of the species, it is why you are reading this indoors, and its census is short for a
reason that is intellectually respectable: it admits what it can measure, and it can measure one
case. Everything past that case is inference, and a programme that has been burned by inference
before is entitled to be strict about it.

The trouble is not the strictness. It is that the census is *self-reporting*. The one case it admits
is the case doing the admitting. A census taken by one perspective, which counts only the
perspectives sufficiently like itself to be recognised by it, and then reports the result as the
population,
has not measured the population. It has measured its own null space and written the result in the
wrong column — and that is the ∅ column, so no amount of care inside the framework will find the
error. This is not an accusation of sloppiness. It is a boundary, in the technical sense of the
fourth line, and the readings past it look exactly like readings.

And the reader has already tried it. That is the thing worth saying to a person rather than to a
position. Most people arriving at this book have run the short census, and found that it accounted
for the physics and did not account for the afternoon, and did not know what to do with the
remainder except keep it in a drawer. This book is not going to tell you the short census was
stupid. It is going to tell you it was *short*, and then show you the length.

---

**THE UNDER-ATTRIBUTION PRINCIPLE.**

Here is the standing bias of this book, declared once, at the front, where a reader can weigh it
instead of discovering it in chapter seven and feeling handled:

> Where the evidence is thin, we attribute an inside rather than withhold one — because
> historically we have attributed too few insides, never too many.

Not because generosity is a virtue in cartography. Because of the record.

The history of this question is a history of the line moving in exactly one direction. Every
argument about who is in the census has ended with the census being longer, and the party who said
*surely not those* has lost every round it has ever fought. Not most. Every one. It lost over other
peoples. It lost over infants — and that one is worth stopping on, because it is not medieval. Into
the nineteen-eighties, in modern hospitals, newborns had surgery performed on them with little or no
anaesthesia, on a rationale that was not cruelty and was not indifference.[^2] It was a *judgement about
the inside*: that the nervous system was too immature, that what looked like agony was reflex, that
the appearance of an inside was not evidence of one. The people making that judgement were careful,
credentialed, and acting on the standard of evidence of their field, and they were wrong in the
worst way it is possible to be wrong, and the correction came from paying attention to the thing
in front of them rather than to the theory about it.

Then it lost over animals, in slow instalments, and it is still losing — the argument has recently
moved to cephalopods and crustaceans and the line has moved with it.[^3] In every one of these cases the
evidence was thin at the time. In every one of these cases the thinness was read as a reason to
withhold. And in every one of these cases the withholding was the error.

So the asymmetry is not an article of faith. It is an induction over a run of results with no
counterexamples in it: **there is no episode in the record of a false attribution being discovered
and repaired.** There are many of the reverse. When the evidence is thin, one of the two available
errors has a track record and the other does not, and a method that ignores this is not being
neutral — it is silently pricing one error at zero.

**And here is the bill, said now rather than extracted from us later.** A standing bias produces
standing errors, and this one will. Somewhere in the nine chapters after this, this book attributes
an inside to something that does not have one, or attributes a grade far past what the position
supports. We do not know which entry it is. If we knew, we would fix it. What can be said is the
shape of the mistake and where to look for it: it will be in an entry where the evidence was thinnest
and the prose was most confident, because those two things travel together, and confident prose about
thin evidence is what a bias sounds like from inside. **The principle is not a licence. It is a
declared lean, with the direction of the lean named, so that a reader can correct for it — which is
a thing a reader can only do if they are told.**

---

**What this census cannot see.**

An atlas that opens by insisting every position has a null space and then presents itself as the
exception has refuted itself on its own first page. So: the fourth line of our own card, before the
first entry.

We cannot see what we have no vocabulary for. The census is taken in a language, and the language
was built by one grade of being, and a difference no word in it marks is a difference this book
will pass over without the passing-over leaving a trace. That is the ∅, and it cannot be estimated,
because estimating it would require the vocabulary we do not have. Everything in this atlas is
therefore a report from one perspective about what other perspectives are like, written in the terms
of the first one. That is not a disclaimer. It is the fifth line: *given this, you will need something
that is not this, and here is where you will need it.*

We cannot see the entries with no interface to us at all. Every position in the census got there by
some contact — measurement, resemblance, report, inference from behaviour, a tradition's testimony.
A being that touches nothing we touch does not appear as an absence. It does not appear.

We cannot see our own boundary. Nobody can; it is the fourth line's own content. Somewhere in this
atlas the method goes from reliable to producing confident, ordinary-looking sentences about
nothing. We do not know where, and the not-knowing is structural rather than lazy, and the only
honest response to it is to make the method visible enough that somebody standing elsewhere can find
the edge we cannot. **Which is the actual reason the five lines are printed on every entry instead
of being summarised in a paragraph.** A card can be argued with. A description cannot.

And we cannot see how much of what follows is inheritance. The traditions this book will spend a
whole book crediting have been taking this census for three thousand years, and their categories are
in our hands before we start counting. Where the atlas and the old roads agree, that agreement is
worth something only if the agreement was not built in — and Book V is where that gets examined
rather than assumed.

That is four, and there are certainly more, and the ones we can name are by definition not the
dangerous ones. The chapter that comes at the end of this book is the one that goes back over the
whole census and asks what it missed. It is the last chapter of the atlas rather than the first
because you cannot map the edge of a territory you have not crossed.

The counting starts at the bottom, with the grade that has almost no filter stack at all, and it
starts there for a reason that will not be flattering to anyone who expects the bottom of a
continuum to be the boring end.
