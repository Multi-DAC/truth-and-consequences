## VII.7 — FREEDOM WHEN EVERY PATH ALREADY EXISTS

Four books ago a chapter called THE WALKING IS REAL answered the objection that the walking is
superfluous, and it answered it well. The argument was that superfluity is a relation and this one
has no second term: your afternoon is not surplus to the Ground, which keeps no count; not to any
other inside, from where your walking is the most consequential thing in the room; not to you, from
whose position the walking is not a portion of the afternoon but the whole content of it. The seat
from which walking looks redundant turned out to be the seat with no position on it — the same empty
chair the book has been emptying since Book II, arriving for the first time in the first person.

That argument holds. It has not been challenged since and it is not challenged here. But it was made
in a chapter about a metaphor, in a book about a metaphor, and it stopped exactly where a chapter
about a metaphor has to stop. It established that choosing is not nothing. It did not say what it
is.

The reader who accepted it noticed the gap within a page, and it is the same experience that reader
had after III.3 — an argument that is sound, that was worth making, and that does not reach far
enough. Three chapters have now told you what choosing is **not**. It is not selection: to be
decided is to have been picked out, picking out removes, and there is nothing outside the whole to
remove with. It is not addition: the whole is not larger when you have finished. It is not
superfluous: there is no position from which it is surplus. Three negations, each valid, and a
reader can hold all three at once and still be unable to say what the act *is*.

That is the debt. This chapter is where it comes due, and it is the last place in the book where it
can be paid, because Book VIII is a manual and a manual that opens on an unanswered question is a
manual nobody follows.

---

### I. The question the free will debate has been asking, and why we cannot ask it

Start by being precise about what is being demanded, because the demand is older than the objection
and has a shape the objection inherits without examining.

Every version of the free will argument that has run in the West for two thousand years takes place
at a **node**. There is a moment; two or more roads leave it; the question is whether the agent
could have taken a different one. Libertarians say yes and pay for it with a causal gap.
Compatibilists say the question is malformed and freedom is a matter of the right kind of cause
running through you. Hard determinists say no and accept the consequences. They disagree
ferociously and they agree completely about the furniture: freedom, whatever it is, is a property
of a **branch point**, and it is measured by the availability of an alternative.

This book cannot enter that argument, and the reason is not tact. It is that the furniture is gone.
Plenitude does not remove the alternatives; it makes every one of them fully actual, permanently,
without exception. There is no node at which one road is open and the others shut, because there is
no operation that shuts a road. The libertarian's alternative is available — all of them are, always,
which is more availability than the libertarian ever asked for and is not the same as what he
wanted. The determinist's closure never happens — nothing closes, and nothing needed to.

So the classical question does not get answered here. It gets **starved**. There is nothing for it to
be about.

I want to be careful with that sentence, because it is the most dangerous one in the chapter and it
is the exact move the reader should have been trained by now to distrust. A framework that dissolves
a hard question rather than answering it has done one of two things: it has made a genuine advance,
or it has changed the subject and hoped the reader would enjoy the new one. This book does it
here, and does it in the open. The theorem arrives with a gloss attached:

> This sidesteps the classical free will debate (libertarianism vs. compatibilism vs. hard
> determinism) by reframing the question entirely. The issue is not whether a stream's navigation
> is causally determined. The issue is whether navigation feels directed and whether that felt
> direction has phenomenological consequences.[^1]

Read that twice. The first sentence declines the metaphysical question. The second replaces it with
a phenomenological one — and then, in its final clause, does something slightly better than a
retreat: it commits to the felt direction **having consequences**, which is a claim about the world
and not merely a report about the inside. That last clause is the only asset the sidestep keeps, and
it is worth about a third of what it looks like, because the consequences named are consequences
*for experience*. The circle is smaller than the classical one. It is still a circle.

So the honest statement of the position, before any of the good news, is this: **the account declines
to answer the question it names, and the reason it can afford to is that its own axioms have removed
the question's subject matter.** That is a legitimate move exactly once, and only if you can say what
replaced it. If the replacement is a redescription of the feeling of choosing, dressed better, then
the objection wins and everything after this paragraph is decoration.

The rest of this chapter is the argument that something did replace it, that the replacement is
larger than what it replaced, and — this is the part the book owes and has been deferring since
Book III — that it costs something real to make the trade.

---

### II. What choosing is

Here is the answer, stated flatly, and then unpacked.

**Choosing is not the selection of a path. It is the maintenance of a direction.**

Every path exists. What varies — what can vary, what is the only thing left that *can* vary — is
which one a given stream is in contact with, and the rate and consistency with which contact moves.
The word for that is navigation, and the claim the framework makes about it is Theorem 6: free will
is the capacity of a stream to navigate toward or away from its own coherence.[^2]

That sentence is easy to read as a slogan. It is not one, and the way to see that it is not is to
notice what it has quietly done to the *measurement* of freedom.

At a node, freedom is a one-bit quantity. Either the alternative was available or it was not. You
either could have done otherwise or you could not. There is no such thing as being *slightly* able
to have done otherwise; the whole two-thousand-year argument is a fight about the value of a single
binary digit at a single instant, which is why it has never moved and why nobody's answer has ever
changed anybody's afternoon.

Orientation is not like that at all. A stream can be more or less oriented toward its own coherence.
The orientation can be steady or intermittent. It can hold under load or only in comfort. It can be
strong on one axis and absent on another — a person can be exquisitely oriented in his work and
completely unoriented in his marriage, and the same person can watch the second one improve over a
decade. Orientation is continuous, it is multi-dimensional, it varies within a single life, it
responds to practice, and it is the thing every reader has actually been tracking about himself the
whole time he thought he was worrying about branch points.

**So the trade is this: a binary quantity at an instant, exchanged for a continuous quantity across
a life.** That is not a consolation prize handed over after the real thing was lost. It is a larger
object. Whether it is larger *enough* is the question beat four settles, and it is settled by
counting the losses honestly, not by admiring the gain.

Now the mechanism, because "maintenance of a direction" is a phrase and not yet an account.

What is a stream in contact with a region of configuration space? Book II answered that: a focusing.
What determines which region? The whole apparatus of Book VI — the filter stack, what the attention
is trained on, what the body is doing, what the language permits, what the surrounding streams
render as available. And what moves the contact? **Attention, sustained, over time.** That is the
entire machinery, and its poverty is the point. There is one lever. It is not a lever you pull at
the node; it is a lever you are always already holding, and its effect is not to open a road but to
change which roads are near.

This is why the framework's account of choosing cannot be located at an instant, and why every
attempt to catch it at an instant fails. Ask *at the moment of decision, what did you do?* and the
honest answer is: at the moment of decision, nothing much happened, because the decision had been
made over the preceding six months by what you had been attending to, and the moment merely
displayed it. That is not a defect in the account. It is a well-known feature of how choices
actually feel in retrospect to anybody who has made a large one, and no node-based theory has ever
explained it, because a node-based theory has to treat the six months as preparation for the real
event and the person living it experiences the reverse.

### III. The existentialist we are using and the one we are not

The engine has a name, it has been named exactly once, and it has not appeared in this manuscript
before now: Sartre. The mechanism set out above is deeply informed by the existentialist
philosophy of Jean-Paul Sartre — specifically the concept of the **project** and the dictum that
**existence precedes essence** — that a person is not born with a nature and instead becomes one
through choices, continuously, without ever arriving at a fixed thing that could be described in
advance.[^3]

The mechanism is exactly right and the attribution needs correcting — against this book's own
interest, and in a way that makes the position stronger rather than weaker.

Sartre's freedom is groundless. That is not incidental to it — it is the whole load-bearing claim of
*Being and Nothingness*. Consciousness for Sartre is a **nothingness**, a hole in being, precisely
because it has no nature to consult; the anguish of freedom is the discovery that there is no
standard outside your choosing by which the choosing could be assessed, and the name for pretending
otherwise — for appealing to your nature, your role, your telos, your circumstances, anything that
would make the choice for you — is **bad faith**. A Sartre handed an *entelechy*, an inherent drive
toward the expansion of perspective, is a Sartre with the floor put back under him. He spent seven
hundred pages taking that floor out.

So the synthesis just set out — the *what* is inherited and teleological, the *how* is authored and
Sartrean — is not a reconciliation of Aristotle and Sartre.[^4] It is the use of Sartre's
**mechanism** against Sartre's **conclusion**. We take the account of self-creation through project
and we refuse the groundlessness that motivated it.

That is a defensible theft and it should be conducted in daylight rather than under a borrowed
authority. The defence is this. Sartre's groundlessness is a consequence of his ontology, not an
independent discovery: if consciousness is a nothingness in a world of brute being, then of course
nothing in the world can orient it, and of course the orientation must be invented, and of course
the invention is anguish. Change the ontology and the consequence does not follow. On this book's
account consciousness is not a hole in being — it is what being is doing, locally, at a position.
A stream oriented toward its own coherence is not consulting an external standard and is therefore
not in bad faith by Sartre's own test; it is doing the one thing a structure of that kind can do
without ceasing to be that kind of structure. **The telos is not a rule handed down. It is a
description of what coherence-seeking systems are.**

And the price of the correction, paid here rather than hidden: we lose the anguish, and the anguish
was doing work. Sartre's picture explains why people flee from freedom, and it explains it
elegantly — they flee because it is genuinely unbearable to be the sole author of your own
standard. Our picture has to explain the same flight without the same cause, and the explanation is
weaker and more mundane: people do not flee from groundlessness, they flee from *effort*, because
orientation is metabolically expensive and drifting is free. That is less dramatic and probably
more true, and it is the version Book VIII can actually work with, because you cannot build a
practice on anguish and you can build one on the observation that the default is drift.

One more name, and a correction to how this book used him one chapter ago. **Frankfurt** appeared at
VII.6 as a foil — his account of love as conferring value was refused there, because on our axioms
nothing confers value that was not already there to be seen. He appears here as **support**, on a
different work and a different argument: the hierarchical theory of freedom, in which what matters
is not whether you could have done otherwise but whether you *want to want* what you want — whether
your first-order desires are endorsed by your second-order ones.[^5] He belongs at exactly
this point, and the citation is the right one, because hierarchical freedom is orientation described from
the inside of a single agent at a single time. Frankfurt got to the same structure through a
question about addiction; we get to it through a question about configuration space. **The turn from
foil to ally in adjacent chapters is declared here so that it reads as two arguments rather than as
one inconsistency**, and the reason the same author can be wrong about love and right about freedom
is that his error at VII.6 was about where value comes from and his insight here is about how a will
is layered, and those are separable.

### IV. Why this is not fatalism, and what the difference actually costs to check

III.3 drew the distinction and III.7 defended the walking. Neither did the thing this chapter has to
do, which is to say **what in the world would look different** if the fatalist were right. A
distinction with no visible consequence is a preference.

Set the two accounts side by side at the only place they can be compared.

**Under fatalism**, the quality of your attention over the next six months makes no difference to
where you end up, because where you end up was never open. Attention is part of the display, not
part of the mechanism. Whatever you attend to, you were going to attend to, and the trajectory was
fixed independently of it.

**Under navigation**, the quality of your attention over the next six months is the *only* thing
that makes a difference to where you end up, because it is the sole mechanism by which contact
moves. Nothing else in the account does any work at all.

Those are not the same claim and they diverge on something observable: **whether sustained
reorientation of attention is correlated with change in trajectory.** Not in one case — we will come
to that — but across a population and across time.

The answer is boringly, massively yes. Every practice that works, works this way. Every therapy with
an effect size has the same shape at its core: the systematic redirection of attention, held long
enough to move what is near. Every training regime, athletic or musical or intellectual, is a
protocol for putting a body in repeated contact with a region it could not previously reach.
Every recovery from an addiction that holds, holds because the person's attention stopped being
organised around one thing and started being organised around another. Book VI's entire history is a
record of this at the scale of civilisations: change what a culture attends to and you change what
its members find thinkable, and the change is legible in the sources within a generation.

The fatalist has an answer to all of it, and the answer is good, and this chapter is not going to
pretend it is not. He says: *and all of that was going to happen too.* The reorientation was fated,
the therapy's success was fated, the whole correlation is fated, and the fact that it looks like a
mechanism from inside is exactly what a fated correlation would look like. He is right that no
single case refutes him. He is right that the correlation can be absorbed.

**So here is the honest epistemic situation, stated as this book states these things elsewhere: the
difference is checkable in one direction only.**

If sustained reorientation of attention were *not* correlated with change in trajectory — if the
practices did not work, if the effect sizes were zero, if attention were epiphenomenal to outcome —
then navigation would be dead. Not weakened. Dead, immediately, because attention is the only
mechanism the account has and a mechanism that does nothing is not a mechanism. Navigation is
therefore a claim that could have failed, and did not.

Fatalism cannot fail. It absorbs every result, including the opposite one; had the practices not
worked, that too would have been fated. **A position that cannot fail has not passed a test when the
evidence comes in its favour, because no evidence could have come otherwise.**

That asymmetry is the whole of what is available here, and it is less than a proof and more than a
preference. It is precisely the grade this book has committed to naming every time: convergence and
survival are evidence, not demonstration, and the chapter that pretends otherwise is the chapter a
careful reader stops trusting.[^6] Navigation is the account that put something at risk. Fatalism is
the account that did not. On this book's own standard for grading claims, that is a real difference
and it is not a decisive one, and both halves of that sentence are load-bearing.

There is a second difference, smaller and cleaner, and it belongs to the reader rather than to the
argument. Fatalism is **unlivable in the specific sense that nobody has ever lived it.** Not as a
rhetorical point — as an observation about the class of people who hold the view. The determinist
still looks both ways. He still deliberates about which job to take, and the deliberation is not
theatre; it has the full phenomenology, including the part where it is difficult. He would say, and
correctly, that his deliberating is itself part of the causal chain and he never claimed otherwise.
Granted. But then his position and ours have converged on everything that happens, and differ only
about a description applied afterward — which means the disagreement has stopped being about the
afternoon and become about the vocabulary. **Our account at least explains why the deliberation is
not theatre. His has to say it is real and does nothing, which is a strange thing to say about the
only activity he cannot stop performing.**

### The census entry

The node-based account of freedom is an instrument, and a good one, and it earns a card on the same
terms as every instrument this book has carded. What is unusual about this one is that it is the
instrument the reader arrived holding — every other card in the census has been made for a
framework the reader was being shown, and this one is for the framework he has been using to read
the others.

**Whose:** the classical free will debate, entire and undivided. Libertarian, compatibilist and hard
determinist share this instrument and disagree only about its reading. It is also the instrument of
every legal system on earth, of the ordinary attribution of praise and blame, and of the reader's
own account of his last difficult decision.

**Renders:** what it renders superbly, and the list is long enough that nobody should be
condescending about it. Coercion. Duress. Manipulation. Addiction, which is where Frankfurt built
his half of it. The difference between a signature given freely and one given at gunpoint. The
entire apparatus of legal responsibility, which works, and which no account developed in this
chapter could replace or would want to. At the scale where human beings actually adjudicate each
other, the node-based instrument is not merely serviceable — it is the correct tool, and its
readings are the ones this book's own ethics defers to.[^7]

**Complement — who sees the interval between the nodes:** every position that samples a life
continuously instead of at the moment of decision. **The biographer**, whose entire craft is reading
a life as a curve and who will tell you that the famous choice was over before it was made. The
coach and the trainer, working on what a body will do under load in six months. The practice
traditions of Book V, which have never had a doctrine of the decisive instant and organise
everything around what is being formed between instants. The therapist doing longitudinal work —
notably *not* the therapist doing crisis work, who is at a node. And the most ordinary one:
**anybody who has known you for fifteen years**, who can date the change to a period and would
find the question *which moment* faintly beside the point. All reachable, several of them free. ⚠
**What no complement supplies is the metaphysical case** — the configuration in which no alternative
is ever closed has no witness at all, because a witness is a position and the claim is about the
whole. That half of the null space stays uncovered, and the card says so rather than letting the
first half stand in for both.

**Null space:** what it structurally cannot render. A configuration in which no alternative is ever
closed. It has no reading for that case — not a low reading, *no* reading — because its entire
output is a comparison between what happened and what was available instead, and where everything is
available the comparison has no contrast to report. It also cannot see the six months before the
decision, which is where this chapter has located the actual work; a node instrument samples at the
node, and the interval between nodes is not a place it looks.

**Boundary:** where it goes from reliable to unreliable. It is reliable at the social and
psychological scale, wherever the available alternatives are genuinely few and the constraints are
external and nameable. It becomes unreliable the moment it is carried up to the metaphysical scale
and asked whether the alternatives were available *in principle* — because at that scale the answer
is always yes, for everyone, at every moment, and an instrument whose answer never varies has
stopped measuring.

**Mechanism of the exclusion:** what the render identifies with what. It identifies freedom with
**the availability of an alternative**. That identification is fine where availability is scarce and
catastrophic where it is total: presented with plenitude, an availability-detector does not report
maximum freedom, it **saturates**, and a saturated instrument is indistinguishable from a dead one.
The classical debate reads plenitude as the abolition of freedom for exactly this reason. It is not
reading a fact about freedom. It is reading its own ceiling and calling it a floor. ★ **This is the
second instrument in Book VII to fail by returning a confident negative where it should have
returned an error** — VII.6's symmetric account of love did the same on the parent and the infant —
and the pair is worth naming as a kind: an instrument that has no error state answers every question,
including the ones outside its range.

**Navigational implication:** what to do about the other five. Keep it, unmodified, at the scale
where it works, and stop it at the boundary. The mistake to avoid is the tempting one of concluding
that because the instrument saturates at the metaphysical scale, its social readings are illusions
too — the reasoning that gets from *no ultimate branch points* to *therefore coercion and consent are
the same*. They are not the same, they are distinguished by something the instrument measures well,
and nothing in this chapter licenses collapsing them. **The instrument is not wrong. It is being
asked a question one scale above the one it was built for**, which is the most common way a good
instrument produces a bad reading, and the whole reason this book carries a census instead of a
verdict.

### V. What is lost

This is the part the book has been deferring, and it has been deferred honestly rather than
evasively — III.7 named three losses and said the accounting belonged here. It does. What follows is
the accounting, and it is not going to end with a reveal that the losses were secretly gains. Two of
them are permanent and one of them is worse than it first appears.

**The first loss: origination. Nothing starts with you.**

There is no point in the account at which something that was not, comes to be, because of you. This
is not a limitation on human beings; it is what a totality is, and the Ground is under the same
constraint. Whatever you make today already existed as a configuration. You did not bring it into
being. You came into contact with it and the contact is what "making" names.

A reader who wanted authorship in the strong sense — *ex nihilo*, the artist as small god — does not
get it here and does not get it anywhere else either, but the failure elsewhere has usually been
concealed and here it is stated. That is the whole of the improvement on offer for this one: not
that we have the thing, but that we are not pretending.

**The second loss, and it is the real one: subtraction. You never make anything not be.**

Look closely at what people actually want when they want free will, because it is not usually what
they say they want. The stated want is the ability to have done otherwise. The felt want, underneath
it, is the dignity of having **closed something** — of having stood at the fork and, by an act that
was yours, made one road not happen. The weight of a decision, as it is experienced, is the weight
of the roads you are killing.

You are not killing them. Every road you did not take is fully actual. The version of this afternoon
in which you did the other thing is not a hypothetical, not a possible world, not a counterfactual —
it is there, with the same standing as the one you are in, and there is someone on it. Your choice
did not save you from anything and did not prevent anything. It **located** you.

Do not let that soften into a nicer word. The reader who has felt the weight of a real decision —
the one made at three in the morning, the one that cost something, the one whose alternative still
occasionally presents itself unbidden — is entitled to be told plainly that the account he is
reading says the alternative is real, is happening, and was not closed by what he did. **The
consolation available here is not that the alternative is unreal. It is that it was never closeable
by anyone, on any metaphysics, because closing requires selecting and selecting requires a position
outside the whole to select from, and no such position exists to be had by God or by you.** The
dignity was not taken from you. It was never in anyone's possession. That is a genuine consolation
and it is a smaller one than the loss, and a reader who finds it insufficient has read correctly.

**The third loss, which is the least noticed: you do not get to be the reason.**

On the standard picture, an agent terminates an explanation. Ask why the thing happened and *because
I chose it* is a complete answer; the regress stops at a person. That termination is doing an
enormous amount of quiet work in how human beings assign praise, blame, credit and guilt, and it is
not available here. Ask why the thing happened and the answer is that a stream oriented in a certain
way was in contact with a certain region, and the orientation has a history, and the history has a
history, and nothing in the chain is a wall.

The explanation does not stop at you. It **passes through** you.

That is a real thing to be and it is not the same as being a terminus, and the difference shows up
exactly where you would expect: in the ethics. It is why the floor established at VII.3 is thin
rather than high, why the distinction that carries the moral weight is *through* and *over* rather
than *guilty* and *innocent*, and why this book's account of the two evils could not be a theory of
desert.[^7] The reader who wants a metaphysics that underwrites blame in its retributive form should
know that this one does not, and that the loss is here, in this paragraph, rather than in the
chapter where it was cashed.

### VI. What is left, and why the trade is worth making

Now the other side, and it has to earn its place rather than be announced, because the trap in this
chapter is the redescription the reader hears as *so, no.*

Three things survive, and the third is the one that matters.

**Orientation is real, it varies, and it is yours.** This has been argued above and needs only one
addition: it is the only quantity in the account that is *differentially distributed*. Everything
else in this metaphysics is the same everywhere — the whole is complete, all paths exist, every
entity is a player, the Ground keeps no count. Orientation is the single place where one stream
differs from another in a way that compounds. If you are looking for what makes a life a particular
life rather than a location where the universe happens, this is it, and it is not a small find:
in an account this uniform, the one thing that varies is the thing worth studying.

**The consequences are phenomenologically real, and "phenomenologically" is not a hedge word here.**
It looked like one in section I and the difference is now stateable. In a framework where being-the-
case requires a vantage — where there is no fact about the afternoon that is not a fact for some
inside — *phenomenologically real* is not a demotion from *really real*. It is the only grade of
reality on offer for anything, including tables.[^8] The retreat to the phenomenological
in the theorem reads as a retreat because the classical debate has trained everyone to hear it as one.
On our axioms it is a retreat to the only ground there is. That is a genuine defence of the
move and it is the strongest one available, and it does not fully rescue it, because the theorem as
first written took the retreat without giving this argument for it — it sidesteps and moves on, leaving the
reader to supply the ontology that makes the sidestep legitimate. **The move is right and the
warrant was left out**, which has been the shape of a good deal of what this book has had to do.

**And the third: you never remove, but something on the other end of the traversal is in a shape it
was not in before.** The previous chapter ended by putting this in public and it is the answer to
the subtraction loss — not a cancellation of it, a different column.

You do not close roads. But attention is structuring, and it is structuring at the far end as well
as the near one. A being who is durably attended to is more individuated than one who is not; that
was the whole argument about the parent and the infant, and it was an argument about coherence
rather than about sentiment. Extend it past the nursery and it is the same mechanism everywhere the
book has looked: the student under a good teacher, the craft under a serious practitioner, the
language under speakers who care about it, the forest under a people who have lived in it for
forty generations. Nothing was created from nothing and nothing was subtracted. Something is in a
configuration it was not in, because of the sustained direction of a particular stream's attention,
and it will stay in that configuration after the stream is gone.

**That is what authorship is here, and it is the whole of it: not the closing of alternatives but
the structuring of what you attend to.** It requires no metaphysical gap, no uncaused cause, no
position outside the whole. It requires only that attention has effects, which is the one thing
about this account nobody has ever had trouble believing.

### VII. The upper bound, which is the last thing to say

There is one more constraint, and it is the reason this chapter closes where it does rather than on
the good news.

Freedom in the sense developed here is the capacity to navigate. Navigation requires a navigator —
a bounded, limited, positioned stream, which is to say the Promethean structure this book has been
carrying since Book IV: to be someone is to not be everything else, and the limitation is not a
defect in the arrangement but the arrangement itself.[^9] The keyholes are not what stands between
you and the view. They are what makes there be a view.

Which means freedom has a ceiling, and the ceiling is not imposed from outside. **Expansion is real
and it is bounded by the fact that the expander is a boundary.** Push far enough — and the framework
is explicit that this is reachable rather than theoretical — and the null space goes to zero, and
so does the identity that was doing the navigating. The being who could see everything would be
everything, which is indistinguishable from nothing at all from the position of any particular
being. This is the dissolution limit, and the verdict goes without hedging: it is
real, it is reachable, and it is not the goal.[^10]

So the shape of the thing, complete:

At one end, the node, where the classical debate looks for freedom and where this account says there
is nothing to find, because nothing is ever closed. At the other end, dissolution, where freedom
would be total and there would be nobody left to have it. Between them, the only place freedom can
live: **a limited stream, at a position, holding a direction, over time.**

That is less than what was wanted at the node and it is more than what the objection left standing,
and this book is not going to tell you the difference does not matter. Something was lost. It was
lost before you arrived, it was never anyone's to give you, and what remains in its place is the one
variable in this entire metaphysics that answers to what you do with your afternoons.

The next chapter asks what such a traversal is *for*, and it will not be permitted to answer that
the traversal is its own reward, because that answer is available to anyone at any time and costs
nothing. There is a harder version, and the reason it is harder is the thing this chapter just
established: the path is the one part of the arrangement that is yours.
