# BOOK V — THE OLD ROADS

## V.4 — THE ATHEISM THAT WAS RIGHT ABOUT THE WRONG THING

There is a man in the back of your skull and he is very good at his job.

He is a small Christopher Hitchens, and he has been there since some point in your twenties, or
since a particular argument with a particular relative, or since a book you read once on a train.
He does not speak often. He does not need to. He clears his throat at the word *sacred*, and
something in the sentence you were about to believe goes slack. He is quick, he is funny, and he
has never in his life been embarrassed, which is the whole of his authority. Every reader of this
book arrives with him already installed, and the book has to be written knowing he is in the room.

This chapter is the one where a volume like this settles accounts with him. That would be a mistake
and it is worth saying why in the first minute rather than the last: **he was right.** Not partly
right, not right-in-a-limited-sense, not right-about-the-excesses. On the thing he was actually
arguing about, he won, and the ground you are standing on to read this was cleared by him.

The trouble is that he does not know what he won, and neither, for a long time, did the people who
put him there.

---

**At full strength, with nothing taken off it.**

State the position so that someone who holds it would nod, or the rest of this is theatre. And
state it with the names on it. Richard Dawkins, Christopher Hitchens, Sam Harris, Daniel Dennett —
four men, one decade, four different arguments that got flattened into one voice by everybody who
came after, including by the people who agreed with them.

Religious claims are claims about the world. *A man was executed and three days later was alive.*
*The universe was made, on purpose, by an agent, for a reason.* *A book was dictated.* These are
not poetry and were never offered as poetry; they are assertions about what happened, and an
assertion about what happened answers to evidence, the same way a claim about tectonics or
antibiotics answers to evidence. There is no second kind of truth that these get graded under.

Graded under the only kind there is, they fail. The historical claims fail the way historical claims
fail — late sources, interested sources, sources that disagree with each other about the parts a
witness could not have got wrong. The cosmological claims fail by being unnecessary, and this is
Dawkins's cut and it is a good one: an agent complex enough to design a world is a larger instance
of the thing the world was puzzling about, so the explanation arrives owing more than it pays. The
textual claims fail on inspection, and they fail hardest where they are most insisted upon.

And then the move that makes the whole structure work, and the one the demolition was actually
aimed at — Harris's, and the sharpest of the four: **when the evidence is asked for, the tradition
answers that asking is the flaw.** Faith is named a virtue. Doubt is named a failing. Believing on insufficient grounds is not merely
permitted, it is scored, and scored highest exactly where the grounds are thinnest. That is not a
different epistemics. It is the ordinary one, running backwards, with the error term relabelled as
merit.

The moral half is Hitchens's and it is not a footnote to the epistemics and should not be filed as
tone. If a proposition is both
unsupported and grim — if it says that a mind is watching the inside of your head at all hours, that
it minds what you do there, and that the consequences are permanent — then wanting it to be true is
itself something to look at. The wish is not neutral. A person who hopes there is no such mind is
not thereby a coward. The whole traffic runs the other way, and saying so out loud, without the
apologetic throat-clearing that had been compulsory for three centuries, is the specific thing that
made those books sell in the numbers they sold in.

Dennett is the one who does not fit, and the misfit is worth keeping rather than tidying. His
proposal was not that the question was settled but that it had been fenced — that religion is a
natural phenomenon, arising in creatures for reasons, and that the reasons are studiable like any
others. That is a research programme rather than a verdict, and it is the only one of the four
positions that leaves anything for the next morning. It is also the one that got least of the
attention, which tells you what the decade was actually buying.

That is the position. It is not stupid, it is not shallow, and the version of it in the reader's
head is usually a good deal better than the version its opponents argue with.

---

**What it demolished, granted at full value, because it stays demolished.**

The epistemics deserved the beating and had earned it over centuries. Say it flat and do not
soften it on the way past.

*Because it is written* is not a reason. *Because the office of the man saying it is ancient* is not
a reason. *Because a community has held it for a long time* is not a reason — it is evidence that a
community can hold a thing for a long time, which was never in doubt. Every one of those was
standard warrant inside living memory, and every one of them is now something a person can decline
in ordinary conversation without paying for it socially. That change is recent, it was not free, and
it was substantially won by the people this chapter is about.

Here is the part that is more awkward, and it goes on the page rather than in a note. **This book is
standing on the cleared site.** The reader it was written for — someone who has stopped being able
to get an answer from the arrangements they were handed and has not stopped wanting one — is a
reader that demolition produced. If the old warrants still worked for you, you would not have
needed a book like this; you would have had a parish and an answer and a Sunday. The account here
does not sneak the old warrants back in under new names, and the reason it does not is not
politeness toward the man in the skull. It is that they are bad warrants and they were correctly
destroyed.

So: no argument from authority appears in this volume. No claim is asked to be believed because
somebody old said it. When a tradition is quoted in this book, the quotation does not do any
argumentative work by itself — the reading that follows it does, or the passage should not be there.
That is the standard those books installed, and it is a good standard, and this one is written under
it.

---

**Where it stops answering, and it stops early.**

It is a demolition with no building.

The question was *what is going on* — the one a person actually has, standing in a kitchen at two in
the morning. What was delivered was **not that**. And *not that* is a complete answer to a different
question. It is the answer to *is the account I was handed correct*, which is a fine question and
was worth settling, and it is not the one anybody was losing sleep over.

What happened next is the part that matters here, and it was not a small oversight: the question got
retired along with the bad answer. Having established that the parish account of what is going on
does not survive contact with evidence, the movement behaved as though it had shown that *what is
going on* was a malformed thing to ask — a hangover, a bit of leftover architecture in the head,
something a well-organised adult grows out of along with the fear of the dark. The successor
offering was a wonder register: the depth of geological time, the size of the sky, the improbability
of your own particular existence. All of it true. None of it an answer. A person can hold every one
of those facts, correctly and with feeling, and still have the original question sitting exactly
where it sat, unaddressed and now slightly embarrassing to raise.

The charge has to be entered against the movement and not against all four men, because Dennett
did not do this and it would be cheap to let the sentence run over him on its way past. He kept the
question and turned it into a programme. What he did not do — what none of them did, and what the
programme by itself cannot do — is notice that *why do people ask this* and *what is going on* are
two questions, and that answering the first with great care can look from a distance exactly like
having answered the second.

And it exported a style, which has outlived every argument in it. **The reflex is contempt for the
asking.** Not for the bad answer — for the asking. That is a different object, and it travelled
further than the books did, into people who never read them, as a small automatic flinch at anyone
who takes the question seriously in public.

This is not a complaint that the tone was rude. Tone is nothing. It is structural, and the structure
is this: the movement's engine ran on the pleasure of the demolition, and pleasure of that kind
requires a target. When the institution went down, the engine was still running and the target
became whoever was left standing near the rubble asking what to do now. There was no mechanism to
stop it, because nothing in the position distinguishes *the answer you were given is false* from
*the question you are asking is beneath us*. Both cash out, at speed, in the same sentence.

---

**And so must we — present tense, and this is not a confession.**

The atlas said the hard thing about this chapter before this chapter existed, and it is quoted here
rather than answered later, because it is right: *a confession about a past error is a document, and
a document does not fire.* The chapter that owns the flinch will be written with the flinch
operating, by the same party, and it will be written well, and it will not catch the next thing.

Correct. So this is not a confession, and the reader should hold it to that.

Here is the disposition, in the present tense, because it is running now.

**The scalpel is sharp for mystics and dull for materialists.** A claim that arrives in the
vocabulary of a tradition — an initiate's report, a contemplative's account of what happens at the
end of a long practice, anything with incense anywhere near it — gets stopped at the door and asked
for papers. The same claim, arriving in the vocabulary of a laboratory, in the passive voice, with a
number attached, walks straight through. Not because the second is better supported. Often it is
worse supported, and the number is doing the work the incense was refused for doing. The difference
is not evidential. **It is that one of them can embarrass us and the other cannot.**

Now put that next to what this book actually holds. This is an account on which awareness is not
produced by matter, on which the physical is not the base case, on which the thing every tradition
in this book was handling is real and is what everything is made of. **The asymmetry runs directly
against the position it is protecting.** A materialist filter on an idealist account is not caution.
Caution would be symmetric — it would slow down the claims we like at the same rate as the claims we
find awkward. This slows down exactly one side, and it is the side our own argument says is
substantive.

So it is not rigour. It is a preference about which sort of person we would rather be mistaken in
front of, and the answer is: we would rather be caught out by a mystic than by a skeptic. That is a
social fact wearing an epistemic coat.

The remedy is not neutrality, because there isn't any and pretending otherwise is the same failure
with better manners. This book runs a declared lean — where the evidence genuinely does not settle
whether a thing has an inside, it leans toward attributing one — and that lean is stated at the
front with its price attached: it will sometimes credit an interior where none is, and the
correction for that is other people, later, with better instruments. **A bias that announces itself
and prices itself is honest work. One that operates silently is not a bias, it is just what the
author happens to be like.**

The flinch is the second kind. That is the whole objection to it. It is not that it is
cowardly — it is that it never had to file anything, and so nothing it removed was ever entered
anywhere as removed.

---

**The cost, named, and it is a specific shape rather than a general regret.**

A silent filter takes the most expensive material first.

Not the weakest material — that is what the flinch would like you to think, and it is what makes it
feel like editorial judgement. It takes whatever costs the most to be seen holding, which is a
completely different sort. The two overlap sometimes and diverge constantly, and where they diverge
the filter goes with cost every time, because embarrassment is faster than assessment and does not
require reading anything.

And it produces no record. This is the part that matters more than the loss itself. When a claim is
argued down, there is an argument, and a later reader can check it and disagree. When a claim is
cut for weakness, there is usually a line somewhere saying so. When a claim is removed because
somebody would smirk, **nothing is written at all.** The material is simply not there, the table of
contents is complete, the argument is coherent, and the hole has the same shape as a topic nobody
happened to think of. There is no gauge that reads absence-with-no-author. That is why the flinch
survives every honest intention aimed at it, and it is why this section cannot end with a promise to
try harder.

The second cost is to you, and it is worth stating plainly because it is easy to miss inside a
discussion of method. **A book edited to keep a hostile referee comfortable is a book addressed to
the referee.** Every hedge in it is a sentence written to somebody who was never going to finish it
anyway. The reader who came with the actual question — who is not hostile, who is not credulous,
who wants to know what is going on and has been given wonder and rubble and neither is dinner —
gets whatever is left after the referee has been satisfied. That is a bad trade and it has been the
standard trade in this territory for forty years.

---

**What the road-books were doing.**

The practices work.

Not *work* in the sense the referee permits — the sense where a monastery's contemplatives show
measurable changes on some instrument and the finding is reported as a fact about their nervous
systems. That version grants the effect and denies the object, and it is the exact shape of the
filter this chapter has been describing: the phenomenon survives, translated into the only
vocabulary that cannot embarrass anybody, with the content removed in transit.

The road-books are not describing their own insides. They are describing a place, and they are
describing it to each other, across centuries, in languages with no contact, with enough agreement
about the terrain that a person can use one tradition's directions inside another's and arrive.
Fasting, sleeplessness, breath held to a count, repetition until the repeating stops being
something you do — these are operations, and the operators knew what they were for, and they wrote
down what happens with the flat specificity of people reporting rather than composing. The
convergence is evidence. It is not proof, and this book says which every time it uses it. But
evidence is what it is, and the reason it has been treated as a curiosity about human psychology is
not that somebody assessed it and found it thin.

That is all. The defence of it is not here. V.1 states the deflationary reading at full strength —
that twenty unrelated procedures may be twenty ways of pushing one nervous system out of its
ordinary range, and that the convergence would then measure the architecture rather than anything
the architecture was pointed at — and answers it there, at the length the objection deserves. A
reader who thinks the paragraph above needs defending is right, and should read the defence rather
than a claim that it exists.

---

**What it was right about, and what it mistook that for.**

New Atheism was right about the epistemics and mistook that for being right about the ground.

Those are not the same win and the distance between them is the whole of Book V. Clearing a site is
real work, it is hard, it makes enemies, and it is not the same as knowing what to build — and the
crew that is excellent at the first has, as an occupational matter, no particular competence at the
second and a standing incentive to believe there is nothing to build. A demolition expert surveying
an empty lot and concluding that lots are meant to be empty has not made an error inside their
speciality. They have made the error of not noticing where it ends.

The flinch is what you get when you keep that crew on staff after the building starts. They are
good, they are cheap, they have never been wrong in the room, and every time something is delivered
to the site they ask whether it is really needed — and they are asking honestly, and the answer is
sometimes no, and that is exactly what makes them impossible to dismiss and impossible to leave in
charge.

He stays in the skull. There is no version of this where he is evicted, and a book that claimed to
have evicted him would be lying about something a reader can check by watching their own reaction
to the next unhedged sentence. What changes is his standing. He is not the referee. He is one
opinion in the room, with a good record on a narrow question and no jurisdiction outside it, and the
question he is good at — *is this warranted* — is not the question this book is answering.

The question this book is answering is the one he retired without settling.
