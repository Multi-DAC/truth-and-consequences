# BOOK III — THE GAME

## III.8 — WHAT THE METAPHOR CANNOT DO

Seven chapters have been spent inside one metaphor. A metaphor that is never tested in public is not
a frame; it is a faith with a vocabulary.

So the test is run here, where it can be watched — not as a note of modesty appended to a confident
book, but at the same volume as everything else, because the limits of the game are part of the
account and not an apology for it. There is a self-interested reason to do it in the open. **A limit
you state is a limit you have measured. A limit somebody finds for you is a limit you did not know
you had**, and the difference between those two is most of what a reader is deciding about when they
decide whether to trust a book.

---

**Start with the vocabulary, because that is where a metaphor does its work and where it will have
its way if nobody is watching.**

There are words that belong to the game, that any reader who has ever played anything will supply
without being asked, and that this book does not use.

**Save.** A save is a state held somewhere the state is not — an original, kept, so that a later
running can be started from it. Two things are required: a copy, and a place to put it. Neither
exists here. Nothing is stored, nothing is fetched, and the whole of what is the case where you are
is made at the meeting and nowhere else. To speak of a save is to reintroduce the elsewhere by the
side door, three chapters after the front one was closed.

**Quest.** A quest is assigned. It has a giver, an objective and a reason you in particular were
picked, and the moment the word is used the reader is holding all three. That is the maker coming
back as a job board. He was removed at the start of this book, and he does not get to return in
the frame's own uniform holding a list of tasks with your name on it.

**Sandbox.** A sandbox smuggles two things at once, and they are opposites. It says there is a
serious world somewhere else that this is not, and it says nothing here costs anything. The first is
the elsewhere again. The second is the error the second chapter of this book spent its length
removing: stakes are not lowered by there being no one outside to keep score, they are raised, since
what is lost here is not lost in a practice mode kept safe by an original somewhere.

**Level.** Already refused, out loud, at the definition: in the game, a grade is not a level. What is
earned is earned *from* somebody, and there is no gate here and nobody standing at one. That refusal
has been in force since the term was defined; it is on this list because a list of refusals with a
live one missing is worse than no list.

**Respawn.** Refused hardest, and refused for a reason that is not the same as the other four. It
will be given at the end of this chapter, because it is the reason the whole rule of this chapter
takes an exception.

**Now the pattern, which is the actual finding and is not visible from any one item on the list.**

These are not five temptations of five different kinds. **Each of them smuggles back exactly one
thing this book has already spent a chapter removing** — *quest* the maker, *save* and *sandbox* the
elsewhere, *level* the gate, *respawn* an interval on the other side of cessation that no argument
here has established. Every word on the list is a refused claim, returning in the frame's own
clothes.

Which is precisely what makes them dangerous rather than merely wrong. A reader who met *there is
somebody outside who assigned you this* as a sentence in plain English would want an argument for it
and would notice not getting one. The same claim arrives inside *quest* with no argument attached
and no place to attach one, because it is not being asserted. It is being presupposed by a noun the
reader has already agreed to.

---

**The mechanism has a name, and it was worked out in 1954 by somebody with no stake in any of this.**

**Max Black** read a paper to the Aristotelian Society called simply *Metaphor*, collected in 1962
in *Models and Metaphors*, and it is the reason the vocabulary question above is not fussiness. He is arguing against the comfortable view that a metaphor is a decorative substitute for
something you could have said plainly. His counter-view is that the metaphor's own subject brings a
whole system of implications with it, and that the system does work on the thing being described.
One link upstream and he says so himself: the interaction he is describing he takes from **I. A.
Richards**, *The Philosophy of Rhetoric*, 1936 — Richards' two ideas *active together* — and Black's
contribution is to say what they are active *with*.[^1]

His example is not a poem. It is this chapter's problem, arriving seventy years early:

> Suppose I am set the task of describing a battle in words drawn as largely as possible from the
> vocabulary of chess. These latter terms determine a system of implications, which will proceed to
> control my description of the battle. The enforced choice of the chess vocabulary will lead some
> aspects of the battle to be emphasized, others to be neglected, and all to be organized in a way
> that would cause much more strain in other modes of description.[^2]

*Control* is his word and it is the load-bearing one. Not *colour*, not *suggest*. The vocabulary is
not a set of labels laid over a description that was going to happen anyway; it decides which
features of the battle are available to be mentioned. And the mechanism is the one the five words
demonstrate: **you cannot import a word without importing its neighbours.** They are what the word
means. A term arrives carrying everything a competent speaker already believes about the thing it
names, and it delivers all of it at once, unlisted, in the time it takes to read.

That is why a metaphor which runs ahead of the argument starts doing the thinking. It is not that the
writer gets lazy. It is that the frame is generating claims faster than the argument is generating
grounds, and both arrive at the reader in the same sentences, in the same voice, indistinguishable.
What was argued for and what merely came with the furniture end up on the same
page looking identical — and the reader, quite reasonably, credits the whole page to the argument.

**And the cut, since Black is not defending us.** He is describing a hazard for someone who has a
battle to check against. His describer, and his describer's audience, can walk out and look at the
war. Ours cannot. The subject of this book is not available for independent inspection by the reader
in the way a battle is, which means the frame's implications have no external referee here. **A
metaphor's authority is inversely proportional to the reader's independent access to the thing being
described, and our reader's access is the lowest it ever gets.** So the fence has to be explicit and
written down. Taste will not do it.

---

**Then the largest implication of all, which the game brings whole and this book denies entirely.**

The word carries a boundary. Every game anyone has played is bounded — it starts, it ends, it takes
place somewhere, and there is a life it is not. That is not an accident of the games that happen to
exist. It is close to being the definition, and the man who made it one was **Johan Huizinga**, in
*Homo Ludens* in 1938:

> Play is not "ordinary" or "real" life. It is rather a stepping out of "real" life into a temporary
> sphere of activity with a disposition all of its own.

> All play moves and has its being within a play-ground marked off beforehand either materially or
> ideally, deliberately or as a matter of course.[^3]

He is right about every instance he had. The chess board has an edge, the pitch has a line painted
round it, the ritual has a threshold you cross to enter, and inside that **bounded space** the rules
hold and outside it they do not. Marked off *beforehand* — before the play, by somebody, for the
duration.

Ours has no edge. There is no ordinary life this is a temporary sphere within, because there is no
*within*; there is nothing on the other side of the line for the game to be a holiday from, and no
moment at which it was marked off, because marking off is something done inside what is the case by
something that is already in it. What Huizinga describes as play's condition is, for us, exactly the
elsewhere that was refused at the ground. **The frame's most defining feature is one we deny**, and
we deny it while going on using the frame.

What we take from him is the name, because a thing you deny still needs one. The bounded space is
what every reader means by a game, and saying so plainly is cheaper than letting them assume we
meant it.

**Now notice what all of that had in common.** The five words, and the boundary that is larger than
all five together, are refusable. Each is an entailment — a claim the metaphor makes — and a claim
can be stated, examined and denied in a sentence the reader can follow and disagree with. That is
the manageable half of the problem, and it is manageable precisely because the metaphor's claims can
be brought into the open and made to answer.

---

**The other half is not refusable, and it is the one place this frame genuinely strains.**

Black saw it, one page after the passage everyone quotes, and put it in a subordinate clause:

> …the vocabulary of chess has its primary uses in a highly artificial setting, where all expression
> of feeling is formally excluded: to describe a battle as if it were a game of chess is accordingly
> to exclude, by the choice of language, all the more emotionally disturbing aspects of warfare.
> (Similar by-products are not rare in philosophical uses of metaphor.)[^4]

Read the mechanism in that: *by the choice of language*. Not by anything asserted. The game register
excludes feeling the way a room excludes weather — not by ruling on it, by being indoors. Nobody
writes *and none of this hurts*. The sentence is never made, so it is never available to be denied,
and it arrives anyway.

**An entailment can be refused and a connotation cannot.** You can write *in the game, a grade is
not a level* and the reader will take the correction, because you have named a claim and struck it.
You cannot write *in the game, nothing is trivial* and have it take. The word goes on meaning what
it means. A reader does not consult your definition when a word does its work on them; they consult
everything the word has ever meant to them, which is the weather around it, and no author has ever
had jurisdiction over the weather.

And the weather around *game* is that it does not finally matter. That is not a mistake the reader
is making. It is what the word is for. A game is the thing whose stakes are agreed to rather than
imposed, and that single feature is why the frame has carried everything it has carried up to here
and why it has one subject it must not touch.

**So, plainly.** A woman whose child has died is not in a game. Say it to her in this book's frame
and the sentence is not merely tactless, it is false — and false in a way argument cannot repair,
because what is wrong with it is not its content but its key. The content might survive translation.
The register will not. Everything the game does well it does by making consequence *legible*, and
the way it makes consequence legible is by making it the kind of thing a person agreed to.

That is the strain. It is not that the frame is the wrong shape. It is that it is in the wrong key
for the one subject on which being in the wrong key is a lie.

---

**Which brings the rule, and it has been in force since the first page of this book.**

*If a claim cannot be said in the game, that is evidence the claim is not yet clear. It goes back. It
does not get a second metaphor built for it.*

The second half is the half with teeth, and the reason for it is worth stating rather than assuming.
A new analogy always looks like generosity toward the reader. It is usually the opposite: it is how
a confused claim escapes discovery. Each fresh metaphor arrives with a fresh system of implications,
free to do the work the argument has not done, and the reader — who is following the sense of
increasing illumination, which is a real sensation and not a foolish one — experiences the arrival
of a new frame as progress. A book can run three hundred pages that way, sounding explanatory
throughout, and never once have explained anything. The single sustained metaphor is not an
aesthetic preference. **It is a device for finding out when we are stuck**, and it only works if we
are forbidden the escape.

The rule has already cost this book claims, and the five words above are the visible part of the
bill.

**And it takes one exception, stated here in public rather than discovered later by a reader who
would be right to feel misled.**

**The test does not apply where the game is barred in the first place.** The bar is a criterion and
not a list of chapters: **the game is barred wherever the subject is irreversible loss borne at a
position.** Where that is the subject, the claim is not tested against the frame at all, because
failing to be sayable in the game is not evidence of anything there.

The wording is exact and the exactness is the point. *Harm done to a person* would have been the
natural phrasing and it would have been a trap, because Book VII is going to say that cessation is
not an event for the one ceasing — which is precisely the denial that anything was done to them. A
bar written to protect a chapter must not contain the premise that chapter refuses. Loss borne at a
position asks a different question, and one this book can answer: is there somewhere that this is
being undergone? For the woman whose child has died, yes, and that is the whole of it. For the one
who has ceased there is no position left for anything to be borne at — and the bar still covers the
chapter, because the fear belongs to a reader who has a position now and knows it will not always
be occupied.

Without the exception the rule misfires exactly where it can least afford to. The hardest true things
this book has to say concern affliction and cessation, they are not in the least unclear, and they
are unsayable in this register — the second of them unsayable because *respawn* is what the register
would reach for, which is the whole reason that word is refused and not merely avoided. A rule that
returns *not yet clear* on the clearest sentences in the book is not a strict rule. It is a rule
that will be quietly abandoned the first time it is inconvenient, and after that it protects nothing
anywhere.

So the promise attached to it, which a reader may hold us to: where that subject arrives, the game
is put down. Not softened, not translated, not carried over with the sharp edges filed off — put
down, and the plain sentence used, and nothing that was lost by putting it down is smuggled back
later by a metaphor.

---

Book III has used one frame for seven chapters and taken it as far as it goes. It removed a maker
and did not hand the game to anyone else. It made *already there* stop meaning *already decided*. It
put the render at the point of contact and left nothing anywhere else. It found no props in the
world and no scenery in the crowd, described the stack of filters every position looks through, and
established that the walking, which makes no road, makes the walker.

None of that was proved by the game. **A metaphor cannot be evidence, and no sentence in this book
is true because it sounded right in the frame.** Every one of those results was argued in plain
terms, in Book II's vocabulary, where it could be refused. What the game did was make the results
inhabitable — and inhabitable is a real thing to have made, since an account you cannot live inside
is one you will put down before it has finished, but it is not the same thing as true and it never
converts into it.

The frame is a lens. You have now been handed the lens and the list of what it distorts, at the same
time and in the same voice, which is the only arrangement under which a reader can use one safely.

What comes next takes the census: everything that has an inside, counted, one grade at a time.
