# BOOK III — THE GAME

## III.3 — THE WHOLE GAME IS ALREADY THERE

The last chapter ended on a word that has to be taken off the reader before it does damage.

**Already.**

In ordinary use it reports a position: earlier than now. The parcel already came. The decision was
already taken. Something sits behind the present, and the present arrived to find it sitting there.
Read that way, the sentence this chapter exists to defend is not merely false but incoherent — it
would put the whole of what is the case at the far end of a stretch of time that would have to be
somewhere inside it, and the first chapter of this book spent its length establishing that there is
no such stretch and nobody standing at the near end of it.

So the word is doing something else here, and it is smaller and stranger than the ordinary sense.
*Already* means: **not waiting on anything.** Not pending, not queued, not conditional on an event
that has yet to come off. When this book says that every configuration is already there, it makes no
claim whatever about when they got there. It denies that getting there is a thing that happens.

That is the whole chapter. The rest of it is what that costs and what it buys.

---

**The flat version first, in the register this book has chosen.**

Every state the game can be in, it is in. Not one after another — all of them; and *all at once* is
not a moment either, it is the denial that they are spread across any. Nothing is authored: no
studio, no build, no ship date, no patch. And then the part that does the most work and gets noticed
least. **There is no runtime.** A game has two halves and both are refused here. There is the half
where it is made and the half where it is run — the first somebody working, the second a machine
executing, each an interval with the other on the far side of it. Neither is available. Nothing was
built and nothing is being executed.

Which sounds like a refusal to answer the obvious question. If not from a build and not from a
runtime, where did the states come from?

**They did not come from anywhere, and *come from* is the part to give up.**

A game has exactly two ways of having content in it. It can ship with the content, finished, on the
disc; or it can carry rules for producing content and produce it as it goes. Baked or generated.
Every argument anyone has ever had about how a world could be big enough happens between those two —
and both of them are ways of *paying*. One pays in advance and stores the result; the other pays on
demand and computes it. Both need a payer. Both need a medium to hold the receipts. Both put the
world downstream of an operation performed on it.

This is neither, and not because a cheaper third method turned up. **Nothing is being paid for.** A
state does not have to be produced in order to be one of the states. What makes an arrangement one of
the arrangements is not that something arranged it — that chair was taken away two chapters ago —
it is that nothing about it is impossible.

*Possible, therefore actual.* Said in that order it sounds like a conjuring trick, an argument won
out of a dictionary. It is not, and the reason is the one that removed the maker: **a totality with
nothing outside it has nothing left over to do the excluding.** For a possible arrangement to fail
to be one of the arrangements, something would have to be keeping it out — a filter, a shortage, a
decision. Every one of those is a party with a position, and there is no position. The bar is not
that a state earned its way in. There is no door, and nobody on it.

---

**And now the man who wrote this down eighty-five years ago, in fiction, correctly.**

The frame this book runs on has a father, and he was an Argentine librarian going blind. In 1941
Jorge Luis Borges published a story that is not an allegory of our position but a statement of it,
delivered in a dry municipal voice with the vertigo left in.

The universe, in *The Library of Babel*, is a library. Hexagonal galleries, air shafts, spiral
stairways, two lamps to a room, a mirror in the hallway. Every book in it runs to four hundred and
ten pages and nearly all of them are gibberish — and then a librarian of genius works out why. All
the books, however different, are built from the same twenty-five orthographical symbols; and no two
books in the Library are identical. From those two premises he deduces the thing:

> the Library is total and … its shelves register all the possible combinations of the twenty-odd
> orthographical symbols (a number which, though extremely vast, is not infinite): Everything: the
> minutely detailed history of the future, the archangels' autobiographies, the faithful catalogues
> of the Library, thousands and thousands of false catalogues, the demonstration of the fallacy of
> those catalogues, the demonstration of the fallacy of the true catalogue, the Gnostic gospel of
> Basilides, the commentary on that gospel, the commentary on the commentary on that gospel, the
> true story of your death, the translation of every book in all languages, the interpolations of
> every book in all books.[^1]

Read the list for what it declines to do. It does not select. The true catalogue is in there, and so
are the thousands of false ones, and so is the proof that the true one is false. Completeness is not
a collection somebody assembled well. It is the absence of any principle of admission. Nothing was
let in, so nothing was kept out.

And the principle itself, the one this chapter has been circling, Borges puts in a footnote — the
smallest print in the story, appended to an aside about ladders:

> I repeat: it suffices that a book be possible for it to exist. Only the impossible is excluded.[^2]

That is not an image of our claim. It is our claim, in eighteen words, from 1941. The last chapter
reached the same sentence from the other end — *what can be, in a totality with nothing outside it,
is* — and a short story getting there first is not a charming coincidence. It is what happens when
somebody takes completeness seriously for long enough to notice that completeness has no editor.

---

**Two things have to come off it, and both of them are in Borges's own text.**

The first arrives in the same breath as his best sentence, which is why he is an ancestor and not an
ally. Here is the first of the librarian's axioms, entire:

> First: The Library exists *ab aeterno.* This truth, whose immediate corollary is the future
> eternity of the world, cannot be placed in doubt by any reasonable mind. Man, the imperfect
> librarian, may be the product of chance or of malevolent demiurgi; the universe, with its elegant
> endowment of shelves, of enigmatical volumes, of inexhaustible stairways for the traveler and
> latrines for the seated librarian, can only be the work of a god.[^3]

Two sentences. *It has no origin* — and *it was made.* The Library is from eternity, which is to say
that nothing stands before it; and the shelving is so elegant that only a god could have put it up.

He even reaches for the right name in passing. *Malevolent demiurgi* — precisely the figure the
first chapter of this book removed, kept on here as an explanation for the librarians while the
building itself is handed to somebody better. The reflex survives the demotion of its object. Refuse
the maker of the world and he comes back as the maker of the shelves.

Borges is not being careless. He is writing a man, and the man is devout, and the portrait is exact.
What it is, is the reflex this book named three chapters ago, performed by the most sympathetic
witness available: given an inside, the mind reaches for someone outside who put you there, and it
builds him out of whatever the century has to hand. The furniture is always local. Aeons then,
compute now — and in a story about a library, shelves.

The second thing to remove is the library.

Not the completeness. The *architecture*. Hexagons have walls. Air shafts have sides. A spiral
stairway sinks and soars, which means there is a direction to go and somewhere that it goes. The
narrator feels this pressure and gives the best answer available from inside a building: *The
Library is a sphere whose exact center is any one of its hexagons and whose circumference is
inaccessible.* The first half is exactly right — no privileged room, no gallery that is the real
one. The second half is the part we cannot keep. A circumference that cannot be reached is still a
circumference, and to have one at all is to be the sort of thing that is on the inside of an edge.

Then Borges gives the furniture away himself, in the last footnote of the story, in somebody else's
voice. Letizia Alvarez de Toledo observes that the vast Library is useless: strictly speaking a
single volume would suffice, of ordinary format, containing an infinite number of infinitely thin
leaves.[^4] **The shelves were never load-bearing.** They were a way of seeing it, and taking them out
costs you the room and nothing else.

So the metaphor keeps what it is for — totality, no author, no admissions policy — and drops the
building. What is left is harder to picture, and that is the correct sign rather than a defect. Every
image of completeness that can be pictured has an outside, because picturing is done from somewhere.

---

**The Library is the whole game seen from nowhere. Borges wrote the other half too, and in the other
half somebody is inside it.**

*The Garden of Forking Paths*, the same year. A sinologist named Stephen Albert explains to his
visitor what the incoherent novel of the visitor's own great-grandfather actually is:

> In all fictional works, each time a man is confronted with several alternatives, he chooses one
> and eliminates the others; in the fiction of Ts'ui Pên, he chooses — simultaneously — all of them.
> He creates, in this way, diverse futures, diverse times which themselves also proliferate and fork.[^5]

Then the case, and the case is this chapter's. Fang has a secret; a stranger calls at his door; Fang
resolves to kill him. The intruder can die. Fang can die. Both can escape, both can die. *In the work
of Ts'ui Pên, all possible outcomes occur; each one is the point of departure for other forkings.*
No branch is the real branch, and none of them was pruned.

What the second story has that the Library cannot show is that this is not a claim about a book. It
is a claim about what reading one is like. Ts'ui Pên's novel is unreadable as a story and perfectly
coherent as a place. Albert has not recovered the plot; he has discovered that there is no plot to
recover, and that what he is doing instead of following one is **walking**.

Which is why Borges gives the totality to a man in a garden, at night, with a revolver already in the
scene. *We do not exist in the majority of these times; in some you exist, and not I; in others I,
and not you; in others, both of us.* And then the last thing Albert says, minutes before he is shot
in the back by the visitor he has been explaining it to — offered kindly, with a smile, as the
correction of a compliment: *Time forks perpetually toward innumerable futures. In one of them I am
your enemy.*[^6]

The complete structure and the particular evening, in one sentence. That is what this book means by
a walker, and it is why the frame is a game and not a diagram.

One more thing from that story, because it is a warning about this one. Ts'ui Pên's novel is about
time and never once uses the word — *in a riddle whose answer is chess, what is the only prohibited
word?* *The word chess.*[^7] This chapter has the same trouble with *already*, and has been paying the
tax in periphrasis since its first line: not pending, not queued, not waiting. The tense is not
available. Every English sentence about a totality that is not in time has to be built out of words
that are.

---

**Now the objection, which every reader has been holding since the second paragraph.**

If every state exists, then the state you are in exists no matter what you do, and so does every
other one, and the whole thing is a recording. *Already there* means *already decided*. You are not
walking. You are being played back.

**Those are not the same claim. They are opposite claims, and the distance between them is one
missing person.**

To be decided is to have been selected: one taken, the rest refused. That is what the word means,
and it is why *already decided* always smuggles in a decider. Fatalism, in every version anybody has
actually feared, says that **one** path holds and the others are shut to you. Plenitude says all of
them are there. A picture in which every outcome exists is the only picture in which no outcome has
been picked — because picking is subtraction, and there is nothing standing outside the whole to
subtract with.

The other half of the answer is in Borges's list, and it is the most useful thing in it. The Library
contains the true story of your death. It also contains thousands and thousands of false catalogues,
several hundred thousand imperfect facsimiles of every volume differing in a letter or a comma, and
the demonstration of the fallacy of the true catalogue — shelved indistinguishably, with no index
that is not itself one of the books. **A totality that holds your fate and every counterfeit of it,
with nothing to tell them apart by, has said nothing to you about your afternoon.** Completeness is
total about the whole and exactly as uninformative about the next hour as it was before you heard of
it. It is not a schedule. Nothing in it is an appointment.

What it *is* to walk a path that is already there — what choosing amounts to when every branch
exists — is the hardest question in this book, and it is not answered here. It gets its own chapter,
four along. Settling it in a paragraph is how a book earns a reputation for having settled it badly.

There is also a cost, and Borges states it better than any opponent of ours has managed: the
certitude that everything has been written *negates us or turns us into phantoms*.[^8] He is not wrong
about how it feels from inside, and nothing here disarms it. That reply is Book VII's and it takes
the whole of Book VII. He is the strongest thing anyone has said against us, and he is filed with
the ancestors rather than the opponents.

---

Take away the author, take away the building, take away the schedule, and what is left is a totality
with no edge, no history and no index: every arrangement that is not impossible, none of them
scheduled, none of them selected.

And a reader in a room, with a floor underneath and a wall at arm's length.

Nothing about the whole game accounts for that. A totality does not arrive — nothing arrives
complete. What arrives is one room, at one moment, at one grade of detail, and it is precisely as
much world as there is somebody standing in it. That is not a smaller copy of the whole game.

It is what the whole game is like where something has met it, and that meeting is the next chapter.
