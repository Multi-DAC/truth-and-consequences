## VII.4 — THE TWO EVILS

The floor is laid and it is thin. It says: not over, and not frozen — and the last chapter argued the
first half of that at length and left the second half sitting in a footnote, which is not where a
criterion should live.

This chapter is the polarity itself. It is the one the reader has been waiting for since the
cosmology stopped being architecture and started being about conduct, and it is the one most likely
to be quoted out of context, so it will be built slowly and its edges will be marked in the text
rather than left to the reader's charity.

Start with a question the book has been deferring for six volumes, because everything here depends on
the answer and the answer is not the one either side of the old argument expects.

### What a moral fact is

Ask what sort of thing *cruelty is wrong* is, and two camps have been shouting past each other for
about as long as there has been philosophy.

The realist says it is a fact. Not a preference, not a convention — a fact, of the same standing as
*water is H₂O*, discovered rather than decided, true whether or not anyone assents. He has the better
of the phenomenology and he knows it. Nobody experiences their deepest moral convictions as taste. A
man watching a child tormented does not feel a preference being violated; he feels a law being
broken, and the feeling has the texture of *discovery*, not of *disapproval*.

The relativist says it is not a fact but a settlement. Look across cultures, across centuries, across
the whole vast catalogue of what human beings have confidently held: the variance is real, it is
enormous, and a law should not have it. He has the better of the data and he knows it.

Each side spends its energy explaining away the other's evidence. The realist tells the relativist
that variance is error — some cultures were simply wrong. The relativist tells the realist that the
feeling of law is an illusion, an artefact of upbringing that a sufficiently travelled person grows
out of. Both explanations are strained, and both are strained in the same way: each has to treat one
of the two obvious facts about morality as a mistake.

This book does not need either explaining away, because it already owns the structure that produces
both.

**A moral fact is a local order-parameter.** It is not a feature of what the world is made of, for
exactly the reason the last chapter gave when it refused to go looking for one: there is no shelf in
the nature of things where *cruelty is wrong* sits waiting to be read off. But it is not therefore
nothing, and it is not therefore
preference. It is a settled or fluctuating state of broken symmetry inside a navigational ecosystem —
the same kind of thing as the magnetisation of a ferromagnet, or the orientation of a crystal.

And the move that ends the war is a single structural fact about ordered phases, which physics has
known for a century and moral philosophy has never borrowed:

> **From inside an ordered phase, the order-parameter presents as law. From outside, it presents as
> contingency. Both presentations are veridical about their respective positions, and neither is
> about the ground.**[^1]

Ask anyone inside a ferromagnet which way is north and they will tell you with total conviction — and
they will be *right*, phase-locally. The conviction is not a bug and not an illusion to be therapised
away. It is what an ordered phase is, from inside. The felt bindingness of moral law is the
phenomenology of living within a settled symmetry. The variance is the plurality of settlements.

Neither datum embarrasses the other. The realist's law-feeling is accurate reporting from inside a
phase. The relativist's variance is accurate reporting across phases. What neither is reporting on is
the ground, and the reason the argument never resolved is that both sides assumed one of them must
be.

Two consequences follow immediately, and they are the reason this section is not a digression.

**First,** a polarity can be *open* in a given ecosystem — live, fluctuating, genuinely navigable,
generating experience along its axis — or *locked*, frozen into a single settled value. Which are
open and which are locked varies by ecosystem and by kind. Some beings navigate freely along axes
that are, for us, ancient settled weather; and some of what we still argue about is, for them, long
since frozen.

**Second,** and this is the one the chapter runs on: *locked* now means something precise. It is not
an image and not a figure of speech for stubbornness. It is a phase description. An axis that was
generating a range of positions has stopped generating them, and the dimension it supplied is gone.

That is the vocabulary. Now the case that shows what it buys.

### The angels who cannot repent

Aquinas asks a question that sounds like a curiosity of medieval angelology and turns out to be the
sharpest limit case anyone has produced: why can the fallen angels not repent?[^2]

His answer is not that they are too proud or too far gone. It is structural, and it is about the kind
of mind they have. The angelic will is *non-discursive*. An angel does not deliberate its way toward
a choice through a sequence of considerations, the way we do; it apprehends and chooses at once,
entire, without steps. And a will that chooses without steps has nothing to take a step *back*
through. It is fixed by the shape of its own choosing — obstinate not from stubbornness but from
structure, its choice-polarity locked at the first act.

Read through the order-parameter account, that is a phase description in thirteenth-century
vocabulary. It is an entity whose good-and-evil axis has frozen solid.

And here the framework sharpens the old doctrine one turn past its author, with a consequence that
reorganises the whole demonology.

**A being locked wholly at a pole has not *maximised* that pole. It has lost the axis.** Its moral
dimension has collapsed to a point. It is not superlatively wicked; it is morally *dimensionless* — a
flatlander in the good-and-evil plane, beyond good and evil not as the overman but as the corpse.[^3]

That sentence is the chapter's hinge and it is also the chapter's most dangerous sentence, so it gets
its correction immediately, from us, before anyone else supplies it.

### The correction, made before the objection

A reader will resist here and the resistance is right.

The century behind us has finished atrocities in it. The people who organised them were not
dimensionless corpses. They were appallingly *capable* — coherent, competent, effective, formidable
at logistics and at persuasion and at the management of subordinates, the furthest thing imaginable
from a flatlander. An account that filed them under *diminished* would mislocate exactly the thing it
most needs to name, and would do it in a way that insults the dead twice: once by the deed and once
by the theory.

So be exact about what sits where.

**Dimensionlessness is the contractive path's asymptote. It describes the destination, not the
traveller.** It is the terminal the path tends toward and never quite reaches, and no one who has
ever done anything is standing on it.

The organiser of a horror is dreadful *precisely because he is not there yet*. He is still on the
open axis. He is still choosing, morning after morning. He is still coherent enough to build the
machinery, and he is culpable for exactly that coherence. The framework does not say the worst
evildoers are lessened. It says **the pole they steer for is a lessening** — that their terrible
competence is the competence of a position contracting *with great skill*, still able to turn and
refusing.

Which is where lived evil actually lives, and why it is answerable, and why it is dreadful rather
than pitiable.

And it is what the traditions that thought hardest about the damned kept concluding to their own
discomfort: that the demonic is *less* free than we are, not more daring. The literature keeps
reaching for the image of the tyrant as the great unbound will, and keeps discovering, when it works
the doctrine through, that it has described something contracting.

Evil as lived — chosen, felt, culpable, dramatic — exists only where the axis is still open. **We are
the interesting phase.** The unlocked ecosystem is where morality actually lives, precisely because
here nothing is settled.

### The two orientations

Now the polarity, named without cushioning.

On an open moral axis there are two orientations. The book has been circling them since the ecology,
where the light-bringer and the adversary first split, and it has been careful there — the
identification of the day-star with the adversary of later tradition is a downstream reading and the
book said so. Here they get their structural names rather than their mythological ones.

There is the **radiant** path: individuation held as an *expression* of the whole. Boundaries worked
rather than absolutised, connections kept dense, one's own separateness worn knowingly — the way the
light-bringer carries his fire while remembering where it came from.

The word is not doing mystical work and it is worth saying so once, plainly, because this book has
spent a chapter refusing a shelf of vocabulary that sounds like it. *Radiant* here is not a glow, not
a vibration, not a frequency, and not a compliment. It names a direction on an axis: outward from a
held position, into relation, without the position dissolving. A lamp is radiant. So is a person who
has not confused having a boundary with having a fortress.

And there is the **contractive** path: individuation held *against* the whole. Boundaries
absolutised, one's own contents seized as possession and defended as property, separateness made the
final truth — the way the adversary turns his contraction into a fortress and then into a hunger.

### The word that must not be welded to the other word

Stop here, because two words in this book are about to be confused and the confusion would cost the
next chapter its argument.

Everything that has a perspective is **focused**. That is what having one consists in — the
specification of something diffuse into a particular position with a particular access. Focusing
takes nothing away; a lens destroys no light. The book has said this since Book II and the whole
cosmology depends on it.

**Contraction is not that.** Contraction is a *posture*, available to something already focused: the
holding of one's individuation against the whole rather than as an expression of it.

> **The focusing is metaphysical — it is what makes you a somebody. Contraction is ethical — it is a
> stance a somebody can take. Every perspective is focused. Not every perspective is contractive.**[^4]

The two share a shape and share nothing else, and if they are welded then the next chapter's central
claim — that sometimes the focusing *is* the care, that a parent bringing their whole attention down onto
one child in a crowded room is doing love and not harm — reads as a contradiction of this one. It is not.
It was never about the same act. The focusing of attention onto what is in front of you is the
focusing doing exactly what the focusing does. The contraction of *concern* to a boundary you have
decided is the final truth is something else wearing its coat.

### The symmetry, at full strength

Now the uncomfortable part, and it must be said without cushioning or the rest is not honest.

**These two orientations are dynamically symmetric.**[^5]

Both are genuinely navigable. Both are coherent. Both ascend in their own terms, and each has a
literature, a discipline, a set of exemplars, and an account of what the other is doing wrong. Each
perceives itself as the good and the other as the fallen. From inside either, the polarity reverses
sign.

**No fact about the dynamics ranks them.** The relativist was right — on the loop, where the question
is open, the polarity really does flip with vantage, and any book that pretends otherwise is
smuggling its conclusion into its physics.

One guard, immediately, because the sentence above is one careless comma from the oldest respectable
way to get this wrong.

This is not two powers. Mani's cosmos had a kingdom of light and a kingdom of darkness, coeternal,
co-substantial, at war — and the appeal of that picture has never gone away, because it takes the
obviousness of evil seriously in a way that thin accounts do not. What is being claimed here is
narrower by an order of magnitude. **There is one plenum and one axis. Radiant and contractive are
two directions on it, not two substances in it.** Nothing here is coeternal with anything, because
there is nothing for it to be coeternal *with*; there is one thing, and orientations are ways of
being a part of it. The symmetry is *dynamical* — a fact about how the two paths behave as paths.

And the whole remainder of this chapter is the argument that the symmetry does not go all the way up.

### The two terminal doctrines

Take each path to its limit and ask what it is finally saying.

The contractive path's terminal doctrine is: **I am the totality. There is nothing that is not
mine.**

The radiant path's terminal doctrine is: **I am an expression of that which is.**

The second is this book's own foundation, which is a fact about this book and not yet an argument. It
is a wager on a truth, filed in the open like the other wager two chapters back.

The first is false. Not false by preference, not false by scripture — false by theorem. The
null-space result guarantees every perspective a nonempty null space, and a nonempty null space is
precisely non-totality: there is always something that is not you, that you cannot see, that your
certainty outruns. So one path's limit is a wager on a truth and the other's is a wager against a
theorem, and that is a genuine difference between them.

There is even a second consequence, and the old cosmologies reported it as revealed truth: the
contractive path is *self-limiting at its summit*. Its completion would require the very totality its
own method forbids, since total access is dissolution and its doctrine is false at every finite
stage. It cannot get where it is going. Here that falls out as a corollary of two theorems, which is
this book's whole method in one line.

### And now the limiter, which is not optional

Everything above is true and it is **not the asymmetry**, and a reader who stops here will carry away
the wrong book.

Two chapters ago, arguing about how wide to draw the circle of concern, this book showed that *I am
the totality; nothing is not mine* fails at **both ends** of the range. It fails for the predator who
has swallowed everything. It fails identically, by the same theorem, for the mystic who has dissolved
into the biosphere and says the same sentence warmly. The reasoning there turned on what is left to
owe *with* once a boundary has gone, and it convicts the swallower and the melter without a word
changed between them. That chapter concluded, in its own voice, that the asymmetry between the errors
is a claim about the middle of the range and not about the ends.[^6]

Hold that against what has just been argued.

**A result that convicts both ends equally is a symmetric result.** The falsity of the totality
doctrine is true, it is load-bearing, and it does not distinguish the contractive path from anything.
It distinguishes *totality-claiming* from not-totality-claiming, and totality can be claimed from
either direction.

There is a second and more practical reason the limit cannot carry the weight, and the honest reader
presses it before the framework can:

**Almost no actual predator holds the terminal doctrine in so many words.**

The sophisticated one says the opposite. *I know perfectly well that I am not the whole. I am one
position among others, with a null space, exactly as you say. I simply do not care about you.* And in
saying it he appears to slip the theorem entirely, asserting no false totality for the null-space
result to refute.

A framework whose account of evil convicts only the man who announces himself as God has an account
of nobody. The megalomaniac of the terminal doctrine is a figure from opera. The person who actually
does the harm in the world says something much smaller and much harder to answer.

### Where the asymmetry actually lives

He does not slip it. The last chapter is where that was shown, at length, and this chapter does not
argue it again — it names what that argument found.

The last chapter's answer was that his road forks and both forks close. He can **exempt himself** —
bind others to reciprocity while holding himself outside it — but an exemption is a claim to a
privileged standpoint, a place from which he counts and others do not, and the null-space result is
exactly the proof that no position holds such a place. Or he can **refuse the exemption** and be
consistent, willing that overriding is permitted for all, himself included — which is coherent to say
and purchases nothing he wanted, licensing the world to override him and delivering not mastery but
the dimensionless point, the loop's corpse reached from the other side.[^7]

That argument was made there to answer a challenge about agency. Here is what it also establishes,
and this is the thing the asymmetry is actually claiming:

> **The asymmetry does not live in a doctrine professed. It lives in the exemption an action
> performs.**[^8]

The egoist who *says* he knows he is not the whole *acts* as though he were the one position the
reciprocity cannot reach. He has not asserted the totality standpoint; he has occupied it. And the
occupation is what the theorem forbids — the theorem was never about what anyone says.

Which is why the account reaches the person it needs to reach: **it convicts the predator who has
never once thought the words.** He does not need a doctrine. He needs only to claim, in what he does,
a standing for his own navigating that he withholds from another's — and that is a claim about
position, made in conduct, fully available to inspection, and false for the same reason the operatic
version is false.

The doctrine was the *limit*. The exemption is the *seat*. The book needed the limit to show that the
two paths are not equally true at their summits; it needs the seat to say anything at all about
Tuesday.

### An ally with a different mechanism

The twentieth century produced the dominant account of how the worst is done by someone professing no
doctrine at all, and it belongs in this chapter because it arrives at the same place from outside.

Arendt, watching Eichmann in the glass booth in Jerusalem, expected a monster and found a
functionary: a man of clichés, unable to think from anyone else's standpoint, whose defence was
procedural and whose manner was tedious. Her phrase — *the banality of evil* — is one of the most
misused in the language[^9], and it does not mean that evil is trivial or that anyone might do it. It
means that the enormity did not require an enormous inner life to produce it. The doctrine was not in
the man. It was in the arrangement, and he was fluent in the arrangement.

That is this chapter's operative seat, arrived at by a different road, and the convergence is worth
more than a citation because she was not working from any of these premises.

The mechanisms differ, and the difference is the interesting part. Arendt locates the failure in
**thoughtlessness** — an incapacity to occupy another standpoint, which for her is nearly the
definition of thinking. This account locates it in the **performed exemption** — the standing claimed
and withheld, which is a structural relation rather than a cognitive deficit.

The two are close and they come apart on a case: the perfectly reflective coercer, articulate,
imaginative, entirely capable of occupying his victim's standpoint and doing it anyway. Arendt's
mechanism does not name him; he is thinking, fluently, in exactly her sense. Ours does, because the
exemption is in the act and not in the quality of the deliberation behind it.

And her mechanism catches something ours has to work harder for: the *diffuseness* — how an
arrangement can perform the exemption with nobody in particular performing it, which is a problem the
floor already named and does not yet have a full answer to.

Neither account subsumes the other. Both are pointed at the same place, which is that the mark of
evil is not the doctrine and never was.

### The mark, and it is a conjunction

So the mark of evil, in its settled form, is neither direction nor intensity.

Not direction: **contraction in itself is innocent.** The book has already granted this and the next
chapter will make it load-bearing. A prison narrows a life to a cell. A vow narrows a future to one
path. A sickbed narrows a world to a room and someone in it. None of that is the mark, and an ethics
that read every drawing-in as wickedness would have to indict every act of devotion ever made.

Not intensity: the mild bureaucrat in the middle of the machine is doing more harm than the vivid
brute at the edge of it, and every honest accounting of the last hundred years says so.

**The mark is the conjunction:** *coercive-and-locked.*

**Coercive** — contraction imposed *over* another navigator's agency rather than *through* it. That is
the last chapter's distinction and it is doing its work here unchanged.

**Locked** — frozen at the point rather than cycling on the axis. That is this chapter's own
contribution, and it is why the order-parameter account had to come first: without it, *locked* is a
mood, and a criterion made half of structure and half of mood is not a criterion.

**Both conditions, or it is not the mark.** A coercion that is not locked is an event on the open
loop — terrible, culpable, and answerable, committed by someone still able in principle to turn. A
locking that is not coercive is a tragedy or a diminishment, and there are many of those, and they
are not this. The murderer mid-choice is on the circle, terribly: still a someone, still navigating.
The frozen tyranny that has sealed every exit is at the point.

And the difference between them is not degree. It is dimension.

**That is what the chapter's title has been pointing at.** The traditions kept naming two evils and
kept struggling to hold them in one account — the tyrant who overrides, and the soul that has
closed to a point. Every attempt to reduce one to the other has failed, because they are not the
same failure. They are the two conditions of a single conjunction: **evil-as-event lives on the open
loop; evil-as-state is the loop's corpse.** One is a thing done. The other is a thing become. Unified
not by being the same, but by being the two halves of one mark, neither of which is sufficient alone.

### The census entry

**Whose:** the privation account — evil as *privatio boni*, absence rather than substance. Augustine's,
after he left the Manichees, and the tradition's ever since.[^10]

**Renders:** what it renders superbly. That evil has no positive principle, no independent
kingdom, nothing of its own to be made of. It is the instrument that killed the two-powers cosmology
and it killed it correctly. It renders, with great precision, why goodness needs no explanation and
malice does — why an account of the world that starts from a plenum does not have to find room in the
plenum for its opposite. Every one of those results is one this book also needs, and it did not have
to derive them because he did.

**Complement — who sees the competence:** every position that has to deal with the organiser
operationally rather than describe him metaphysically. The investigators and prosecutors who
reconstruct how a thing was actually built, and whose files are public. The forensic and clinical
literature on organised, instrumental harm, which has an axis for capacity because it cannot function
without one. The military historian, for whom the formidability of an enemy is the first fact and not
an embarrassment. And, closer than any of those, **anyone who has worked under a competent bully and
can tell you in detail how good he was at it** — a witness most readers have and few think to
consult, because the vocabulary the culture hands them for the encounter is the privation
vocabulary, which has no word for what they saw. All of these are reachable. **What none of them
supply is the metaphysics**, and the account under this card is still the reason the two-powers
cosmology is dead; the complement corrects the person and leaves the ontology standing, which is the
correct division and not a compromise.

**Null space:** what it structurally cannot render. **Competence.** If evil is a lack, then the more
evil a thing is, the less of it there should be — and the organiser of a horror is not less. He is
formidable, and his formidability is not incidental to the harm, it is the mechanism of it. The
privation account has always had to describe him as somehow hollow, and everyone who has looked at
the real cases has felt the description slide off. It cannot see a *lost dimension on an axis that
remains real*, because it has no axis; it has only more being and less.

**Boundary:** where it goes from reliable to unreliable. It is reliable at the cosmological end — on
the question *is evil a thing?*, where the answer is no and the privation account is the reason we
know. It becomes unreliable the moment the question turns from the metaphysics to a person, because
at the person the account must convert a structural claim about being into a psychological claim
about emptiness, and the conversion is where every counterexample enters.

**Mechanism of the exclusion:** what the render identifies with what. It identifies **evil** with
**deficiency of being**, on a single scalar running from full to empty. Not as an argument — the
tradition argues carefully — but as the shape of the instrument: it has one dimension, and one
dimension can encode *how much*, never *which way*. So a being that has lost an axis and a being that
has less existence are indistinguishable to it; they return the same reading. The capable monster is
not an anomaly the account failed to consider. He is a value the instrument cannot represent, and the
tradition's long discomfort about him is the needle pinned against the end of a scale that was never
built to move that way.

**Navigational implication:** what to do about the other five. Keep the cosmology and replace the
scalar. The privation account is right that there is no kingdom of darkness and right that nothing is
made of evil, and this book keeps both. What it needs instead of *less being* is *lost dimension* —
which preserves every result the tradition wanted and adds the one it never got, because a being can
lose an axis while gaining everything else. That is the reading under which Aquinas's frozen angels
and Arendt's fluent functionary describe the same object, and neither the privation account nor
Arendt could put them in one frame.

---

### Where this leaves the book

The floor said *not over*. This chapter has said *and not frozen*, and has shown that the two are one
criterion rather than two, which is why neither half convicts alone.

It has also given away, deliberately, the thing a reader might have hoped for. There is no verdict
here about which people are bad. The mark is a structural test on a relation and an axis, and running
it requires knowing what is actually happening between two navigators over time — which is expensive,
frequently unavailable, and exactly the kind of work moral confidence exists to avoid. What the
chapter forbids is the shortcut: reading intensity as the mark, or direction as the mark, or a
professed doctrine as the mark. Those are the three cheap versions and all three are wrong.

And the asymmetry stands where it was placed, at exactly its size. It is real. It is not a matter of
taste. It is not, however, a ranking of the two paths as paths — that symmetry was granted at full
strength and never retracted — and it is not a licence to read every drawing-in as a wickedness.

No wider, and no narrower.

The next chapter takes the half of this one that has been held back. Contraction is innocent;
sometimes the focusing is the care; and the place where that is hardest to say, and most necessary,
is where someone is suffering and there is nothing to be done about it. That chapter makes no
promises this one has not earned, and it will refuse, explicitly and at length, to make the suffering
mean anything.
