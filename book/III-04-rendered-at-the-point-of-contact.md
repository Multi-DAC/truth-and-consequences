# BOOK III — THE GAME

## III.4 — RENDERED AT THE POINT OF CONTACT

The last chapter left two things on the table and refused to put them together. On one side, a
totality with no author, no schedule and no edge. On the other, somebody in a room, with a floor
underneath and a wall at arm's length. Nothing about the first accounts for the second, and saying
so was the honest end of that chapter rather than a defect in it.

This one is the join.

**Procedural generation is what focusing looks like from inside.**

That is an identity, not a comparison, and the difference decides how everything after it reads. A
comparison would say the two resemble each other — that the world works *rather like* a game that
computes its terrain as you reach it — and a reader who takes it that way is owed the question of
how far the likeness runs and where it gives out. An identity is not owed that, because it is not
claiming two things and a resemblance. It claims one thing and two ways of putting it.

The first way is the one already made. *The Ground being the case somewhere in particular* — an act
with nobody performing it, stated in the grammar of the whole. That sentence is not spoken from
outside; there is nowhere outside to speak from, and the phrasing is careful precisely because it
has to keep the whole in view while standing nowhere. The second way is spoken from the only place
anyone has ever been, and it says: a world arrives, already made, exactly as far as you are in it.

Those are the same event. There is no third fact about which of them is the real one.

---

**And the first thing to establish about the arriving is that nothing arrives.**

Three denials, and they are not three qualifications on one claim. They are one claim, said three
times, because the reader has three separate pictures ready, and the third is the one they hold.

**Not stored.** There is no file this is a picture of. The scene sitting complete on a disk, waiting
to be looked at from a chosen angle, was taken away at the definition; the whole game as a warehouse
the render is drawn from was taken away last chapter, along with everything that could have stocked
it. What is left has no inventory. Not a hidden inventory, not an inaccessible one — no inventory,
in the way that there is no door and nobody on it.

**Not fetched.** Fetching wants two places and a trip between them: the world kept over here, you
standing over there, and something crossing. Nothing crosses. There is no over-here for it to leave.

**Not streamed**, and this is the one the reader actually believes, because it is how almost
everything they look at reaches them. The picture: the world sits finished on a server somewhere,
and what you get is a feed of it — delivered on demand, at the resolution the channel can carry,
buffered a little ahead of where you are. It is the most sophisticated version of the mistake and it
is the worst, because it needs everything the other two needed *and a journey as well*. An elsewhere
to hold the original. A channel. A rate. And a *before* for the crossing to start from — a stretch
in which the world is already finished and not yet yours. Duration is not the trouble; you have
duration, and a later chapter will need you to. The trouble is whose. This picture wants a stretch
of time that the world spends without you in it, which is a position outside everything, and the
first chapter of this book spent its length showing there is no such seat to occupy.

The three pictures differ in machinery and agree in form. Each of them puts the world somewhere
else, and then moves it.

**There is no elsewhere, and nothing moves.**

---

Which leaves a word that has been doing work for two chapters without being examined, and it has to
be examined now, because the last chapter used it to name something it refused and this one uses it
to name something it asserts.

*Generated.*

There, the objection was that a game has two ways of having content — baked or generated — and both
are ways of paying, and the states of the whole game are not paid for. Here the same word is being
used approvingly, of the render. That looks like a reversal and it is not, and the distinction is
one sentence long.

**A render is not a production.**

In the trade, generating is something a machine does. A procedure runs; cycles are spent; there is a
moment before the terrain and a moment after, and an engineer can measure the gap in milliseconds.
That is a production: an operation, with a duration, that leaves something standing where nothing
stood. None of that is what the word is here for.

What is borrowed is the other half of the same fact — the relation between what goes in and what
comes out. Nobody thinks that six sevens are forty-two *because* somebody multiplied them. Do the
sum on paper and there is certainly an event: the pencil, the ten seconds, the small effort. The
event is yours. The fact was not waiting on it, was not produced by it, and would not have been
otherwise had you never picked up the pencil. What the multiplying did was put you in front of
something that follows.

The game supplies the pencil, and the pencil is the part that does not come across. What comes
across is that a world can be **determinate without being stored** — fully specified, the same for
anyone who brings the same, and nowhere in particular until it is somewhere in particular. The word
was already stripped of its budget: nothing here is being economised, and a limit you meet is not a
machine's compromise showing through. It is now stripped of its clock as well. Generation, in this
book, is not an event. It is what follows from what.

---

**So: where.**

The world is drawn where a perspective meets the Ground. Not delivered *to* the meeting — the
meeting is not a transaction between two parties who were both already furnished and then made
contact. **The render is what the meeting is.**

That is easy to write and it is the hardest thing in this chapter to actually hold, because every
ordinary way of saying it reinstates one of the two parties as the one that was there first. The
strongest available help comes from a man who was not doing metaphysics at all. He was measuring
pilots.

James J. Gibson spent the war directing the Motion Picture Research Unit of the Army Air Forces'
aviation psychology programme — the assignment was visual aptitude tests, and the practical question
behind it was who could see well enough to fly — and the thirty-four years after it taking apart the
account of perception those tests had been built on. *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*
came out in 1979, the year he died. In it he gave the thing he had arrived at a name he had to
invent:

> The affordances of the environment are what it offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes,
> either for good or ill. The verb *to afford* is found in the dictionary, but the noun *affordance*
> is not. I have made it up. I mean by it something that refers to both the environment and the
> animal in a way that no existing term does. It implies the complementarity of the animal and the
> environment.

A surface affords walking on — to this animal, at this weight, with these legs. Not to a beetle,
which finds a plain where you find a stair; not to a cat, which finds a road. The affordance is not
a fact about the stair and not a fact about you, and Gibson says so in the sentences that make him
an ancestor rather than a source of quotable atmosphere:

> But, actually, an affordance is neither an objective property nor a subjective property; or it is
> both if you like. An affordance cuts across the dichotomy of subjective-objective and helps us to
> understand its inadequacy. It is equally a fact of the environment and a fact of behavior. It is
> both physical and psychical, yet neither. An affordance points both ways, to the environment and
> to the observer.

*Both physical and psychical, yet neither.* That is this book's central claim about what is the case
at a place, made in 1979 by an experimental psychologist who got there from stairs and cliffs and
the problem of seeing a runway, and who would have refused most of what this book does with it. It
is the best sentence anybody outside this frame has written for it.

And then, in the same section, he settles which way it really points.

> The organism depends on its environment for its life, but the environment does not depend on the
> organism for its existence.

Those two passages do not contradict each other, and their compatibility is the whole problem. He
can hold both, easily, and the price of holding both is fixed: the two-sidedness has to live in the
*offer* and nowhere else. The environment is there either way. The animal turns up, and what was
already the case acquires a second description relative to it. That is not a fact with two sides. It
is a fact with a label, and the label is the only part that needed anybody. Four sentences after
saying that the affordance is a fact of the environment *and* a fact of behaviour with neither
prior, he puts the environment first and leaves it there, and he means it — the environment as a
whole, he says, existed prior to animals, and had to be invariant for animals to evolve at all.

He is not being careless either. He had a reason, and it was the best reason available to him. His
opponents held that the meaning of things is added by the mind to a world that has none; Gibson's
whole argument was that the meaning is out there, in the light, free for the taking, so that no
inner apparatus has to build it. Making the offer belong to the object is how he got that. Set
against Koffka, who had said an object's pull is bestowed on it by a need — the postbox invites
letter-posting only when you have a letter — Gibson drew the line exactly where he had to:

> The affordance of something does not change as the need of the observer changes. The observer may
> or may not perceive or attend to the affordance, according to his needs, but the affordance, being
> invariant, is always there to be perceived. An affordance is not bestowed upon an object by a need
> of an observer and his act of perceiving it. The object offers what it does because it is what it
> is.

**He is right about Koffka and he pays for it with the world.** The correction we would make to
Koffka is the same one — a render is not solely yours, wanting does not put anything in it, and the
position that says otherwise is refused at the definition. But Gibson buys that refusal by making
one of his two poles independent, and an offer made by an object that is what it is regardless is
not two-sided at all. It is a property, of a thing, that happens to be described using a second
term. What he has, in the end, is a world complete before contact with a relational label attached
to its features. That is the trap this book's own claim is written to avoid, arriving in its most
respectable form: not *you make it up*, but *it is all there, and what it offers varies*.

The word he reaches for at the hinge is worth one moment. *Points both ways, to the environment and
to the* **observer** — a watcher, a party who arrives at a scene and takes it in. This book refuses
that word for exactly the property it carries here, and the most careful user of it in the
literature demonstrates the cost inside four sentences. A perspective is not a spectator. It is not
at the world; it is where the world is.

---

**And the correction is not ours.** It was made in 1991, on that same sentence, by the people who
took Gibson's two-sidedness and refused to pay for it.

Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson and Eleanor Rosch quote the invariance passage and answer it in a
line:

> In a nutshell, then, whereas Gibson claims that the environment is independent, we claim that it
> is enacted (by histories of coupling).

Their name for the position is *enactive*, and their statement of it is the closest thing in the
scientific literature to the sentence this chapter exists to make:

> cognition is not the representation of a pregiven world by a pregiven mind but is rather the
> enactment of a world and a mind on the basis of a history of the variety of actions that a being
> in the world performs.

**The half that gets dropped when that line is quoted is *and a mind*.** It is not a claim that the
knower makes the known; it is a claim that both terms come up together, and that neither of them was
sitting there in advance waiting to encounter the other. They were explicit about what that is for —
a way between the recovery of a world that is already finished and the projection of one that is
not, with both of those refused as versions of the same error. That is co-constitution, stated by
working cognitive scientists, with an experimental programme under it.

Where they stop is one word in their own definition, and it is *history*.

The world, for them, is brought forth by **a viable history of structural coupling** — a lived
trajectory, an organism and its milieu shaping each other over evolutionary and biographical time.
That is a magnificent account of how a particular animal comes to have the seed it has, and this
book will take it whole in the chapter on filters. It is not an account of what a render is. Two
things follow from it that cannot come across.

The first is duration. A bringing-forth performed by a history is a production after all — slower
and less anthropomorphic than a studio's, and still an operation with a before. Push the question
back one step and it returns: what is the case at a position *now*, before any of the history is
consulted? Their answer has to be that the now is the leading edge of the coupling, which locates
the render at the end of a process, which is the picture the whole of Book III has been taking
apart. **We keep the codetermination and drop the clock, exactly as we dropped it from the word
*generated* earlier in this chapter. It is the same subtraction, made twice, because it is the one
subtraction this frame is for.**

The second is the floor. Their world-bringing is done by organisms — autonomous, self-maintaining,
alive — and the warrant for the whole account is biological. That is a boundary with everything
non-living on the far side of it, and it is exactly the boundary the next chapter removes. One
sentence forward: if the render is at the point of contact, and contact is not a privilege of things
with metabolisms, then the account does not stop where biology does.

Set the two ancestors side by side and the pair says something neither says alone. **Gibson kept the
world prior. The enactivists fixed that and kept the process prior. Neither is prior — and *prior*
is the word this book has spent Book III taking off one pair at a time.** It came off the whole and
its origin: nothing stands before the whole, and nothing overflows from it either, because overflow
has a direction and a direction is priority in softer clothes. It comes off here at the last and
smallest pair, the one inside a single room. **There is no side of a meeting that the other side is
made out of.**

---

**Which can now be said in mechanics, which is the whole reason the frame was chosen.**

Two things are required and neither of them contains a world.

**The seed** is what you are — a body of this kind, these organs, a language with its joints, a
history, a stack of installed filters. It is not an opinion and not a preference. It goes in.

**The procedure** is not a program. Nobody wrote it, and nothing is following instructions. It is
the whole game: what can be the case, in the only sense of *can* this book allows — which is the one
where nothing is keeping the other arrangements out.

Neither is the world. A seed is a number until it is run; a procedure is a form until something is
run through it. And what comes out is not a compromise between them, not a blend, not one of them
filtered through the other. It is what the two come to, and it comes out the same for anyone who
brings the same, and it is the only place the thing is.

Which is what keeps this chapter's answer and the last one's from being one answer said twice. The
whole game is arrangements — every one that is not impossible, none of them scheduled, none of them
picked. A render is an arrangement with somebody standing in it. That is not a further ingredient
added to an arrangement; there is nothing to add, and nowhere to add it from. It is the difference
between a state of affairs and a world, and the difference is not in the state of affairs.

So the question that has organised the argument since Descartes — whether what is at a place is
made of mind or made of matter, and which of the two the other reduces to — is asking which of two
things the other is made out of, when neither of them is available on its own to be made out of
anything. There is no unrendered original and no unworlded knower. At a render there is a side that
is world and a side that is somebody, and they are not two items that met. They are what a meeting
has.

---

You have been looking for the seam. Everyone does; it is the natural next question, and it sounds
like a request for a detail rather than a category error. Where does my share stop and the world's
share start — which of these arriving facts are contributed and which are received, and if somebody
could go carefully enough, could the two piles be separated out?

They could not, and the reason is not that the seam is hidden or fine or beyond our instruments.

The seam is the room.

The wall at arm's length is not the world's half of a joint production, and the arm is not your
half of it.
There is a distance, and it is a real one, and it is what a body of this size standing in this place
comes to. That is not less than a world. It is the only form a world has ever taken, and the
question of what it is to be one of the many who are standing in one is where this book goes next.
