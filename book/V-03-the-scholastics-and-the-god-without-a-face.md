# BOOK V — THE OLD ROADS

## V.3 — THE SCHOLASTICS AND THE GOD WITHOUT A FACE

Three men, three cities, three languages, and all three of them said the thing this book says.

That sentence is the problem and not the achievement, and the chapter has to start by taking it
apart, because in the form just given it is worth nothing.

The previous chapter ended on Aquinas in Rome, Eckhart preaching in Cologne and Maimonides ruling
in Cairo, and it ended there for a reason that had to do with loss: a reader who left the church
because the face went should know that the tradition's own best minds let the face go first and
stayed. That was the point being made and it was made. It is not the point here. Here the question
is the one that ending raises and does not touch, and it is a flat, checkable, unfriendly question:

**When they arrived at a ground with no face, and this book arrives at a Ground with no face, is it
the same place?**

Not *is it similar*. Not *are they pointing in the same direction*. The same object, or not the same
object. Because if it is the same object, then twelve centuries of extremely careful thinking is
available as work already done, and this account has ancestors instead of a coincidence. And if it
is not, then the resemblance is a pun on the word *ground*, and leaning on it would be the cheapest
move in the book.

The first thing to do is take away the argument that would settle it too easily.

---

**The roll-call is one witness, and the previous-but-one chapter is what proves it.**

The obvious way to establish the identity is convergence: independent minds, no contact, same
report. That argument is not available for these three, and the reason is already on the page.

V.1 counted the instruments and found the number smaller than the roster. Plotinus goes into
Proclus, Proclus into the writer who signed himself Dionysius, and that body of work is in Latin by
the ninth century and in the hands of both Aquinas and Eckhart. The Baghdad translation movement
put excerpts of the same Alexandrian material into Arabic, and the Jewish philosophical tradition
Maimonides is writing inside received it there. **So the three names that closed the last chapter
are not three witnesses. They are one school, arriving in three languages, and a convergence
argument that counts them as three has committed the error it was built to prevent.**

This is a loss and it should be taken as one. It also does something useful, which is to close the
only road that would have let this chapter be a roll-call. Agreement between cousins establishes
that the transmission worked. It cannot establish that the object is there.

So the identity claim has to be made a different way, and the different way is the whole chapter.

---

**Augustine is here for the route, and the route is ours.**

Before the school, and off to one side of it, there is a man in North Africa doing something else.

Augustine does not reach the ground by climbing a ladder of denials. He does not begin with the
world and remove predicates until nothing is left that can be said. His instruction is the opposite
of an ascent and it is stated as an instruction: *do not go outside; return into yourself; truth
dwells in the inward man.* And when he finds what he was looking for, he describes its location with
a phrase that has been quoted for sixteen hundred years because nobody has improved on it — *you
were more inward than my innermost, and higher than my highest.*

Read that as a piece of method rather than as devotion. He is not saying that God is very deep
inside him in the way a memory is deep inside him. He is saying that whatever he finds by going in
is not another thing found at the bottom of the going-in; it is nearer to him than the innermost
part of him is, which means it is not located in the series at all. The search that goes inward far
enough stops finding contents and starts finding the condition of there being contents.

**That is this book's method, performed in the year 400, by somebody who had never heard of it.**
The whole approach here has been to take the inside view seriously and refuse to step outside it,
on the grounds that there is no outside to step to — and the man who wrote the *Confessions* got
to the same place by the same road, going in until in stopped being a direction.

And then he arrives somewhere this book does not arrive.

Augustine's terminus is a person. Not a face in the crude sense the last chapter was worried
about — he is far too careful for that, and he says explicitly that whatever you have understood is
not God. But the thing he finds is a *thou*. It hears him. It has a will and the will is directed at
him in particular. It is three and one, and the threeness is not decoration: it is how a ground can
be personal without being lonely, which is a genuine problem and a genuine answer to it. He goes in
by our road and comes out holding something we do not have.

**Both halves of that have to stay on the page.** A chapter that took the route and quietly dropped
the terminus would be doing the thing this book keeps accusing other people of — reading a tradition
until it agrees and stopping there. Augustine's method is ours and his conclusion is not, and the
gap between those two facts is the first evidence that route and terminus come apart. Which is
convenient, because the rest of the chapter is about a terminus.

---

**The claim, at full strength.**

Here it is with nothing around it:

**The Ground described in Book II and the God of the classical theologians are the same object.**

Not a parallel. Not an analogy that flatters both sides. *Actus purus* — act with no unrealised
remainder — and the Ground that is everything that could be the case, being the case, are two
descriptions of one thing, produced eight hundred years apart, by people with incompatible reasons
for producing them.

That claim gets attacked from two directions, and the attacks look opposite and are the same attack.

From the theologian's side: *you have taken a word that named the object of worship and emptied it
until it names a metaphysical posit. Your Ground is not God. It is the residue left when you removed
everything that made anyone care.*

From the other side: *you have taken a metaphysical posit and dressed it in a word that carries
twenty centuries of freight you have not earned. You smuggled God back in and gave him a capital
letter and a new job description.*

Both say the identity is a rhetorical move rather than a finding. **And both are correct that it
would be, if it were asserted and left there.** An identity claim between two descriptions is one of
the easiest things in the world to fake, because the descriptions are made of words, and words can
be made to overlap by anyone willing to be vague at the right moment. Every conversation in which
two traditions turn out to have been saying the same thing all along has been conducted at exactly
the altitude where the difference would have shown.

So it does not get asserted. It gets audited, and the audit has a rule that this book has already
committed to.

**Two descriptions may be held as one object only where they predict the same thing, and where they
diverge you must pick, and the divergence is where all the work is.** That rule was stated in the
atlas and it was not stated for this chapter's convenience; it was stated about a case where holding
both was tempting. It applies here without amendment. Everything the theologians deny of their God,
this book must deny of the Ground. Everything they affirm, it must affirm. **Every place the two
lists disagree is a place where one of the two accounts is wrong about a single object, and the
disagreement has to be resolved rather than admired.**

That is a test that can fail. Run it.

---

**Where it holds.**

**One: not a being among beings.** Aquinas is unambiguous and he is unambiguous in the central
document of the institution, not in a marginal work anyone can disown. God is not a member of a
class. There is no genus that has God and other things in it. He is not the largest item on any
list, not the first item, not an item. The scholastic apparatus for this is dense and its conclusion
is one sentence: whatever it means for a thing to exist, it does not mean that in the same sense.

This book says the same and says it as a rule that binds every sentence: the Ground is not the
server, not the developer, not the engine, not the map, not the player. Five denials, each killing a
different smuggled error, and the fifth was cut against a friendly source because it was the most
tempting one. **The lists are the same list. The scholastics arrived at it from the direction of
existence and this account arrived at it from the direction of what a perspective is, and neither
route left anything on the far side that the other route kept.**

**Two: no positive statement about what it is in itself is true.** Maimonides is the hardest version
and he means it without the softening that later readers keep applying. Every affirmative attribute
of the essence is false. Not *inadequate*, not *approximate* — false, and the honest form of speech
about God's essence is denial. Say what it is not. The list of what it is not can go on forever and
every entry is an increase in accuracy. What he does *not* deny, and this matters for how exact the
agreement below can be, is speech about what God **does**: attributes of action he admits as true
predications, and it is by that gate that the man who ruled the essence unsayable goes on reciting
the thirteen attributes of mercy.

This account carries the same rule under a different name and enforces it at the level of grammar.
Any sentence with the Ground as its subject is under a scope restriction, and the restriction is not
a courtesy. It is there because predicates belong to positions and the Ground does not have one, so
a sentence that gives it a predicate has already made it into a thing, several words before the
error becomes visible. **The scholastics fenced this with a doctrine and this book fences it with a
constraint on verbs, and the fences are in the same place — with the same gate in them.** Neither
fence is absolute and it would be convenient here to say both were. Maimonides lets through what God
does; this account's rule, stated exactly, restricts predicates that require a position to occupy —
wanting, lacking, falling, intending — and lets through what the Ground is the ground *of*. Two
gated fences, gated on close to the same principle, is a better finding than two absolute ones and
is the one the texts support.

**Three: it is not an addressee.** This is the cut the atlas made and it is cashed here rather than
argued again. Meeting requires an addressee with a position. What has no position has no inside;
what has no inside cannot be met, cannot be disappointed, cannot love back. The apophatic
traditions all built a fence at exactly that spot, and — this was the atlas's finding and it holds —
they built it pointing the *other* way from how it is usually read: not to protect the divine from
crude description, but to stop the describable one being inflated into the indescribable and lost in
the process.

Eckhart is the sharpest instrument on this point because he needed two words where everyone else
made do with one. *Gott* is God as met, addressed, prayed to. *Gottheit* is what is behind that, and
about the Godhead his position is that nothing whatsoever can be said, including that it is God.
**He is not describing two objects. He is describing what happens to one object when you stop
addressing it**, and this book's distinction between what is met and what everything met is made of
is that distinction, with the mysticism taken out and nothing else changed.

Three predicates, three agreements, and the agreements are not achieved by softening this side. The
Ground has not been given a warmer description to make it fit. The scholastic God has not been
thinned to make him portable.

---

**Where it fails.**

Two places, and they are not emphases.

**The first is the Good, and it is not close.**

For the classical tradition, being and goodness are convertible. This is not piety attached to the
metaphysics afterwards; it is a structural claim, worked out in detail, that to be and to be good
are the same fact under two descriptions, and that a thing is good exactly to the extent that it is.
God, who is *ipsum esse subsistens* — subsistent existence itself — is therefore goodness itself,
and is the end toward which every appetite in the world is actually directed whether or not the
appetite knows it. **That is not an ornament on their ground. It is what makes their ground the
foundation of an ethics.** Take it away and Aquinas's entire moral architecture has nothing under
it.

This account cannot say any of that.

The Ground here cannot play, and the reasons it cannot play are the reasons it cannot want: playing
takes not-knowing, stakes, duration, and an inside for the stakes to matter to, and it has none of
these. It is aware in the sense of being what awareness is made of, and not in the sense of being
aware *of* anything, because being aware of takes a gap and there is no gap. **A thing with no gap
and no inside does not have ends. It does not prefer. It is not the direction of anything's
appetite, because directions are had by things with positions.**

So: their ground grounds ethics and ours does not. Meaning in this account is neither issued from
above nor invented from below, and the *neither* is doing real work — it is the refusal of exactly
the move the scholastics make, in which value comes down from the ground because the ground is
value. There is no theodicy here, and the reason there is none is the same reason: a ground with no
preferences has nothing to justify.

**This book does not get to have the transcendentals, and it should stop being pleasant about that
for a moment and say what is lost.** What is lost is the answer to *why be good*, delivered as a
fact about the structure of reality rather than as a fact about creatures. The scholastics have that
answer. It is a real answer, it is coherent, and it follows from their ground. It does not follow
from this one, and there is a volume at the end of this account that will have to earn its ethics
somewhere else, from inside, without help from the floor.

**The second is what the ground does, and it is sharper.**

Aquinas's God creates. The creation is an act, it is free, and its freedom is load-bearing rather
than devotional: God wills things other than himself, and does not will them necessarily. There
could have been no world. Nothing in the divine nature required this one, or any. That contingency
is the hinge on which a great deal of the tradition turns, and removing it does not leave the system
standing with a small hole in it.

The Focusing is not an act. It is the Ground being the case somewhere in particular. It is not a
move, nothing is played, and there is no moment before it at which it had not yet happened and no
alternative under which it does not. **There is no could-have-been-otherwise anywhere in it, because
a could-have-been-otherwise requires a chooser with a position and an unrealised option, and both of
those are things this account has already ruled out of the Ground on other grounds entirely.**

So put the two lists side by side at the one place they touch:

> **Theirs:** the ground freely wills a world that need not have been.
> **Ours:** the ground has no will, and the world is what it is for the ground to be the case at a
> particular place, with no alternative.

**Those are contradictory predicates of a single object.** They cannot both be true of the same
thing, and no amount of respectful framing makes them compatible. The rule says pick.

**Picked: the Focusing.** Not because it is ours, which would be no reason at all, but because the
free-creation account needs a chooser, and a chooser is a position with an inside, and the same
tradition that asserts the free creation also asserts — in the same books, sometimes on the same
page — that this object has no position and no inside and is not a member of any class. **The
scholastics' own first three claims are what make their fourth one unavailable.** Aquinas saw the
difficulty and worked on it for a lifetime, and the machinery he built for it is the most impressive
thing in the corpus and it does not hold. The will has to be identical with the essence, and the
essence is necessary, and the effect is contingent, and a necessary cause identical with its own act
producing a contingent effect is not a resolution — it is the problem restated in a register where
it is harder to see.

---

**What that costs, without the clause that would pay it off.**

The tempting sentence at this point writes itself: *the identity holds on what the ground is and
fails only on what it does, and since the ground does not do anything, the divergence is
narrow.* That sentence is available, it is well-formed, and it is a rescue, and it should be
refused where it stands.

**A ground that wills is a different object from a ground that cannot will.** If the two accounts
are about the same thing, then one of them is wrong about that thing. This chapter has said which
one it takes to be wrong and given a reason, and the reason is an internal inconsistency in the
other account rather than a preference. But an internal inconsistency in their system is not a
verification of this one. **Both could be wrong. There is a live possibility that willing and
groundhood are compatible in a way neither the scholastics nor this book has found, and that the
correct account has a feature no one in this chapter is holding.**

And it would be dishonest to leave without saying what would settle it, so: the predicate that
decides this is contingency. If the world is contingent — if there is a real sense in which it might
not have been, and the sense is not merely that we can imagine its absence — then the ground has an
option, and something with an option is nearer their description than ours. If the world is not
contingent in that sense, they are wrong and the whole free-creation apparatus is a very careful
answer to a question that does not arise.

**Nobody in this chapter can run that test.** There is no observation, from inside a world, that
distinguishes a world that had to be from a world that did not. The account here goes with the
Focusing because it is what the rest of the structure forces, and that is a reason, and it is not
evidence. It is the weakest joint in this book, it is load-bearing, and it is where a reader who
wants to break the thing should push.

What survives the audit is three predicates out of five, and the three are the hard ones. The
scholastics got to a ground with no face, by argument, from inside an institution that needed a
face, and they did not blink. This account gets to the same three denials by a different road and
finds them already occupied. **That is not agreement in the sense the roll-call wanted, and it is
better than agreement, because it came with two places where the lists disagree and a statement of
which one this book abandons.**

They were not almost saying this. They were saying it, about what it is, and something else about
what it does, and the difference is where the next eight centuries of work is.
