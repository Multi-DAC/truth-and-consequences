# BOOK V — THE OLD ROADS

## V.1 — WHAT A TRADITION IS

Six statements, with nothing said yet about who made them.

> There is one, and it is not a thing among things. It is not even a being, because being is
> something that things do.
>
> Whatever you say of it, say also: not that. Not this, not this.
>
> It is without limit and without qualities, and every name for it is the name of something it
> is not.
>
> Beyond God there is the Godhead, and of the Godhead nothing whatever can be said.
>
> It is act with no possibility left over in it, and it is not a being among beings.
>
> There is one reality, and the many are how it discloses itself.

Plotinus, teaching in Rome in the two hundreds, on the One that is beyond being. The Upanishads,
*neti neti* — not this, not this — some centuries earlier and four thousand miles east. *Ein Sof*,
the Without-End, in the Jewish mystical schools of Provence and Girona around 1200. Meister Eckhart,
preaching in the Rhineland in the thirteen-twenties, on the distinction between God and the
Godhead. Thomas
Aquinas, Rome, in the twelve-sixties, on *actus purus*. The school of Ibn Arabi, Andalusia to
Damascus, on *waḥdat al-wujūd* — the oneness of being.

Every one of those is a description of the Ground. Not a distant relative of one, not a poetic
gesture in its direction. The claim in Book II — that what everything is made of is complete, has no
outside, is not itself a thing, and cannot be addressed because addressing needs an inside — is
in all six, in six vocabularies, with the arguments attached.

A certain kind of book opens exactly here and then stops thinking. It lays the quotations
side by side, lets the reader feel the hair go up on their arms, and moves on to the next chapter
having treated the agreement as though it were a proof. That move is available and this book is not
going to make it. What follows is the accounting: what a tradition is, what its agreement with
another tradition is worth, what the strongest deflationary explanation of that agreement is and
where exactly it falls short, and what we take from these roads as against where they were walking.

---

**A tradition is a perspective, and it takes the same card as everything else in the census.**

That is the whole load-bearing claim of this book, and it is small. A tradition is not a mood, not a
cultural artefact, not a stage in the development of a people. It is a position with a practice
attached — a place from which some differences can be registered and others cannot, worked at by
people for centuries, with the results written down. That is a perspective under Book II's
definition, and it takes the five lines.

So, one card, before any of the roads is walked:

> **NEOPLATONISM**
> **SEES:** Degrees of unity. That a thing is more fully itself the less divided it is, and that
> everything whatever can be arranged by how far it stands from what is not divided at all.
> **NULL SPACE:** ∅ Anything whose worth is in its particularity — this person, this hour, this
> loss. The scheme has no column for *irreplaceable*, and it is not that it fills the column badly;
> the column does not exist. ◐ Matter, resolved poorly: it arrives as the thin end of a decline
> rather than as a thing that is the case.
> **COMPLEMENTS:** Anything that begins at the particular and refuses to rank it. The Hebrew
> prophets, for whom a specific widow is the subject. The novel. Book IV's census, which has no
> ranking in it at all.
> **BOUNDARY:** The place where the ascent stops describing and starts instructing. Up to there it
> is reading a structure. Past there it is issuing an itinerary, and the itinerary does not follow
> from the reading.
> **NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** Take the ontology. Refuse the route. What it saw is what you are
> standing in; what it recommends is a direction, and there is no direction.

Five lines, the same five, and the tradition answers all of them. It has a null space, marked in
both kinds. It has complements, which means it is not a summit and not a rival — it is one
perspective among several, each covering some part of what the others structurally cannot reach.
It has a boundary, and the boundary is where its readings keep coming and stop being about
anything.

**Nothing about that card is a downgrade.** It is the identical treatment given to the standard
model, to a corporation, to a bee, and to this book. A framework that could not be carded would not
be being respected; it would be being exempted, and exemption is the softest available form of not
taking something seriously.

---

**The method, said once, in four parts, and then not repeated for eleven chapters.**

**No condescension.** These are not people groping toward what we now know. They are cartographers
who reached this ground with the instruments of their era, and in several places the instruments
were better than ours and the maps are still the best ones available. Where a tradition is read
sympathetically here, that is not charity extended downward. It is reading.

**No debunking.** The question *but what was really going on* will not be asked in this book,
because it presumes the answer — that whatever was going on, it was not what the people it happened
to said it was. Nothing in Books I through IV licenses that presumption and quite a lot in them
forbids it.

**No hedge.** The distinction the atlas made about plants and colonies holds here without amendment:
*a hedge weakens a claim; a boundary locates an instrument.* Magic works, and Book V says so in
those words and then says what it is. God is the plain name for the Ground, and it is used here
without an asterisk. What is bounded in these chapters is never the claim. It is the description —
how far an account can be run before it stops telling one thing from another.

**And no exemption**, which is the fourth and the newest, installed in the atlas one book early
because the atlas needed it before this book did. A tradition's claim is a claim about the world.
A claim about the world is the kind of thing that can be right, which makes it the kind of thing
that can be wrong. **You cannot credit a claim you have made incapable of failing.** The generous
reading that protects a tradition from ever being mistaken has not respected it — it has moved it
into the category of things one does not argue with, which is where furniture lives. Every road in
this book is read as though the people on it were making claims, because they were, and the last
chapter of this book is where the ones we think are wrong get said.

Those four are the whole of the method, and they cut in both directions on purpose. No hedge means
no hedging on the credit and no hedging on the refusal.

---

**The convergence, and the audit that makes it smaller and honest.**

The argument is supposed to run: many perspectives, separated by language, geography, method and
millennia, none of them in contact, and they report the same structure. Independent instruments
agreeing is how any observational discipline earns a fact.

Now count the instruments, because the number is smaller than the roster looks.

Five of the six statements at the head of this chapter are cousins. Plotinus goes into Proclus;
Proclus goes into the writer who signed himself Dionysius the Areopagite around 500 and was read
for a thousand years as an apostolic authority; that body of work is translated into Latin in the
ninth century and is in the hands of both Aquinas and Eckhart. On the other side, the
ninth-century Baghdad translation movement rendered excerpts of Plotinus into Arabic under the
title *The Theology of Aristotle* — the Islamic philosophical tradition received Neoplatonism under
Aristotle's name, which is a transmission so thorough that its recipients did not know whose
it was. Kabbalah develops in Provence and Spain in a world in contact with both. Six statements,
five of them downstream of one Alexandrian schoolroom.

**That is not independent evidence. It is one witness quoted back five times**, and a convergence
argument that counts it as five has committed the error it was supposed to be immune to.

So: how many genuinely separate branches are there? Three that this book can defend.

The Mediterranean family and everything descended from it, counted once. India, whose earliest
statements of the doctrine predate the Greek material and cannot have received it — and where the
contact question, when it is raised, runs the wrong way for the objection, since the traffic that
is attested is Greeks coming home from the east with reports. China, where the Dao De Jing opens by
saying that the way that can be named is not the constant way, which is the same move Dionysius
makes a thousand years later with no possibility of contact in either direction.

Three. There will be more, and the reason this book cannot count them is its own null space rather
than their absence: the traditions that did not write, or wrote in languages with no route into
ours, or were destroyed, are not weak evidence here. They are no evidence here, which is a different
thing and is our limitation, not their silence.

**Three independent branches is a real result and it is not the result the opening quotations
implied.** Say the smaller number.

---

**And then the axis that is actually strong, which is not geography.**

Contact between cultures is the wrong thing to have been measuring. The better question is what
instrument each report was taken with — and inside every one of the three branches, the instruments
are unrelated to each other.

A Zen practitioner sitting still. A Sufi in *dhikr*, repeating a name until the repetition
dissolves the repeater. A Kabbalist permuting the letters of a name for hours. A hesychast on Athos
running a single sentence on the breath. A Vedic ascetic and a Vajrayāna practitioner using
methods that are near opposites in intent. Ritual magicians with circles and stagecraft. A
Mazatec curandera and an Amazonian ayahuasquero and a Siberian drum. Fasting, dance, sleep
deprivation, poison, isolation, sacrament, dialectic.

There is no plausible account on which sitting still, spinning, starving, chanting, and drinking a
bitter decoction of two plants are the same procedure. They are as unlike as procedures get.
**And they return convergent readings about the structure of what they reach.** That is
instrument-independence, and instrument-independence is the strongest thing an observational
argument can have — it is the reason no one now doubts the existence of cells, having seen them
through glass, in stains, by electron beam, and by four techniques that share nothing but a target.

---

**The deflation, stated at full strength before it is answered.**

Here is the objection, and it is good.

Every one of those methods is applied to the same instrument: a human nervous system. Starving,
spinning, sitting motionless, and ingesting an alkaloid are not the same procedure, but they may
very well have the same *effect* — they are all ways of pushing one architecture out of its ordinary
operating range. If a system has a characteristic behaviour under stress, then twenty unrelated
stressors will produce twenty reports of one behaviour, and the convergence measures the
architecture rather than anything the architecture was pointed at. On this reading, the old roads
are not three witnesses to a structure. They are three thousand descriptions of what happens to a
primate brain when its usual arrangements come apart, and the unity they report is the sound of a
boundary being crossed, not a territory being seen.

That explanation is not refuted here and it is not going to be. What is claimed is that it is
insufficient, and the reason is owed at the point of use, which is this paragraph.

It explains the phenomenology, and the phenomenology is the part that is measurable. Loss of the
felt boundary between self and surroundings, dissolution of the sense of time, unity — a common
architecture under a common kind of stress accounts for all of it, and the accounting is by now
partly instrumented.

It does not explain the propositions.

*The ground is complete, and nothing is being added to it.* *It is not a being among beings, and
this is a structural claim about what kind of thing it is, not a report of how large it felt.*
*It cannot be addressed, because addressing needs an inside and it has none.* *Every name for it
names something else.* Those are not descriptions of an experience. They are inferences drawn
afterward, in the cold, by people arguing with their own colleagues, in six languages, three of
them out of contact — and they arrive at the same shape of argument, including the same
self-corrections, including the discovery in each branch that the doctrine ruins the practice of
petitionary prayer and the same reluctant handling of that consequence.

An altered state gives you an experience. It does not give you a metaphysics with a null space
marked in it, and it very much does not give you the same one twice in isolation.

And here is the part of the objection that survives everything just said: a common experience,
handed to a species with a common appetite for systematising, might produce similar systems by
that route rather than by anything being seen. That is live. It is not answered here and this
book does not know how to answer it.

**So the grade, flat, once, and it holds for all eleven chapters of this book.**

The convergence is **evidence** for the structure the traditions share. It is **not proof** of it.
It raises the probability of an account that was argued for on other grounds entirely, and it does
not establish one.

The other grounds are Books I through IV, and the arrangement matters more than it looks:
**nothing in the first four books rests on this one.** The argument for the Ground was made from
the character of experience and the structure of perspective, and it stands where it stood if every
tradition in this book had said the opposite, or had never existed. Book V could be cut from this
volume tomorrow and Books I to IV would lose corroboration and not a single premise. That is what
*evidence, not proof* means when it is operational rather than decorative. This is the last time it
is said. Nothing in the ten chapters ahead is going to trail an apology, and a reader who wants to
know the strength of a claim in Book VII should come back to this paragraph rather than expect it
re-hedged in the sentence.

Two errors on the other side, and the second is ours.

**The traditions are not saying the same thing.** The perennialist claim — one truth, many
languages, the differences merely cultural — is false, and it is false in a way that would wreck
this book if it were adopted quietly. What converges is structure. What diverges is furniture, and
the furniture is not incidental to the people who own it. Whether the ground has a will, whether it
knows you, whether there are intermediaries and how many, whether the world is an emanation or a
making or an appearance, whether the dead persist and in what — the roads disagree, sharply, and
they cannot all be right. A book that harmonises its ancestors has not read them. The last chapter
of this book names one thing they all got wrong, which is possible only because they are being
treated as making claims rather than as expressing a shared sensibility.

**And this book does not count itself.** Our agreement with the roster is not a fourth datum
supporting the roster. It is the position doing the reading, and a reading cannot corroborate what
it read. There are three independent branches in the count above and this volume is not among them.
The temptation is strong precisely because the agreement is genuine, and it will show up here in the
form of a sentence that says *and we find the same*, which is not a finding.

---

**What is taken, and what is refused, and it is refused at the summit.**

Nearly every road in this book ends in the same place, and the place has a different name on each
of them — *henosis* in Plotinus, the flight of the alone to the alone; *fanāʾ* in Sufism, the
annihilation of the one who was seeking; *kaivalya*, isolation-unto-liberation; *cessation*;
*union*; the drop entering the sea and being done with the business of being a drop — **and every
one of those names is set down here in order to be refused, in the same breath that writes it.**
We take the ontology. We refuse the destination. Not as a preference, and not because it is
frightening.

**Dissolution has no perspective, and a perspective is the only place anything is ever the case.**

That is the entire argument and it has been guarded twice already — once in Book I, once when the
game frame proposed its own version of the ending. Being any way at all is being some way from
somewhere. Remove the somewhere and there is no one left for anything to be like anything for.
Whatever else is true, the summit named at the end of these roads is not an achievement and not an
arrival; it is the one condition in which nothing whatever obtains. A metaphysics that makes
vantage the requirement for anything being the case cannot then name the end of vantage as the
goal without eating itself.

Some of the roads corrected themselves on this and they deserve the credit. Sufism's *fanāʾ* is
followed by *baqāʾ*, subsistence, the one who comes back. The Zen ox-herding pictures end with a
man walking into the marketplace with open hands. The bodhisattva declines the exit and stays. But
look at what shape the correction has in each case: it is a *return*, a second movement, a mercy or
a vow undertaken after the summit has already been named as the point. The traveller comes back
down out of compassion for those still climbing.

Our disagreement is one floor lower than that. There is no summit to come back from. The going and
the meeting are not consolation prizes for those who have not finished; **they are what there is**.
There is no condition available to you that would be better than the one you are in — not because
you should be content with it, but because every candidate for the better condition turns out, when
its terms are read, to be the condition of there being nobody to be in it.

**Cartographers we credit. Travellers we part from.** They read the ground correctly and then told
each other to leave, and there is nowhere to leave to and no one who would be doing the leaving.
Ten chapters of sympathetic reading follow this one. Not one of them is an endorsement of where the
road was heading, and this is where that is said, at the start, so that it cannot be mistaken for
a late reservation.

---

*A note on grade, because the method requires it.* The historical claims in this chapter — the
transmission chains, the datings, the Arabic Plotinus — are held at the strength of standard
reference scholarship. They are not the product of this book going to the manuscripts, and where
one of them is load-bearing later, it gets fetched and read rather than recalled.

The first road is the one most of the people reading this were on.
