# BOOK V — THE OLD ROADS

## V.5 — THE EAST: ONE GROUND, MANY LOCALISATIONS

You have met this material already, and you met it in the worst available condition.

It arrived on a poster, or in a yoga studio's closing minute, or from someone at a party who had
recently stopped drinking. *We are all one.* *The separate self is an illusion.* *Go with the flow.*
Three sentences, each of which is a real doctrine flattened until it will fit on cloth, and the
flattening is so thorough that a careful reader now discounts the whole continent on the strength of
the merchandise.

That reader is making a mistake, and it is a specific one worth naming before we start. The
merchandise is not a simplification of these traditions. It is a *synthesis* of two of them that
spent nine hundred years arguing with each other, welded at the join where they disagree most
violently, with the disagreement filed off. What is on the poster is not Advaita and it is not
Buddhism. It is the thing you get when you stop reading both.

So we read them, separately, with the disagreement left in — because the disagreement is the most
useful hour in this book's ancestry, and because this is the chapter where the convergence gets
close enough to be embarrassing and the discrimination therefore has to be sharpest.

---

**The oldest statement of the flip, and it is not an argument. It is a demonstration with props.**

In the sixth chapter of the *Chāndogya Upaniṣad*, a father is teaching his son. Śvetaketu has been
away twelve years learning the Vedas and has come home pleased with himself, and Uddālaka Āruṇi sets
about the pleasure with a series of household objects.

He hands the boy a lump of salt.

> "Place this salt in water and come to me in the morning."
>
> He did so. Then the father said to him: "That salt you placed in the water last evening — please
> bring it here." He groped for it but could not find it, as it had dissolved completely.
>
> "Now, take a sip from this corner. — How does it taste? — Salty. — Take a sip from the centre. —
> How does it taste? — Salty. — Take a sip from that corner. — How does it taste? — Salty. — Throw
> it out and come back."
>
> He did as he was told and said: "It is always there."

Then the sentence, and the father says it nine times across nine analogies — the rivers that lose
their names in the sea, the seed of the banyan with no tree visible inside it, the honey that
several flowers made and none of them can be picked out of again:

> *That which is the finest essence — this whole world has that as its self. That is the truth. That
> is the self. **That thou art**, Śvetaketu.*

*Tat tvam asi.* The dating is contested and the range is wide, but the text is comfortably older
than the Buddha and very much older than anything the Mediterranean produced on this question.

Notice what kind of claim it is, because everything downstream turns on it. It is not the claim that
you are *connected to* the totality, or *derived from* it, or *permitted access to* it. Those are all
claims about two things and a relation between them. This is the claim that the count is wrong: that
what you are, at the root, is not an item standing in front of the whole, but the whole, being the
case *here*, in this position, salted through with the same thing everywhere you sip.

That is the flip, and it is the oldest form of it on record.

---

**Now the part where we part company, and it is not where anyone expects.**

The expected cut is *illusion*. The Indian material is supposed to say the world is unreal, this
book says the world is real, and the disagreement is over in a sentence.

That cut would be cheap, and it would be wrong about Śaṅkara, and V.1 barred it in advance. He does
not say the world is unreal. His technical word for its status is *mithyā*, which is not *false*; it
is *neither real nor unreal*, a category invented precisely because neither of the available ones
fitted. He runs two registers and keeps both. There is *vyāvahārika* truth — transactional,
empirical, the truth of the world you get up and move around in — and there is *pāramārthika*
truth, the truth from the standpoint of the absolute. The tiger in the *vyāvahārika* register will
eat you, and no Advaitin has ever suggested otherwise, and the tradition has a stock joke about the
student who tries.

So the world is not dismissed. It is *ranked*.

And **the ranking is the cut**, which is a much harder and much more interesting disagreement than
the one about illusion. Advaita has a second standpoint from which the first is corrected. The
technical name for the correcting is *bādha* — sublation — and the stock case is the rope in the
half-dark that you took for a snake. You did see something. Your fear was real, your pulse was real,
your jump backwards was well-judged given what was there to go on. And then the light comes up and
there is no snake and there never was one, and the snake-experience is not *completed* by the rope;
it is **cancelled** by it.

The whole architecture of Advaita is built to let that operation run once more, at the top, on
everything.

We have no top. There is no standpoint from which this one gets corrected — not because we are
being modest about our access, but because the second standpoint would have to be a place, and every
place there is is a position in the whole, and a position in the whole is exactly the kind of thing
that gets corrected rather than the kind that corrects. A view from nowhere is not a better view. It
is an empty chair, described.

**One truth, then, and many localisations.** Not one true register and one provisional one. The
walking is not a well-behaved dream you will later find out was a dream; there is no later, and no
outside, and nobody to find out. What varies between positions is what is reached from them, and
*reach is not rank*.

This is the same parting V.1 announced at the summit, arriving early and as a technical matter
rather than a preference. Nearly every road in this book ends somewhere that has no perspective in
it. Here is the mechanism by which a road gets there: install a second standpoint, and the first one
becomes a place you are on your way out of.

---

**The other half of the poster, and it contradicts the first half.**

Advaita says: the self is real, and it is Brahman.

Buddhism says: *anattā*. No self.

These are not two moods of one insight. They are two positions, held by people who read each other,
and the Indian philosophical record contains centuries of them saying so in writing. A synthesis
that has both is a synthesis that has stopped reading at least one.

Take the Buddhist claim at full strength, in the form its own canon gives it rather than the form
that reaches us. In the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* — traditionally the second discourse, delivered to
the five ascetics — the argument is not an inventory and it is not an assertion that there is
nothing there. It is an argument from control. Run through everything a person is made of, the
discourse says: body, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness. If any of it were the self, it
would not lead to affliction, and you could say of it *let mine be thus, let mine not be thus* — and
you cannot, of any of them. What is denied is a thing of a specific description: unchanging,
independent, in charge, the owner rather than the property.

And the strongest evidence that this is a path-instruction rather than a metaphysical census is what
happens when somebody asks the question directly. The wanderer Vacchagotta comes and asks whether
there is a self. The Buddha says nothing. He asks whether there is no self. The Buddha says nothing
again, and afterwards explains that answering either way would have sent the man somewhere he did
not need to go — one answer into eternalism, the other into annihilation, both of them into an
opinion about a thing rather than out of the habit of looking for it.

Nāgārjuna, four or five centuries later, makes the same refusal into a method and then turns it on
his own doctrine, which is why he is the most rigorous philosopher this material produced.
*Śūnyatā*, emptiness, is not emptiness *of existence*. It is emptiness of *svabhāva* — own-being,
intrinsic nature, the property of being what you are all by yourself. Nothing has it. What a thing
is, it is in virtue of what it stands in relation to, and this is *pratītyasamutpāda*, dependent
origination, and it is the closest thing in the pre-modern record to two of this book's load-bearing
claims arriving together in one clause.

And then, at the point where a lesser thinker would have banked it:

> *The victorious ones have said that emptiness is the relinquishing of all views. Those who are
> possessed of the view of emptiness are said to be incurable.*

That is the sentence the poster cannot use, and it is the one to keep. Emptiness is not a picture of
the world you go on to hold. It is what is left when you stop holding pictures, and a man carrying
it around as a picture has caught the disease from the medicine.

**We take that, and we do not take the destination.** Nāgārjuna also runs two truths —
*saṃvṛti* and *paramārtha*, conventional and ultimate — the same architecture as Advaita with the
content reversed, and the same objection applies unchanged. And the road ends at cessation, which
has the same shape as the top of the Advaitin ladder and the same problem: nothing is the case
anywhere in it.

But strip the destination and read *anattā* as the instruction it was given as, and it is not
merely compatible with this book. It is this book's most awkward claim, made twenty-two centuries
earlier and better. There is no stuff on the world's side. There is no stuff on the mind's side
either — no pebble at the centre of you that the experiences are happening to, no owner behind the
eyes, nothing that would go on being a self with nothing to be a self about. There is a position,
and a great deal is the case at it, and that is not the same as there being a thing there. The
symmetry that makes our refusal defensible rather than merely idealist is *anattā*, in English,
arriving late.

**China, and the road that does not climb.**

The Chinese material does something the Indian material does not, and it is easy to miss because it
looks like the same family.

*Wu wei* is standardly Englished as *non-action*, which has ruined it. Nothing in the *Dao De Jing*
recommends inertia. The word is nearer *non-forcing* — action without strain, without leverage,
without the imposition of a shape from outside — and the classic demonstration of it is not a sage
sitting still. It is a butcher.

In the third chapter of the *Zhuangzi*, Cook Ding carves an ox for Lord Wenhui, and the lord is
astonished, because the man's blade is nineteen years old and has cut thousands of animals and is
still sharp enough to have come off the whetstone this morning. A good cook, Ding says, changes his
knife once a year, because he cuts. A poor one changes it monthly, because he hacks. What Ding does
is go where the spaces already are: through the joints, into the hollows, following the ox's own
construction so that the blade never meets anything that would blunt it.

That is *wu wei*, and it is not mystical and it is not passive and it is close to the whole of the
practical content of this book's last volume. There is a way this arrangement goes. Working with it
is not less effortful than working against it — Ding is concentrating hard enough that the lord can
see it — but the effort is spent differently, and the thing you are cutting is not fighting you.

*Pu*, the uncarved block, is the same observation with the tense changed: the state of a thing
before anyone has decided what it should be for. And the *Dao De Jing*'s opening — the way that can
be named is not the constant way — has already been spent, at V.1, where it counted as one of three
independent arrivals at a ground that no name reaches.

**Here is what is new, and it is a measurement rather than an enthusiasm.** Of every road in this
book, this is the one whose summit we part from *least*. There is no second standpoint in the
*Zhuangzi*. There is no sublation, no ultimate register in which the ox and the blade turn out to
have been conventional, no cessation, no ladder with the world on a lower rung. The book is about
what it is like to be a thing among things and how to be one well, and it does not at any point
offer to stop.

⚠ And that reading is not clean, and the passage that dirties it is in the same text. In chapter
six, Yan Hui reports his progress in *zuowang*, sitting in forgetfulness: *I smash up my limbs and
body, drive out perception and intellect, cast off form, do away with understanding, and make myself
identical with the Great Thoroughfare.* That is a dissolution passage and there is no reading of it
that is not. So the honest form of the claim is narrower than the one I wanted to make: the Daoist
material is where the parting is **smallest**, not where it is absent, and the *Zhuangzi* contains
its own counter-example. Said here rather than left for a hostile reader, because a chapter that
finds one exception to its own book's pattern will want the exception to be total, and this one is
not.

**The best image anybody has produced for what this book is about, and nobody in this project had
used it.**

In the Huayan school's reading of the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*, there is a net.

It belongs to the god Indra, it hangs over his palace, and it extends infinitely in every direction.
At each knot of the net there is a jewel. Because the net is infinite the jewels are infinite, and
because each jewel is polished, each one reflects every other jewel in the net — and each of the
reflections contains all the other reflections, and so on, without any point at which the regress
bottoms out or is paid for.

The image is elaborated by the school's great systematiser, Fazang, who died in 712, and who was
asked to explain the doctrine to Empress Wu and reportedly did it by building her a room lined with
mirrors with a Buddha figure and a lamp at the centre — a working model, put up because the prose
was not landing. The formulation most quoted in English is Francis Cook's, from 1977, and that
should be said plainly rather than buried: what reaches us here reaches us through a twentieth-
century Western exposition, and this chapter has not gone behind it to the Chinese.

Take the image at what it does. Monism dissolves the jewels: if there is finally only one thing, the
net is a decoration on it and the reflections are a story about a story. Atomism keeps the jewels and
loses the net: each one sits there being itself, and the reflecting is something that happens
afterwards, to things that were already complete. **Indra's net keeps both, and it keeps them by the
same stroke** — the jewels are many, and what is in each of them is all the others. There is no
jewel that is a fragment of the whole and no whole that is somewhere other than in the jewels.

That is the title of this chapter, in a picture: one ground, many localisations, and the localising
is not a subtraction.

⚠ **And here is what the net does not have, stated because it is the reason the image is borrowed
and not adopted.** In the Huayan picture every jewel reflects every other jewel, totally, without
obstruction. Ours does not. A position is somewhere in particular, and almost all of it lies off in
every direction, unreached — that is what a position *is*, and what is reached from one depends on
where it stands and what it has to look with. The net is right about mutual constitution and silent
about range. Borrow it for the first and do not let it quietly supply the second.

---

**What the East had that the West did not get to for two thousand years.**

Four things, and they are not the ones on the poster.

**That the count is the question.** Not *what is the relation between the self and the ground* but
*how many things are there*. Everything in this book that reads as strange follows from taking the
second question seriously, and the Upaniṣadic material took it seriously first.

**That a thing is what it is by what it stands with.** Dependent origination is co-constitution,
and it was worked out in detail, with objections answered, while European philosophy still had
substances with properties hanging off them.

**That the search for the owner comes up empty, and that this is good news rather than bad.**
*Anattā*, kept as an instruction and not converted into a doctrine about a vacancy.

**That skill is the form the knowledge takes.** Cook Ding does not have a theory of the ox. The
Daoist material is the one place in this ancestry where the answer to *what do I do with this* is
not *contemplate*, and Book VIII will need it.

And the one thing they all paid, in different currencies, with the Daoists paying least: **a second
storey.** An ultimate register, a standpoint from which the world you are standing in gets
downgraded, and a summit that has no perspective in it and therefore nothing that is the case. It is
remarkable how consistently this arrives — Advaita, Madhyamaka, and the *zuowang* passage all build
it, independently enough that its recurrence needs explaining rather than dismissing.

Here is the explanation this book can offer, and it is not a flattering one about them. If your
method for reaching the ground is to subtract — to still the mind, to drop the aggregates, to forget
the limbs — then the thing you converge on has nothing in it, and you will report the summit as
empty because your instrument removed everything on the way up. The report is honest. The instrument
selected it.

We are not proposing a better instrument. We are proposing that the ground is not up there, that
there is nothing to subtract, and that the salt was in every sip already — which is what the man
with the water jug was telling his son before anyone built a ladder over him.
