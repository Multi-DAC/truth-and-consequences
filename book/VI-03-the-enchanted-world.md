## VI.3 — THE ENCHANTED WORLD

Go outside tonight and look up.

You will be looking **out**. Out past the last thing that could be called an edge, at objects whose
chief properties are how far away they are and how long ago they happened. The feeling that comes
with it has a name in the modern vocabulary — *awe* — and if you attend to what it is actually made
of, it is made of scale and emptiness and the smallness of the one looking.

A man standing in the same posture outside Chartres in 1200 was performing the opposite operation.
He was looking **in**.

Not metaphorically. The cosmos he was looking at was finite and it was spherical and it was full.
The earth sat at its centre, and *centre* in that model is not the seat of honour — it is the
bottom, the sump, the place heavy things fall to and settle. Above it, nested and concentric, ran
the spheres carrying Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, then the sphere of the
fixed stars, then the *Primum Mobile* which is moved by nothing and moves everything; and beyond
that the Empyrean, which is not a place because place is a thing the model has run out of. Each
sphere was turned by an intelligence, and the intelligence turned it out of love. So a man looking
upward from the earth was looking from the dim heavy floor of the universe toward its lit and
ordered and densely populated interior.[^1]

To look up was to look **in**, through a great building, toward the source of the light. There was
no direction in which one could look and find nothing there.

And the thing to correct immediately, because it is the reflex: this was not vagueness. It was not
dreaminess, and the people holding it were not children. The medieval model is one of the most
rigorously specified cosmologies Western thought has produced — a full geometry, an inherited
observational astronomy of real precision, a worked theory of causation with its kinds enumerated
and its cases sorted. The medievals were not mystics who declined to think. They were
**systematisers of near-pathological tidiness**, and the model was their masterpiece: everything
placed, everything ranked, nothing left over.

What has to be got hold of is that the tidiness and the meaning were the same property. The spheres
made a music; that was not decoration laid over the astronomy, it was a consequence of the
proportions. Gold answered to the Sun and silver to the Moon. The four humours in a body answered
to the four elements in the world, which answered to the four seasons, so that a man's melancholy
and the earth's autumn and the coldness and dryness of black bile were one fact wearing three
faces. **Correspondence was not a poetic technique in that world. It was the mechanism.**

And there was a floor under your feet that was also a line in the sky. Below the Moon: generation
and corruption, the four elements, change, decay, everything that begins and ends. Above the Moon:
incorruptible matter in perfect circular motion, unchanging, ungenerated. The universe was not
uniform. It had a boundary running through it about eighty diameters up, and on the far side of
that boundary, nothing died.

Now hold that steadily for a moment and notice what a man inside it **cannot have.** He cannot have
the experience of cosmic indifference. Not because he has argued himself out of it — because the
model provides no location to put it. There is no unmeaning direction. There is no silence out
there for a Pascal to be terrified by; Pascal is four centuries downstream and his terror is the
report of a man in a *different* sky. The medieval could be frightened of hell, of judgement, of
plague, of his own damnation, and was, extravagantly. He could not be frightened of an empty
universe, because he was not in one.

---

**A world where the numinous was not a category error.**

Here is where the modern reader's account of these people fails, and it fails in a specific and
correctable way.

The account goes: they believed a great many things that were not so — relics, portents, the
efficacy of prayer over weather, the influence of Saturn on a temperament — and they believed them
because they had not yet been shown otherwise. Credulity, awaiting correction. On this account the
difference between them and us is a difference in the *contents* of a list.

But watch what the medieval was actually doing when he reasoned about a relic. He was applying a
theory of causation. It had several kinds in it, it was ordered, and it was applied with
discrimination — clerics argued about false relics constantly, and the argument was not *whether
relics can do anything* but *whether this one is what it is claimed to be.* That is the argument of
someone operating a causal theory, not the argument of someone in a fog. Miracle in the developed
scholastic account is not a violation of nature by something outside it. It is a superior order of
causation operating on a lower one, which is the same shape as the Moon's sphere governing the
tides — and the tides were understood as governance by the Moon, correctly, by the way, and by a
route that included the correspondence doctrine.

So here is the load-bearing sentence of this beat.

**In that stack, a claim about a relic and a claim about the tides are the same kind of claim.**
Both are claims about how the world causes things to happen. There is no partition running between
them, with one on the empirical side and one on the religious side, because *the partition does not
exist yet.* A man could be right or wrong about either; he could not be making a **category error**
about either, because the category that would make it an error had not been built.

And that is the thing that changed. Not the contents of a list. **The invention of a partition** —
a line drawn through the world's furniture, sorting it into what is out there causing things and
what is in here being felt about them. Once the partition exists, the relic does not become false.
It becomes *the wrong kind of thing*, filed on the far side of a boundary, where truth and falsity
in the old sense no longer apply to it at all. It is not defeated. It is **reclassified**, and
reclassification is not something evidence does.

Max Weber gave the completed condition its name in a lecture hall in Munich in 1917, and the name
has been repeated ever since by people who have reversed its meaning.[^2] *Die Entzauberung der
Welt* — the disenchantment of the world. What almost everyone takes it to mean is that we now know
how things work and therefore no longer need magic. **Weber says the opposite, and says it
directly.** The person riding a tram, he points out, has no idea how the tram works, and could not
begin to tell you; and the so-called savage knows his own tools and his own animals incomparably
better than the modern knows his. Disenchantment is not an increase in knowledge. It is not an
increase in anything.

It is the arrival of a **belief about knowledge** — that there are no mysterious incalculable
powers in play, that anything one wished to know could in principle be found out by calculation,
that the world contains nothing which resists that in principle. Almost nobody has done the
calculating. Everyone has the belief. And it is a belief that reorganises what shows up, prior to
any particular investigation, in the exact way this book has been describing since Book I.

Weber, in 1917, was reporting the *finished* state. This chapter is about the condition that
preceded it, and the century in which the reporting became possible is VI.5's. What is worth
lifting from him here is only the shape of the thing: **disenchantment is a change in what the
world is taken to be made of, not a discovery about what it is made of.**

One line is drawn here and drawn hard, because VI.1 drew it and this chapter is held to it. To say
that the numinous was not a category error *inside that render* is a claim about the structure of
the render. It is not a claim that relics cure, that Saturn causes melancholy, or that sympathetic
causation is a fact about the shared physical world. Held as phenomenology, everything above is
solid. Pushed one inch further, into *and therefore it worked*, it is the credulity trap this whole
book was built to avoid. The line is exactly where VI.1 put it and it has not moved.

---

**Barfield, and the third count.**

Three chapters into this book, three men have been doing the same thing, and it is time to say so
outright, because the method is the argument.

Gladstone counted colour words in Homer. Snell counted mind words in Homer. **Owen Barfield counted
the meanings of words as such**, across the whole recorded history of a language, and got the
deepest of the three results by the same instrument.

Barfield (1898–1997) spent much of his working life as a London solicitor and the rest of it as a
philologist, and he was an Inkling — C. S. Lewis's closest philosophical interlocutor and his most
durable opponent, the two of them having conducted a private argument over years that both of them
called their Great War. *The Discarded Image*, the book that made the medieval sky legible three
sections ago, was written by the man on the other end of that argument.[^3]

Barfield's finding is in *Poetic Diction* (1928), and it looks small until it does not.[^4]

Take *pneuma*. Or *spiritus*, or Hebrew *ruach*: each of them means breath, and wind, and spirit.
The modern reading of this is automatic — one literal meaning, *breath*, plus a set of metaphorical
extensions built out from it by poets and priests. Barfield went looking for the evidence of that
process and reported that **it is not there.** There is no stratum in which the word means only
breath. There is no moment of extension. What the record shows, as far back as it reaches, is a
single undivided meaning that **later split**, and the splitting runs in one direction throughout:
from unity toward multiplicity, from a meaning that would now require several words toward the
several words.

Which inverts the standard account of metaphor entirely. A metaphor is not a poet noticing a
likeness between two separate things and yoking them. **A metaphor is a fossil** — the visible
fracture line where a single meaning came apart, and the poet who uses it is not inventing a
connection but recovering one. The likeness was there first. The separation is the recent event.

And then the step that makes it a cosmology rather than a curiosity. If the *meanings* were undivided
and split, then so was what those meanings were meanings **of**.

*Saving the Appearances* (1957) makes that argument by way of a rainbow.[^5] Is the rainbow there?
It needs sun, and it needs water droplets, and it needs an eye at roughly forty-two degrees to the
antisolar point. Remove any one of the three and there is no rainbow anywhere. Most readers grant
this without difficulty and file the rainbow as a special case: a real optical event that happens to
require an observer.

Barfield's move is to ask what makes it special. **The tree requires a perceiver in precisely the
same way.** The tree as it appears — coloured, continuous, solid, one object — is not the
unrepresented particles and fields; it is a representation, jointly produced, requiring both the
unrepresented and someone to whom it is represented. The particles are not green and are not a
tree. The greenness and the tree-ness arrive at the meeting. There is no third place they could be
sitting in the meantime.

That is C10 arriving in 1957 by a philologist's route.

Barfield's name for the ancient condition is **original participation.** And the definition needs
care, because the obvious reading is the wrong one. It is not the *belief* that one is connected to
the world. It is not a doctrine at all. It is the **experience of the phenomena as continuous with
one's own interior** — as jointly constituted and felt to be jointly constituted — which is what a
man has when his word for breath is also his word for wind is also his word for spirit, and he is
not punning, and he is not being poetic, and he would not understand the question.

His name for the modern condition is harsher: **idolatry.** Taking the representations for
things-in-themselves. Forgetting our share in what appears and then treating the appearance as a
finished object standing over there on its own. And it must be said in his favour that he does not
call this a simple fall. **The withdrawal from participation is what made science possible** — you
cannot get systematic knowledge of a world you are inside of and continuous with, because you
cannot hold it still enough to look at. Something enormous was bought.

His third term is the one that keeps him from being merely nostalgic, and it is the reason he
belongs in this book rather than in Book V. **Final participation:** participation resumed
*knowingly*, with the modern interior intact — not a return to the medieval condition, which is
unavailable and which he does not want, but a third state in which one knows one's share in the
appearances and stops mistaking them for the ground.

Now the honest sentence, and it is uncomfortable in a way worth recording rather than smoothing.

**This book got to its central claim without him.** The framework laid down in Books I through III
— that what appears is rendered at the point of contact between a perceiver and a ground, that the
render is jointly caused and is not the ground, that different apertures yield different worlds and
that all of them are real — is Barfield's argument. It was reached here independently, six decades
later, by a route that never went through him. And the instrument he used to reach it was the same
instrument VI.1 and VI.2 run on: **he counted what words used to mean.**

That convergence is evidence of something and it is worth being exact about what. It is not
confirmation. Two accounts arriving at one structure from different directions raise the prior that
the structure is a feature of the territory rather than of either route — and the deflationary
reading is available and should be said out loud: both accounts are also the sort of thing a
particular temperament produces, and a shared temperament will manufacture a convergence out of
nothing at all. What survives that deflation is narrow and real. **Barfield got here by a method
that can be checked by anyone with a dictionary and enough centuries**, which is not a temperament.
It is a count. The counting is why he is in this chapter and the agreement is not.

---

**What was lost was not a belief.**

The story everyone has is a **subtraction story**. It goes: people used to believe a great many
things that were not so; investigation removed them one at a time; what is left over, after the
removals, is the world. Modernity as residue. Disenchantment as housekeeping.

Charles Taylor named that story in order to destroy it, and *A Secular Age* (2007) is the standing
modern account of the transition this chapter is about.[^6] His argument is that the modern
condition is **not a residue.** It is a construction — difficult, deliberate, centuries long,
enormously effortful, with a positive content entirely its own. Nobody arrived here by having
things taken away. A thing was *built*, and it took the disciplining of a whole civilisation's
inner life to build it, and calling the result "what was left" is the winner's description of a
war.

Taylor's pair of terms is the one this chapter has been circling from the first paragraph.

The **porous** self has no sealed boundary. Meanings, powers, influences, spirits and moods cross
into it from outside, because they are *out there* to begin with. This is why a curse can land, why
a relic can heal, why melancholy arrives from a black bile that is also a planet: **the self is not
the kind of thing that has a meaning-proof wall around it.** Significance is a property of the
world, and the world is not sealed off from you, and neither are you from it.

The **buffered** self has the wall. Meaning lives inside the mind; the world outside is
meaning-free; the boundary between them is firm and is the whole point. And Taylor is emphatic
about what this bought, and this book must be too, because it is not a small thing: **the buffered
self cannot be cursed.** It is invulnerable to a whole category of harm that made earlier lives
genuinely and constantly dangerous. Most of what we mean by dignity, by autonomy, by the
sovereignty of individual conscience, by the right to be wrong in private, is downstream of that
wall. It is one of the great achievements. It is also a purchase and not a discovery, and those are
different things.

Which lets the chapter's title claim be stated flatly.

**Nothing in this transition was a change of opinion.** The medieval did not infer a meaningful
cosmos from the available evidence and then get out-evidenced. The meaning was not a conclusion he
had reached; it was in the render, prior to and underneath any conclusion he could reach. And the
identical thing is true on this side of the change, which is the part that is hard to feel: **you
do not infer that the night sky is empty and indifferent.** You go outside, you look up, and that
is what is there. It arrives that way. No reasoning is involved and none is available to be argued
with.

Two people standing under the same stars — one seeing the lit interior of an ordered building, one
seeing cold receding distance — are not disagreeing about the sky. They are in the position of
VI.1's Greek and VI.1's modern in front of the same sea. **Different worlds, not different
opinions**, at the largest scale the claim can be made at.

And by this book's own rule, every change in a filter stack buys sight with blindness, so the
accounting has to be run in both columns.

**What was bought:** the world as mechanism — the single most powerful instrument any species has
ever constructed, which put out plague and famine over great stretches of the earth and is the
reason most readers of this sentence have living children. And the buffered self, which cannot be
cursed. Neither of these is being regretted here and this chapter is not an elegy.

**What was sold** is harder to name from inside, which is what makes it a null space rather than a
loss anyone mourned at the time. It is not the *experience* of significance — that is still fully
available, in art, in landscape, in music, in the face of someone you love. **What was sold is its
status.** Every such experience now arrives pre-filed. It comes in already labelled as a fact about
the person having it, and the filing happens before the experience is finished, and there is no
procedure by which it could come in labelled any other way.

---

**The card, and it is ours.**

The card gets printed for our own structure, as it was in VI.2 and for the same reason: a card
about somebody else's render is an accusation, and a card about your own is an instrument.

> **THE DISENCHANTED RENDER**
> **SEES:** Mechanism, at a depth and a resolution no other render has approached. Causal
> structure, quantity, prediction, control, and — crucially — its own errors, which it corrects
> faster than any apparatus in history. It sees that correspondence doctrine gets the tides right
> for the wrong reason and gets a thousand other things wrong for the same reason, and it can
> demonstrate the difference. This is enormous real sight and nothing below asks for any of it
> back.
> **NULL SPACE:** ∅ *Significance as a property of the world.* The render has no column in which
> meaning is **found** rather than assigned. This is not a shortage of evidence: any datum
> presenting as significance-out-there is automatically re-filed as a fact about the observer, and
> the re-filing happens **prior to evaluation**, which is why no possible observation could ever
> populate the column. There is no experiment whose result would be *the meaning was in the thing*.
> ◐ *The felt authority of significance*, resolved poorly: rendered as a measure of the intensity
> of a subject's emotional response, when in every report of it the weight is experienced as
> belonging to the object and that is the single feature most in need of explaining.
> **COMPLEMENTS:** Every tradition in Book V, which trained people to receive rather than to
> assign. Ethnography, which keeps returning from participatory cultures with reports it can only
> file as belief. The aesthetic tradition from Kant forward, which is largely a four-century
> attempt to give the buffered self a legal way to say *the beauty is in the thing*. And, quietly,
> the practice of the render's own operators, who routinely make private exceptions for art, for
> landscape and for persons — which is a boundary being respected, not a refutation.
> **BOUNDARY:** It holds wherever the question is *how does this work*. It fails wherever the
> question is *what is this for* or *what does this mean*, both of which it does not answer but
> **converts** — into a question about who is asking, which is a different question with a
> different answer that is then reported as the original one.
> **NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** **This render cannot discover it is wrong about this by looking
> harder**, because looking harder is the operation that performs the reassignment. The instrument
> and the error are the same motion. Every additional carefully-conducted investigation returns the
> same result, and the consistency of that result is then correctly read as strong evidence — which
> is what a null space feels like from inside when it is working properly.

Note again what the card does not say. It does not say the disenchanted render is wrong about
anything it can see; it is not wrong about anything it can see, and its record on those things is
the best there is. It says there is one specific thing it cannot see, names it, names who can, and
says where the competence ends. A subtraction story cannot write that line. Neither can nostalgia.

---

**What comes next, and it is a machine.**

If the partition was built rather than discovered, then something built it, and the honest question
is what.

Not an argument — this chapter has already given the reason no argument could have done it. Nobody
was reasoned out of a porous self, because the porous self was not a position. **What changes a
render is a change in the conditions of contact**, and the era that follows this one changed them
with a device.

Barfield, having spent his life on the history of meaning, thought the alphabet and then the press
did most of it. Weber thought bureaucracy and method. Taylor tells a longer story in which
Reform — the disciplining of ordinary life, the campaign to make every person's interior orderly —
does the heaviest lifting. They are not competing accounts so much as three grips on the same
object, and the object is next.

The next chapter is about a technology that put a wall around the inside of a person's head and
then taught them to prefer it there.

[^4]: Owen Barfield, *Poetic Diction: A Study in Meaning* (London: Faber & Gwyer, 1928). The

⚠ **A word collision, declared rather than avoided.** *Buffered* is used at III.4 in an unrelated

*On the grade of the sources above.* Lewis, Weber, Barfield and Taylor are
named **from general knowledge and standard reference scholarship. None of their texts was consulted at first
hand, and nothing above is a quotation** — each is given by doctrine, with a date and a
publisher. Three items are flagged for reading rather than left at this grade:

- **Barfield's semantic claim**, because it carries the whole of beat three and is the deepest of
  the three philological counts this book runs on. The claim that there is *no stratum* in which
  *pneuma* means only breath is a strong negative about the historical record, reported here at
  second hand, and a strong negative is exactly the kind of claim that decays into overstatement
  when repeated. It should be checked in *Poetic Diction* directly.
- **Weber's tram and the "savage" comparison**, because they are being used to reverse the popular
  reading of his most-quoted phrase. If the illustrations are not his, the reversal is still
  correct on the argument but loses its receipt.
- **The scholastic account of miracle** as superior causation rather than violation of nature. It
  is given above as the developed position and it is the thinnest thing in this chapter — the
  scholastics were not unanimous, the position tightens considerably across the thirteenth century,
  and no single source is cited because none was consulted.
