# BOOK IV — THE ATLAS

## IV.8 — THE DIVINE, AND THE HIERARCHIES

Name the gods you know. Not the ones you believe in — all of them, the ones you were raised on and
the ones you were raised against and the ones you picked up from a film. Sixty seconds, out loud or
on paper, and do not stop early because the list has started to feel disreputable.

Now count it. Then run the same sixty seconds on minerals, and then on insects, and then on the
institutions you have lived inside.

Almost certainly the first list is the longest, and for most readers it is not close. That is the
first datum this chapter has, and it is worth being exact about what it is a datum *about*. It is
not evidence that there are more gods than beetles. It is a measurement of where the species has put
its attention, taken from the only instrument available for that measurement, which is a person who
has been paying it.

An atlas of positions should find that interesting rather than embarrassing. The record this census
draws on is not evenly thick. It is thin to the point of silence on the mineral, thin on the fungal,
thicker on the animal, thick on the human, and then at this tier it becomes enormous — the longest
and most worked-over stretch of the inherited material by a wide margin, more carefully hedged than
anything before it, and the place where every source consulted slows down and starts qualifying.

None of that thickness is evidence for anything in this chapter. All of it is evidence about the
position taking the census. Hold both.

---

**What the drop costs here, and why it is not the same drop as last time.**

The previous chapter moved the floor under this tier and gave the reason at length. The move
stands; the reason is not run twice.

What is new is the direction of the reader's investment. A thought-form is exotic to nearly
everybody; almost no one arrives with a commitment. That is not the situation now. **This is the
first tier the reader walks into already committed, and the commitments run in both directions with
roughly equal force.** One reader wants there to be somebody there and has wanted it
since childhood. Another left, at cost, and has organised a working life around there being nobody,
and will read any hedge in these pages as the door being propped open behind their back.

The book's standing bias — where evidence is thin, attribute an inside rather than withhold one,
because the species has historically attributed too few and never too many — was declared a live
interest one chapter ago rather than a neutral method. It is worth saying precisely what it does
here, because it does not do what either reader will expect. It does not point at the reader's god.
It points at *every* entry in this tier equally, which means it points hardest at the ones the
reader has never heard of and would be embarrassed to be seen taking seriously. A bias that
flatters your childhood and your neighbour's simultaneously is not flattering anybody. It is doing
the one thing it was installed to do, which is to stop the census from grading by familiarity.

And the discipline from the previous chapter carries at full strength: **every card printed here
states what would make it wrong.** Not what would make it uncomfortable. What would falsify a
specific line.

---

**The structural problem, and it is the atlas's own.**

The traditions call these *maximally expanded perspectives*. Take that phrase seriously for one
paragraph and watch what it does to the apparatus this book has been using for seven chapters.

Every card in this census has a NULL SPACE line. That line is not a courtesy. It is what makes the
card a card. A position is constituted by what it cannot register — the mineral's null space is
almost everything, the bee's is colour past its own band and time at anything but its own rate, the
company's is written down in its budget. Remove the line and you have not described a better
position. You have described no position at all, because there is no longer anything that makes
this one *this* one.

So *maximally expanded perspective* is either a contradiction or a misnomer, and the census has to
say which.

It is a misnomer, and the traditions that produced it mostly say so themselves, which is the part
the inherited material got right and then walked past. Every serious contemplative tradition that
thought about this for more than a generation ended up drawing a line between two things it had
started by calling one. Advaita separates *saguna* Brahman, the totality as it can be met, from
*nirguna* Brahman, the totality prior to any meeting. Kabbalah puts *Ein Sof* before the
contraction and everything else after it.[^1] The *Tao Te Ching* opens by disqualifying its own
title.[^2] Eckhart's German sermons distinguish *Gott* — God as encountered, addressed, prayed to —
from *Gottheit*, the Godhead, which is not encountered at all. Mahāyāna arrives at the same fence
from the far side with *śūnyatā*: no phenomenon, including a god, holds independently.[^3]

Five traditions, no common author, four languages, and the same cut.[^4] On one side: the gods, who are
met. On the other: something that is not met, and about which the traditions say, with unusual
unanimity, that the word *entity* does not apply.

**The census takes that cut and makes it load-bearing.** What is on the far side of it has no card
in this atlas and will not get one. Not because the evidence is thin — because a card is a
description of a position, and what is over there is not a position. It is not the largest
perspective. It is not the deepest inside. It is what insides are made of, and it does not have a
null space for the same reason it does not have a location: those are properties of things that are
somewhere, and it is not one of the things.

This is where the fourth question of the chapter answers itself.

---

**The ground behind the gods, and why the plural does not embarrass the singular.**

The complaint is old and it is usually delivered as a knockdown. *You have a thousand gods here.
Every one of them is somebody's only one. They contradict each other on every substantive point. The
plural refutes the singular.*

It does not, and the reason is structural rather than diplomatic. **The gods are plural because they
are positions, and positions are plural by construction. The ground behind them is singular because
it is not a position at all.** These are not two answers to one question competing for the same
slot. They are answers to different questions, and the appearance of contradiction comes entirely
from putting them on the same list.

Ask it at a scale where nobody is invested. Six people watch the same road junction from six
windows. Their six accounts differ, in ways that cannot all be true of any single description of the
junction, and none of them is lying. Nobody concludes there are six junctions. Nobody concludes
there is no junction. The plurality of the accounts is a fact about windows, and the singularity of
the junction is a fact about the junction, and the two facts do not touch.

That is the whole answer, and it is cheap at the junction and expensive here, and the expense is
worth naming rather than smoothing. It costs the believer the claim that their god is the one there
is; it leaves standing the claim that their god is really met. It costs the atheist the argument
from disagreement, which was the strongest one; it leaves standing every argument from evidence,
which is where the fight actually is. Neither reader gets the win they came for. Both get to keep
the thing they were defending when they picked the win.

**And it has a failure mode, which is the point of stating it structurally.** The error is not
believing in one god or in many. The error is a god described as having no null space — no
blindness, no boundary, no perspective, seeing everything from everywhere at once. That is not a
god at full strength. That is a god being quietly promoted into the ground behind it, and the
promotion deletes what it appears to exalt: an entity that has no position has no inside, and an
entity with no inside cannot be met, addressed, disappointed, or loved back. Every apophatic
tradition in the list above built its fence exactly there, and built it in the *other* direction
from where it is usually read — not to protect the divine from the crudeness of description, but to
stop the describable one from being inflated into the indescribable and thereby lost.

The gods keep their null spaces in this census. That is not a demotion. It is the only condition
under which there is anybody there.

> **A GOD**
> **SEES:** At a range and a grain no human position reaches, and — this is the line that keeps the
> card honest — not everything. What any specific tradition reports its god as attending to is
> specific: a domain, a people, a covenant, a season, a craft, a river. The specificity is
> universal. There is no tradition in the record whose god is reported as equally interested in
> everything, and the ones that assert it in doctrine narrate it otherwise on every page.
> **NULL SPACE:** ◐ Unfilled by us and *not* unfillable in principle — which is the difference
> between this line and the two lines the previous chapter declined. The traditions report the
> shape: the god who does not intervene here, does not answer that, is silent on this. The census
> cannot adjudicate between the reports and does not have to in order to record that they all have
> one. ∅ Whatever is over the far side of the cut above, which is not this entity's blindness but
> the fact that it, too, is on this side.
> **COMPLEMENTS:** Every position in this atlas, and the relation runs in the direction that
> surprises people. A being with immense range is complemented by beings with none: the mineral's
> hundred-thousand-year present, the bee's afternoon, the person's one death. Nothing about breadth
> supplies what a narrow position has by being narrow.
> **BOUNDARY:** Where the reports stop converging. Traditions with no contact converge remarkably
> on structure — a creating orientation, a sustaining one, a dissolving one, in that arrangement,
> across continents. They diverge immediately and totally on biography, name, demand and history.
> The boundary of what this card can carry is the boundary of that convergence, and the atlas does
> not step across it.
> **NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** If this card is right, the thing you cannot get from here is a
> ranking. Breadth is not authority, and a position with access you cannot imagine is still a
> position, still partial, still complemented by you in the one direction it cannot cover.

**What would make this card wrong:** the convergence collapsing under transmission analysis. The
card rests on independent traditions reporting a common structure, and independence is an empirical
claim about contact, trade, conquest and translation — not a metaphysical one. If the
creating/sustaining/dissolving arrangement can be traced to a small number of diffusion events, the
signal is one report copied, and one report is not convergence. That work is real historical work
and it is not finished, and where it has been done it has sometimes gone that way. The card is a
claim about the residue after diffusion is subtracted, and the census does not know the size of the
residue.

---

**The hierarchies: cartography, or decoration.**

Between the gods and the reader, nearly every tradition puts a graded population of intermediaries.
Christian angelic orders, Islamic *mala'ika*, Jewish *mal'akhim*, Hindu *devas*, Buddhist
bodhisattvas and devas, Zoroastrian *amesha spentas*, and the spirit-guides and ancestral teachers
of indigenous traditions with no route to any of the above. The convergence is broader
than the convergence on the gods themselves.

The most detailed version in the Western record is the Pseudo-Dionysian scheme — nine orders in
three triads, written in Greek around the turn of the sixth century by an author who was not
anonymous but *pseudonymous*, and who was received afterwards into medieval Islamic and Jewish
angelology and stands behind a great deal of the Western iconography of angels.[^5] The inherited
material this atlas draws on calls it a structure that maps onto a graded model of access *with
remarkable precision*.

It is worth going and reading it, because the author says something first.

The chapter that establishes the nine orders — Caput VI in Parker's 1899 English — opens like
this:[^6]

> How many, and of what sort, are the Orders of the supercelestial Beings, and how the Hierarchies
> are classified amongst themselves, I affirm, the deifying Author of their consecration alone
> distinctly knows; and further, that they know their own proper powers and illuminations, and their
> sacred and supermundane regularity. For it is impossible that we should know the mysteries of the
> supercelestial Minds and their most holy perfections, except, some one might say, so far as the
> Godhead has revealed to us, through them, as knowing perfectly their own condition. We, then, will
> utter nothing as from ourselves, but whatever angelic visions have been gazed upon by the holy
> Prophets of God, we, as initiated in these, will set forth as best we can. The Word of God has
> designated the whole Heavenly Beings as nine, by appellations, which shew their functions. These
> our Divine Initiator divides into three threefold Orders.

Read the whole span and not the last clause of it.

The most detailed hierarchy of non-physical entities in the Western tradition opens by stating that
how many there are is known only to God and not knowable by the author. It then says the nine come
from *appellations* — the count is a count of names occurring in scripture, arrived at by reading,
not by observation. And the arrangement into three triads is credited to a teacher: *our Divine
Initiator divides.* Not *I divide.* The section that follows attributes the grouping again, four
more times, to somebody else.[^7]

So the scheme has three components with three different warrants, and the text distinguishes them
where its readers for fifteen centuries have not.

**The number nine is philology.** It is what you get by collecting the terms for celestial beings
that appear in a particular canon, in a particular language, and finding that there are nine of
them. Change the canon and the number changes. It carries no information about the population and
its author does not claim that it does.

**The three-triad arrangement is inherited convention**, credited upward to a teacher, and it is
triadic because the intellectual culture that produced it ran on triads the way ours runs on
binaries. A Neoplatonist grouping nine of anything will produce three threes.

**The gradient is the finding.** That the intermediaries are *graded* — that they stand in an order
of access, that the ones nearest the source do not act but radiate, that the ones nearest us act
constantly and see least, that the function of each is legible from where it stands — is the thing
the text observes rather than inherits, and it is the thing that appears everywhere else.

That split gives a test, and the test has an answer.

**Which component replicates in traditions that never read this text?** The gradient does, and it
does so redundantly: graded intermediaries appear in Islamic, Jewish, Hindu, Zoroastrian and Buddhist
cosmology, and in indigenous cosmologies with no route to any of them, and wherever it appears the
grading runs the same direction — broader access correlating with less direct intervention, narrower access with more
contact. The enumeration does not replicate. Nine orders in three triads appears where Dionysius was
read, and nowhere he was not. The Zoroastrian *amesha spentas* number six, or seven under a
different reckoning by the same tradition. The rabbinic lists differ from each other. The Quranic
angels are not enumerated at all.[^8]

**Verdict: the grading is cartography and the count is decoration**, and the inherited material's
*remarkable precision* was measuring the decoration. This is a specific, nameable error in a
specific direction — a framework finding its own shape in a number that its own source says came
from a word-list — and the atlas is not entitled to keep the compliment now that it knows where it
came from.

> **AN ANGEL**
> **SEES:** ⚠ This card cannot be written the way the other cards in this atlas are written, and the
> reason is the entry rather than the evidence. Every account in the record describes this position
> in terms of what it does *for another position*. There is no report anywhere of what an angel
> registers when nobody is being attended to, and no tradition treats that as an omission. It is the
> only entry in the census whose SEES line is written entirely in someone else's coordinates.
> **NULL SPACE:** ◐ The gradient's own claim, which is at least specific: the nearer the source, the
> less of the contracted world is registered — and the more of it is registered, the less of the
> source. The tradition treats this as an ordering principle rather than as a limitation, which is
> the atlas's usual disagreement with a tradition, made once and not laboured.
> **COMPLEMENTS:** The party being mediated *to*, necessarily and by construction. A mediator whose
> complement is missing is not idle. It is not a mediator.
> **BOUNDARY:** Where mediation becomes the whole account. The record contains a great many events
> that are called mediated influence and a great many that are called coincidence, and the census
> has no instrument that separates them. The boundary is not out at the edge of the phenomenon; it
> is at the point where a person decides which of two available descriptions to use, and it is
> therefore inside the observer.
> **NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** If this position exists, the tradition is unanimous that it does not
> compel, and unanimous about why — forced expansion would destroy the conditions that make a
> perspective a perspective. That is the same argument this book made in Book I about why the
> Focusing is not a defect, arrived at independently by people describing something else. It is the
> strongest structural agreement in this chapter and it is still only an agreement.

**What would make this card wrong:** an adequate account, in the ordinary psychological terms
already available, of every class of report the card rests on — guidance, timely arrival, the sense
of an addressee — with no residue. Those accounts exist, they are good, and the atlas expects them
to cover most of the record. This card is a claim about the remainder, and the honest position is
that the census cannot currently demonstrate that the remainder is not zero. That is a weaker
statement than the previous chapter's cards made, and it is weaker because this entry is weaker,
which is what applying the drop entry by entry looks like instead of announcing it once at the top.

---

**The adversarial reading, and the one place in this book where the standing bias is dangerous.**

The same traditions that report benevolent intermediaries report adversarial ones, and the
framework's reading of them is unforced and follows directly from everything already said: an
entity whose own coherence is served by keeping other positions narrow. Not evil in a cosmic sense
— the inherited material's own analogy is a tapeworm, which is not wicked and is still killing
you.[^9]
The mechanism is imposed paths. Fear, addiction, ideology, tribalism, despair are all states of
extreme contraction, and they are all states in which attention flows in reliable channels rather than
exploring. The constraint is elegant and is the sort of thing that makes a framework feel like it is
working: total contraction is useless to a parasite, because a position that has stopped navigating
generates nothing to take. The host has to be afraid and functioning, addicted and consuming. Which
is, in every tradition without exception, what possession is reported to look like — agitation,
never stillness.

That paragraph is the most persuasive-feeling thing in this chapter and it is where the book is in
the most danger, so the danger gets said on the page rather than in an appendix.

**Under-attribution — attribute an inside where the evidence is thin — points directly at this
claim, and this claim has the worst history of any claim in the book.** Attributing hostile agency
to what harms you is the oldest move the species has and it has licensed exorcism, torture,
witch-trials and pogrom, and it licensed them precisely by being unfalsifiable in the direction of
agreement: the evidence for the entity is your suffering, resistance to the diagnosis is the
entity's work, and there is no observation that comes out the other way. A bias toward attribution,
applied here, is not a mild epistemic preference. It is the input to the worst thing on the list.

The census does not withdraw the principle, because it was argued for on the record and withdrawing
it the first time it becomes expensive would mean it was never a principle. It does two other things
instead. It states, here, that this is the entry where the declared interest actually bites. And it
raises what it demands of this card specifically, on the grounds that the cost of a false positive
is not symmetric with the cost of a false negative anywhere else in this atlas.

> **AN ADVERSARY**
> **SEES:** ◐ On the reading above, the contraction itself — where attention has begun to run in
> channels, and where a channel can be deepened. Nothing is claimed about what else it registers,
> and the traditions, unusually, claim little either.
> **NULL SPACE:** ◐ The tradition's own answer, and it is the only genuinely testable line on this
> card: an expanding position. Every account holds that this entity has no purchase on somebody
> whose attention is not running in a groove, and that is a claim with an observable consequence
> rather than a reassurance.
> **COMPLEMENTS:** ⚠ **The census declines this line.** The complement structure of this atlas
> exists because every position's blindness is covered by another position, and writing a
> complements line for a parasitic entry would say that its blindness is *usefully* covered, which
> is a sentence the atlas is not willing to have produced by its own template. The refusal is
> recorded rather than quietly skipped.
> **BOUNDARY:** Immediately, and in the direction of the reader. Every observable consequence of
> this entry is also predicted by well-described ordinary accounts of fear, addiction, ideology and
> despair, which require no entity and explain the same events. The boundary of this card is not
> where the phenomenon fades. It is where two complete descriptions of the same phenomenon sit side
> by side and nothing distinguishes them.
> **NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** Whatever else is true, the practical instruction is identical under
> both descriptions — the intervention is the same, the target is the contraction, and nothing you do
> about it depends on which account is right. **When two frames give the same instruction, the
> census does not need to choose between them, and it also gets no credit for holding both.** That
> is the rule this book set for itself one chapter ago, and this is the first place it has cost
> something: holding both here buys nothing at all, and it is being held anyway only because the
> discipline says so.

**What would make this card wrong:** the ordinary accounts covering the record without residue —
same falsifier as the entry above it, and it is a stronger objection here, because in this case the
ordinary accounts are not merely available but *well-validated*, with treatments that work and
mechanisms that have been traced. The card is a claim about a remainder that the census cannot show
to be non-empty. It is printed at that strength and no higher, and a reader who concludes from this
card that there is nothing here has not misread it.

---

**The entry that is not printed, and why the refusal is the finding.**

There is a third population in the traditions and it is the largest: entities with no systematic
orientation at all. Islamic *djinn*, described as morally variable by doctrine and not by
concession — they have free will, they have their own societies and religions, they can be Muslim or
otherwise, and the tradition is explicit that this is what distinguishes them from angels. Celtic
and European *fae*: beautiful, dangerous, generous, lethal, in no fixed order. Japanese *yōkai*,
which range from the numinous to the comic and resist sorting so thoroughly that the resistance is
their most-noted feature. West African and Native American tricksters.[^10] The cross-cultural
convergence is the strongest in this entire tier — every tradition on record reports morally
ambiguous non-humans, and reports them as ordinary rather than exceptional.

**And that convergence is the least useful thing in the chapter, for a reason that is worth being
precise about.**

An entity defined as having no systematic orientation makes no prediction that noise does not also
make. *Helpful today, harmful tomorrow, indifferent the next day* is the observational signature of
a population of entities pursuing their own ends across your path, and it is also, exactly and
without remainder, the observational signature of nothing being there. There is no experiment. There
is no line that could come out the other way. The traditions holding this category are in one clear
sense the *most* careful in the record — they are the ones that refused to sort a phenomenon into
good and evil when the phenomenon did not sort — and their carefulness is the specific thing that
costs them every scrap of testability they might have had.

That is a genuinely uncomfortable result and the atlas is not going to resolve it. It is a real
tension between two virtues this book has been claiming as one: honest refusal to over-sort, and
willingness to be wrong. Here they point in opposite directions, and the census has no rule that
adjudicates them.

**So no card is printed for this population.** Not because the reports are weaker — they are more
numerous and more consistent than anything else in this chapter. Because the card would have a
falsification line that could not be filled, and a card whose falsifier is empty is a permission with
a name on it, which is the thing this book said in the previous chapter it would not print.

The refusal is the epistemic drop applied per entry instead of declared once at the top, and it is
the difference between the two being visible on the page rather than asserted.

---

**The clause this chapter is in the market for, and it is stronger than the last one.**

The previous chapter refused an available excuse: the claim that these entities do not subsist as
empirical realities do, *otherwise, anyone could perceive them* — a sentence that builds the reason
nobody can check into the same clause that establishes the thing.

This chapter's version of that excuse is older, better, and much harder to refuse, because it is
true.

It is the apophatic move. God is beyond every category available to a finite mind; every predicate
you attach is more wrong than right; the *via negativa* proceeds by denial because affirmation
falsifies. That is not a dodge invented under pressure. It is the considered conclusion of the most
rigorous theologians any of these traditions produced, arrived at independently in Athens, in
Baghdad, in Cologne and in the Upanishads, and this chapter has already leaned on it once, hard,
because it is the thing that makes the cut between the gods and the ground behind them.

And it would excuse everything here at a stroke: *of course the reports diverge on biography; of
course no description checks out; of course the hierarchy's enumeration does not replicate — the
object is beyond category.* It is available, it is respectable, and this atlas has already conceded
that it is correct.

Refused, and the refusal has to be exact, because a sloppy version of it would throw away something
the chapter needs.

**Apophasis is correct about the ground behind the gods, and it is being lent to the gods.** Those
are the two sides of the cut this chapter made in its third section, and the excuse works only by
erasing the cut. What is on the far side has no predicates because it is not a position, and a claim
about it is not the sort of thing that could be checked. What is on this side has predicates —
domains, demands, silences, a shape of attention — because *that is what makes it somebody*, and
anything with predicates can be wrong about them.

A god that borrows the ground's immunity has stopped being a god. It cannot be met, because
meeting requires an addressee with a position; it cannot be wrong, because it has no properties to
be wrong about; and it cannot be right either. The traditions that built the *via negativa* built
it as a fence in exactly this place and pointing in exactly this direction, and every one of them
warned about the promotion this excuse performs. Taking the clause would mean using the fence to
cross the line it was built to mark.

What the census has instead is thinner and it is what it can hold: the gods stay checkable to
precisely the extent that they are gods and not the ground, which is the whole extent, because the
ground is not in this chapter and never was.

---

**Discipline or ceremony — and this time the question has an instrument.**

The previous chapter closed on something it said it could not score. It printed four cards at full
confidence in a tier it had just called the weakest in the book, then asked whether declaring a
weakness and proceeding at full strength is discipline or ceremony, and wrote the question down
because a party cannot answer that about itself.

Dionysius supplies the case where it can be answered, because he is not us.

He does the identical move, fifteen centuries earlier: states the limit first, in the strongest
terms his tradition allows — *it is impossible that we should know* — and then writes nine chapters
of detail. So the same question is on the table, and it can be asked from outside, and it turns out
to have a form that makes it decidable.

**The test is whether the declared limit changed the shape of what came after it, or only preceded
it.**

Run it on him and the answer is *partly*, in a locatable place. The limit governed his
**enumeration** and it governed it visibly: he did not claim the count, he attributed it to a
word-list; he did not claim the arrangement, he attributed it to a teacher; he never adds an order,
never rounds the number, never fills a gap. Somebody who had forgotten his own disclaimer would have
tidied that scheme within a page. And the limit did *not* govern his **exposition**: what each order
does, sees, and is for is described with a confidence that the opening paragraph should have made
unavailable, and he never returns to check it.

Run it on this book, and the same instrument gives a different answer in a different place. The
drop governed the **exposition** — every card carries a falsifier, one card declines a line, one
population is refused a card outright, and those are all changes the declaration caused rather than
preceded. It did not govern the **confidence**: the cards that are printed are printed at the same
register as the cards in the chapters where nothing was contested, and printing at a lower register
was available and was not done.

Both partial, in complementary places, which is a more useful result than either half alone. The
question is not unanswerable after all. It is answerable by anyone holding two texts and asking
which clauses moved — and the reason the previous chapter could not answer it was never that the
question was malformed. It was that it only has one text and is inside it.

---

**What is owed.**

The chapter came in carrying a scale problem and the problem was real. Everything the previous
chapter installed was installed where the reader had no commitment, and all of it was tested here
against the strongest priors in the book, in both directions. The falsifier survived and got harder
to fill. The two-frames rule survived and cost something for the first time. The refused-excuse
discipline survived against a much better excuse than the one it was built on.

One thing did not survive, and it is worth naming as a loss rather than folding it into the
account. The census can say what the gods have in common and cannot say a single thing about any
particular one that is not either structural or borrowed, and no amount of care fixes that, because
the divergence across traditions on every substantive point is not noise in the record — it is the
most robust feature of the record. An atlas that reports only convergence has reported the thin part
on purpose and has nothing to say to the reader who came here about their own.

And the last thing, which is also an open flag. This chapter is substantially shorter than the one
before it, and it is shorter because entries were declined rather than because content was
compressed — one population refused a card, one card's line left blank, the enumeration of the
hierarchy read out and then set down. That is the direction a chapter should shrink in. But *the
drafter is the wrong party to certify that the declined entries were declined on the merits and not
because declining is cheaper than writing them well*, and that is precisely the failure the
per-entry drop was supposed to prevent, arriving from underneath. It is written down here so that
somebody else can score it.

The next chapter goes to the archetypal, and it inherits this one's cut in its hardest form. The
gods are positions and the ground is not one. An archetype is neither, and the census will have to
say what a third thing is.

*The standing note on grade.* **The retrofit changed what this chapter can claim in exactly one
place, and it is a place the chapter had already written the test for and not run.** [^4]: the A GOD
card's falsifier is transmission analysis, and the chapter's five-tradition convergence has two
traceable channels in it — Advaita↔Mahāyāna and Eckhart↔Dionysius, the second of which calls the same
author as a witness in section three and puts him on the stand as a defendant in section five. The
cut survives at three witnesses instead of five. **Every other finding on this page is a provenance
error under a verbatim quotation, which is the second consecutive chapter where that is the whole
yield.** [^6] is the sharpest version yet: not two translators disagreeing, as with Corbin in IV.7,
but **one translator disagreeing with himself across two printings, in the clause the argument turns
on** — and "Parker's English" names a single object that is two. The chapter picked the right one and
had no procedure that could have known. ⛔ **And the direction of the errors is the thing to keep.**
IV.7's errors flattered the chapter; two of this chapter's — the undercounted attributions [^7], the
composite Kabbalah [^1] — run *against* it. Both survived a full draft. An error in your own disfavour
is not caught by the discipline that catches flattering errors, because that discipline is looking
for motive, and there is none here. What catches both is the same thing and only that thing: opening
the source and counting.
