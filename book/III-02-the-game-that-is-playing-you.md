# BOOK III — THE GAME

## III.2 — THE GAME THAT IS PLAYING YOU

Take the author away and the hand does not disappear. It moves.

A reader who has just been shown that nothing made this does not put the missing party down. He
relocates it. The one who was standing outside goes underneath; the one who was prior becomes the one
who is everywhere; the one who intended becomes the one who is *enjoying himself.* Three properties
were refused and three arrive to replace them, and the replacements are better in every way that
matters to a reader: inside instead of outside, present instead of past, delighted instead of
competent.

That picture is the closest thing to this book's position that is not this book's position. It is
also the one most people arriving here already hold, and it is held warmly, which makes it harder to
take away than the demiurge was. Nobody loves the demiurge.

So here is the flat form of the disagreement, and then the long form.

**Nobody is holding a controller.** Not from above, not from beneath, not from inside a mask.
The player is not a party to the game. **The player is one of the states the whole game contains** —
one of the arrangements that were going to be in it whether or not anything ever arrived to notice,
in the same way a chess position contains the possibility of a fork before anyone has seen it.

---

**And now the version worth taking seriously, which is not mine and is very old.**

The Vedāntic answer to *why is any of this going on* is one word, and it is the best single word
anyone has produced for it. **Līlā.** Play.

It is not a mood or a poetic flourish; it is a formal answer to a formal problem, and the problem is
ours. If the highest thing lacks nothing, it cannot be doing anything *for* a reason, because a
reason is a lack with a direction. Then either nothing happens, or what happens is not for anything.
The *Brahma Sūtra* takes the second horn in four words — *lokavat tu līlākaivalyam*, II.1.33 — which
Thibaut renders **"but (Brahman's creative activity) is mere sport, such as we see in ordinary
life,"** and Vireśvarānanda, working from the same line, **"mere pastime, as is seen in the
world."**[^1]

Śaṅkara's commentary on it does the thing this book keeps asking of an argument, which is to supply
the case rather than assert the principle. He notes that *certain doings of princes or other men of
high position who have no unfulfilled desires left have no reference to any extraneous purpose; but
proceed from mere sportfulness* — their recreations, their amusements, the activity of someone with
nothing left to want. And then, in the same passage, a second analogy that is much better than the
first and that almost nobody quotes: *the process of inhalation and exhalation is going on without
reference to any extraneous purpose, merely following the law of its own nature.*[^2]

Hold onto the breath. We will come back for it.

Into English this arrived through one man, and the debt is real enough that it should be paid before
the disagreement starts. **Alan Watts** is the reason a book like this one can be written in this
language at all — not because he was the deepest reader of the tradition, which he was not, but
because he made it sayable without apparatus and without apology, which nobody else was doing. In
*The Book* he tells it as a story he would tell a child:

> God also likes to play hide-and-seek, but because there is nothing outside God, he has no one but
> himself to play with. But he gets over this difficulty by pretending that he is not himself.

He then does something scrupulous that his admirers rarely copy: he gives it back. *It isn't my story
at all,* he writes, *for any student of the history of religions will know that it comes from ancient
India.* And the thing he was carrying is genuinely the thing this book is carrying. You are not a
fragment posted here from somewhere better. You are not a spark in a jar. You are the whole of it,
having an inside — *the taboo of taboos*, in his phrase, *you're IT.*[^3]

None of that is in dispute. This book says it harder than he did.

---

**Now read the story again for its verbs.**

The world comes and goes, he says, because *if the world went on and on without rest for ever and
ever, it would get horribly tired of itself.* God hides, and *it takes him a long time to remember
where and how he hid himself.* This is *just what he wanted to do.* And *he doesn't want to find
himself too quickly, for that would spoil the game.*

Tired. Wanted. Remember. Too quickly.

Every one of those is an event, and one of them is fatal on its own. **Forgetting is not a state; it
is a transition.** To have forgotten is to have known and then not, which is two arrangements with an
order between them — and the last chapter has already spent what that costs. Before and after are
what an arrangement has. There is no arrangement that everything is in.

**So the Player fails exactly where the maker failed, and it is worth being precise about how much
less he needed in order to fail.** The demiurge required priority: he had to stand at a *before*
that has no coordinates. The Player requires nothing so grand. He never stood outside anything and
never made anything. He only has to have been *different once* — to have held the whole of it and
then, deliberately, to have put it down. That is a far weaker demand and it is still too strong,
because it still needs a moment on one side of it and a moment on the other, with the totality
occupying first one and then the other.

The demiurge and the divine Player have almost nothing in common. One is an incompetent functionary
who does not know what he is standing on; the other is everything there is, at play. **What they
share is a tense.** They are both attempts to explain the world by an *earlier condition of the
whole* — and the whole is the one thing that cannot have had one.

**And here the source turns out to be cleaner than the transmission**, which is why the breath was
worth keeping. Śaṅkara's second analogy contains no psychology whatsoever. Nothing in it is bored,
nothing wants, nothing waits, and nothing has forgotten. It says only that an activity can go on
*without reference to any extraneous purpose, merely following the law of its own nature* — and that
sentence, with the Lord lifted out of it, is very nearly this book's position, written in the ninth
century. *Kaivalyam* is the honest half of the compound: play **alone**, play with nothing behind it.

So the cut is not made against *līlā*. It is made against the genitive. **Play, yes. Nobody's play.**

---

**What the other reading costs is not something we have to press out of it. Watts writes the bill
himself, and he writes it at a deathbed.**

If there is one player wearing every face, then every face is a costume — and he says so, in the
place where saying so has consequences. The word *person*, he notes, comes from the mask of the
ancient stage. *In death we doff the persona, as actors take off their masks and costumes in the
green room behind the scenes.* One's friends should gather, as friends gather backstage, *to applaud
the show.* Earlier, the same claim without the kindness: the universe of seemingly separate things
is *real only for a while, not eternally real.*[^4]

That is the sentence to refuse, and not because it is bleak. It is trying to console, which is worse
— because what it consoles you about is the reality of the one who is gone. Under it, the person you
lost was an appearance the sole wearer set down; the grief is addressed to a role, and the role is
the part that was never real. *(What this book has to say about death is Book VII's, and it is not
said here.)*

The second cost is in the next four sentences of the same children's story, and it is the destination:
*when the game has gone on long enough, all of us will wake up, stop pretending, and remember that we
are all one single Self.*[^5] The many are a phase. The plot resolves by their ending.

**That is refused too, and not on taste.** A picture in which being the case at all requires a vantage
cannot then name the abolition of vantage as the point of the story. Whatever the ending is, it is not
the one where there stops being anyone for it to be an ending *for*. Book V is where that is opened;
here it is only guarded, because it springs early and by Book V it is unfixable.

---

**Now the objection, which is the honest one: you have removed the joy and left a mechanism.**

The reverse. **A game with one player has no stakes.**

Whatever is lost in it is lost to the party that gains it. Every defeat is the winner's other hand.
The suffering is borne by someone who is going to take it off, and it was undertaken for the interest
of the thing. Nothing in that arrangement is ever actually at risk — and an activity where nothing is
at risk is not a game. It is a display, staged by one party, for one party, who already knows how it
comes out and has arranged not to know on purpose.

Take the single player away and the stakes are real for the first time. What is lost is gone. What
the other one has is *theirs*, and not yours in a wig. The distance between you and the person across
the table is not a stage effect; it is the actual shape of the world, and it is the reason there is
anything to cross.

**The theology that takes God out of the game is the one in which the other players are real.**

That is an ontological claim here and nothing more. What it obliges — and it obliges a great deal —
is III.5's, and it is cashed in Book VII.

---

**Which leaves the sentence this chapter exists to state exactly, and then not one word further.**

**The whole game contains the possibility of a player who does not know that the game is whole — and
therefore contains one.** Not by permission. By completeness: what can be, in a totality with nothing
outside it, is.

Set that beside Watts and they look like the same sentence. They stay looking like it right up until
you ask *who forgot* — and the answer here is **nobody.**

**Our not-knowing is not subtractive.** His player had the whole thing and put it down; the limit is a
performance, and behind any performance is somebody who could stop. Ours is constitutive. A vantage
*is* a limit. To be a perspective is to be somewhere, and to be somewhere is for the rest of it to be
elsewhere — not concealed, not withheld, just not *here*, which is what *here* means. Not-knowing is
not something that happened to a perspective. **It is what a perspective is made of.**

So there is no earlier condition in which you knew and no party who arranged for you to stop. There
was nothing to put down and nobody to put it down. Your finitude is not a disguise the infinite is
wearing this afternoon; it is one of the shapes the whole thing has, it is not going anywhere, and it
is yours.

**Nobody is wearing you.**

---

A game nobody wrote, and nobody is playing from outside.

Then the old question arrives one last time, wearing its last costume: *where did all of it come
from?* If no one authored these states and no one is dealing them out, what put them there?

Nothing put them there. They are already there — all of them, at once, walked and unwalked alike —
and what that means mechanically is the next chapter.

Clarendon, 1890), p. 357, and Swāmī Vireśvarānanda, *Brahma-Sūtras* (Calcutta: Advaita Ashrama,
1936), on the same sūtra. **Both renderings were checked against scans rather than quoted from
memory** — Thibaut against the archive.org digitisation `vedntasutrastr01bdar` at the printed p.
357, exact including the parenthesis and both joints; Vireśvarānanda against two agreeing
reproductions. ⚠ **The grades differ and are not rounded up:** Thibaut is *scan-verified*,
Vireśvarānanda is *two-witness but not two-digitisation.* Note also that the sūtra is quoted here
in four words because it is four words; the parenthetical *(Brahman's creative activity)* is
Thibaut's supplement and is printed in his parentheses above, not silently absorbed.

[^2]: Śaṅkara's commentary on the same sūtra, Thibaut's translation, p. 357 — the two analogies
quoted above are consecutive sentences there. ⚠ **Two things about the second one.** First, the
punctuation: the printed text reads "…any extraneous purpose, but proceed from mere sportfulness,"
where the body prints a semicolon; the span crosses a page break in the scan and the joint is
therefore where an OCR running head sits. Second, and it is the substantive one, **Śaṅkara does not
leave the Lord out.** The sentence immediately following the breath analogy is "Analogously, the
activity of the Lord also may be supposed to be mere sport, proceeding from his own nature, without
reference to any purpose." The body says so — *with the Lord lifted out of it* — and the lifting is
this book's operation on his text, not a report of it. That is the whole cut of the chapter, and it
is stated as an amputation rather than as a reading.

[^3]: Alan Watts, *The Book: On the Taboo Against Knowing Who You Are* (New York: Pantheon, 1966),
ch. 1, "Inside Information," where the hide-and-seek story is told and where Watts gives it back:
"In fact, it isn't my story at all, for any student of the history of religions will know that it
comes from ancient India, and is the mythical way of explaining the Vedanta philosophy." *The taboo
of taboos: you're IT!* is the same chapter. All spans quoted from *The Book* in this chapter were
checked against the text rather than recalled.

[^4]: The deathbed passage is *The Book*, ch. 2, "The Game of Black-and-White" — the *persona* as
the megaphone-mouthed mask of the ancient stage, death as doffing it, and friends gathering "as
their friends come behind the stage," "to applaud the show." *Real only for a while, not eternally
real* is earlier, in ch. 1, in Watts's own statement of the Vedānta position. The chapter's charge
is that the second sentence licenses the first, and the two are given from their two places so a
reader can check whether the licensing is really there.

[^5]: *The Book*, ch. 1, continuing the children's story: "But when the game has gone on long
enough, all of us will wake up, stop pretending, and remember that we are all one single Self — the
God who is all that there is and who lives for ever and ever." Taken above at full strength and as
the story's destination, which is what it is — it is the last movement of the passage the chapter
has been quoting throughout, not a stray sentence recruited from elsewhere in *The Book*.
