# BOOK II — THE NAMING

## II.3 — THE PERSPECTIVE

This word arrives already ruined, and the ruin is the most expensive thing that can happen to this
book. Every other term here can be misheard by a reader who then notices they are confused. This one
is misheard by a reader who feels they have understood, agrees pleasantly, and puts the book down
holding the opposite of what it said.

So the definition comes first, and the correction comes before anything is built on it.

**A perspective is not an opinion about the world. It is a place where the world happens.**

Opinions are things a perspective *has*. They sit inside it, they are formed and dropped, and a
person can hold two incompatible ones before lunch. A perspective is not held. It is not adopted, it
cannot be revised over a weekend, and nothing about it is up for a vote. You can talk yourself out of
a view about taxation. You cannot talk yourself out of having eyes that stop at red on one end and
violet on the other, or out of arriving in a language that carved the world at these joints and not
others, or out of standing exactly where you are standing.

In the game: your perspective is not your review of the game. It is the instance the game is running
in.

---

The mishearing is old enough to have an owner, and old enough that almost nobody repeating it has
met him.

**Protagoras of Abdera, fifth century BC:** *of all things the measure is man — of the things that
are, that they are; of the things that are not, that they are not.* The sentence opened a book called
*Truth*, most of which is gone. It survives because Plato took it seriously enough to spend a
dialogue on it, which is the best evidence we have of how hard it was to answer.

He was first, by twenty-four centuries, at the part that matters most: the having is always
somebody's having. There is no report from nobody. Every account of what is the case comes from
something standing somewhere, and a philosophy that forgets this has smuggled in a knower with no
properties. That is our position, and he had it before there was a word for it.

The cut is the word **measure**. A measure judges. It implies a thing on one side, a standard on the
other, and a verdict passing between them — which makes each person the arbiter of what is the case,
and an arbiter is exactly somebody with an opinion who is entitled to it. That is the mishearing, in
its original and strongest form, and Plato's reply has kept for two and a half thousand years: if
every opinion is true, then the widely-held opinion that Protagoras is wrong is also true, and the
doctrine eats itself.

Ours does not have that shape, and it is worth saying why with some care, because the reader who has
met the self-refutation objection will be waiting to spring it. We are not claiming that every
opinion is true. We are claiming that opinions are not what perspectives are. What arrives at a place
is not a judgement and cannot be correct or incorrect — it is simply what is the case there. The
opinions you then form about what arrived are a separate matter entirely, and they are checkable,
and most of them are wrong. Nothing in this chapter protects a single one of them.

That distinction is load-bearing, and dropping it has a specific destination. A reader who hears
*perspective* as *opinion* lands, within about two steps, in the position that meaning is something
you make up and the making-up is all there is. That reader has usually tried it. It failed, quietly,
somewhere around three in the morning, because meaning you authored is meaning you can revoke. Book
VII is the reply and this is not it. The note here is only that we are not agreeing with them.

---

Now the structure the word actually has.

**Every perspective has a shape, and because it has a shape there are distinctions it cannot
register.** Not distinctions it has failed to notice. Not distinctions that better instruments or
more patience or a clearer head would deliver. Distinctions that are unavailable given what that
perspective is, and that no amount of refinement from inside will ever reach. The only thing that
reaches them is a differently shaped perspective — one that is blind somewhere else.

Stated flatly, and it holds without exception: **every perspective has a region of what is the case
that is structurally unavailable to it, and that region is never empty.** No grade buys an exemption.
No amount of intelligence, no discipline, no scale, no holiness, and no accumulation of instruments.
Being everywhere would not help, because being everywhere is not being anywhere, and a perspective
with no shape is not a perspective. It is the Ground, which has no vantage and never did.

That is the whole of it, and it is a theorem rather than an observation — not because it holds widely,
which is what a good observation does too, but because it follows from what a perspective *is* and
uses nothing else. Shape entails omission. A place that omitted nothing would not be a place.

---

The reader who is going to misread this book is now reaching for the conclusion that all of it is
therefore unadjudicable — different eyes, different worlds, nobody in a position to say. That is the
mishearing arriving through the back door, and the answer is a measurement made three hundred and
fifty years ago by a French priest who was not thinking about any of this.

**Edme Mariotte, 1668**, *Nouvelle découverte touchant la veüe*. He had been dissecting eyes, and he
noticed something the prevailing account of vision had no place for: the optic nerve does not enter
at the middle of the back of the eye. It enters higher, and toward the nose. From which it follows
that there is a patch of the retina with nothing on it to receive anything, and therefore a patch of
the visible world that never arrives at all.

He found it in the anatomy first. Then he built the demonstration to make it show itself: two small
white marks on a dark ground, one eye covered, the open one fixed on the first mark — and walk
backwards. At about nine feet the second mark goes out. Not dims, not blurs. Goes out.

Three things about that hole, and together they are the entire reason this position is not
relativism.

**It is determinate.** It has a size and a place — roughly six degrees across, and about fifteen
degrees out toward the temple. Note where that is: *not* where the anatomy is. The nerve enters on
the side toward the nose, and the eye turns its image over, so the gap in the retina comes out on the
opposite side of the world. It is not a vagueness or a matter of degree. It could be drawn on a
diagram, and Mariotte drew it.

**It is mappable from outside.** He knew where it was before he had ever caught it happening, because
he worked it out from the structure of the thing that was doing the seeing. Somebody else, standing
somewhere else, with a different instrument, established a fact about the shape of his vision that he
could not establish from inside it. This is available generally, and it is what the rest of this book
spends its time doing.

**And from inside, it does not present as absence.** You do not see a hole. There is no dark patch,
no ragged edge, no sense of something withheld. The world arrives seamless, and the seam is the point
at which you would have had to notice.

So: relativism says the differences between perspectives cannot be adjudicated — all reports equal,
nothing checkable, everyone politely wrong together. What Mariotte establishes is the opposite. A
perspective's blindness is a *fact about it*, with a determinate structure, discoverable by
somebody who is not inside it. The differences are real, they are not matters of taste, and they can
be worked out. *That's just your perspective* is a move for ending arguments, and nothing here
licenses it — because the shape of a perspective is precisely the kind of thing two perspectives can
sit down and establish together.

---

The nearest ally this chapter has held the position a century ago and gave it its name. This book
came to him late, and by a road that did not run through him.

**José Ortega y Gasset**, 1923, *El tema de nuestro tiempo*. Perspectivism, as a doctrine, with the
sentence this chapter is built on already in it: reality is such that it can only be had from a
particular place, and a perspective is therefore not a *deformation* of reality but a **component**
of it. He said that into the teeth of a century that thought objectivity meant subtracting the one
who was looking. He was not hedging, and he was not being modest on behalf of human knowledge — he
was making a claim about what reality is like.

He keeps one thing back, and it is a **sum**. For Ortega there is an absolute truth, and it is the integrated
articulation of all the perspectives; his God is that name, not a divine viewpoint standing apart
from the human ones but the harmonisation of every one of them. He was careful about it — the sum is
incomplete by definition, since the set of those who could contribute is open and always will be. It
is a more honest position than most of what followed it.

It still needs one thing that is not available: a place where the perspectives add up. They do not
add up, because a sum of perspectives is not a perspective. Put two places together and you do not
get a larger place, you get an abstraction that is nobody's and is had from nowhere, and there is no
nowhere. The Ground is not the total of the renders. It is what each of them is a render *of* — and
it is already complete, and it is not waiting for the count to come in.

That is the second time in two chapters that the same error has been taken off a good thinker, and it
is worth saying so out loud rather than letting it pass as coincidence. The previous chapter took the
*Umgebung* off a biologist: the environment as such, against which each creature's world could be
laid out as a slice. This one takes the sum off a philosopher. **Both are a room outside all the
rooms.** Nobody adopts that room on purpose. It arrives as bookkeeping — the totals have to go
somewhere — and it is load-bearing before anyone has decided to believe it.

The most careful modern version is also the most famous, and it gets nearer than either.

**Thomas Nagel**, 1986, *The View from Nowhere*. Objectivity as a direction of travel: step back from
your own standpoint, include it as one among many, and what you get is less yours and more the
world's. Nagel is not naive about this and the book's honesty is its subject — he holds that some
things are not reachable that way at all, having argued twelve years earlier that no amount of
stepping back gets you what it is like to be a bat, and he never claims the direction terminates.

The cut is one word: **direction**. Stepping back is not movement toward a view from nowhere. It is
movement to another place. Every act of standing back is performed by something with a shape —
a primate's eyes, a language full of nouns, an era's instruments and an era's questions — and what
you arrive at is not a thinner perspective but a different one, with a null space of its own. Physics
is not less perspectival than birdwatching. It is a differently shaped place with an extraordinary
reach and a determinate blindness, and its reach was bought with that blindness rather than in spite
of it. The gain is real and it is a **trade**, not an approach to a limit. Keep subtracting standpoint
and you do not converge on a view. You converge on no view, and there is nothing it is like to be
nowhere.

---

Which leaves the last thing, and it is the one that changes how the rest of the book reads.

The natural response to all of this is to treat a null space as damage — an unfortunate feature of
being finite, to be reduced as far as possible and regretted for the remainder. It is not damage. It
is the same fact as your existence, said from the other side.

A place is a place by having a shape. A perspective is a *this* rather than an *anything* precisely
because some things arrive at it and others cannot; the arriving and the not-arriving are one
specification, not two, and there is no version of the first without the second. Take the blindness
away and you have not improved a perspective. You have removed one. What is left — everything
registering at once, from nowhere in particular, with nothing outside it to be blind to — is the
Ground, complete, with no vantage anywhere in it. That is not an achievement to work toward. It is
what there was before there was anywhere to be.

So the null space is not the price of being someone. It is the shape of being someone. What you
cannot see is not a subtraction from who you are; it is part of the specification, exactly as much
yours as your sight, and the same fact about you.

Two things follow from that and neither is settled here. What to *do* about a blindness you cannot
see from inside is a practice, and the practices are Book VIII's. What it costs when perspectives
with different null spaces have to share a room — when the thing one of them cannot register is
another one — is Book VII's, and it is the harder of the two.
