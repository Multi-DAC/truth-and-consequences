## VIII.7 — DO BE DO BE DO

Every instruction in this book so far has been a thing to *do*. Navigate rather than tour. Read your
own filter stack. Edit. Hold it open. Notice the second arrow and decline to fire it. Recognise the
other players. Seven chapters of verbs.

This one is not another verb, and it is not a summary of the others. It is the shape they make when
they are running in a life rather than listed on a page — and the shape is a rhythm. **Doing alternates
with being.** Not as a discipline you impose on the doing, and not as a reward at the end of it. As the
actual structure of a consciousness that is getting anywhere at all.

The book's source compressed this into three syllables and attributed the compression, with a straight
face, to Frank Sinatra by way of Kurt Vonnegut by way of Alfred North Whitehead: *do be do be do*.[^1]
The joke is load-bearing, so it is worth being clear about which part is the joke. That the phrase is a
scat lyric is the joke. That the lyric is the correct formula is not.

---

### I. The oscillation, stated plainly

Consciousness alternates between two modes, and the alternation is not a personality trait, a cultural
preference, or a stage you graduate from.

**Doing is the creation of boundaries.** It is the making of a distinction — this and not that, here
and not there, now rather than later. Every act of attention that goes *toward* something is a doing.
So is every decision, every cut, every sentence. Doing generates the structure that makes movement
possible, because you cannot move within a space that has no features. This is the mode in which
something happens.

**Being is the recognition of wholeness.** It is what is left when the distinction-making stops: not
absence, not blankness, but the perceptual state in which the boundaries you have been maintaining
relax and what shows up is the field rather than the figures in it. This is the mode in which nothing
happens and something is nevertheless the case.

The gloss is the best short one available and it is worth having exactly: *being is the
inhale, doing is the exhale, and consciousness is the breathing itself.*[^2]

Take the metaphor seriously enough to notice what it rules out. **You cannot inhale as a lifestyle.**
A breath held at the top is not a deeper breath; it is a stopped one, and it kills you at almost exactly
the rate that not breathing at all does. The same is true at the bottom. Neither phase is a destination,
neither phase is the good one, and the pathologies at both ends are real and are not symmetrical in how
they present — the person who cannot stop doing looks productive right up until they are not, and the
person who cannot start looks serene.

And the thing the breath metaphor gets right that nothing else does: **you do not have to run it.** You
have to stop overriding it. Nobody achieves respiration. What people achieve, and what costs them, is
the constant partial suppression of a rhythm that was going to happen anyway.

---

### II. The pair this book cannot use

Here is where the argument nearly went wrong, and it went wrong in a way worth performing rather than
quietly avoiding, because the error is available to any reader who has been paying attention and would
be *invisible* to one who has not.

The natural way to write the oscillation, in the vocabulary this book has built, is: *radiance and
contraction*. It sounds right. It has the correct shape — an opening and a closing, an outward and an
inward, one word for the phase that reaches and one for the phase that gathers. It is also how the
source glosses the oscillation at small scale, where it speaks of attention *expanding and
contracting*.[^3]

**It is wrong here, and it is the one weld this book has already stood up an entire section to refuse.**

Book VII was explicit. *Radiant* and *contractive* are not descriptions of what a consciousness is
currently doing; they are the two directions of the ethical polarity, the axis along which the adversary
is defined. And VII.4 stopped mid-argument to build a fence between that vocabulary and *the focusing*,
on the ground that the resemblance between them is entirely in the silhouette. The fence was put in
these words, and they are
worth having back verbatim rather than in summary, because the summary is where the fence goes soft:

> **The focusing is metaphysical — it is what makes you a somebody. Contraction is ethical — it is a
> stance a somebody can take. Every perspective is focused. Not every perspective is contractive.**

That is VII.4's, and the whole of the present difficulty is that the oscillation's inward phase is a
*phase* and contraction is a *posture* — the fortress, the individuation held against the whole rather
than expressed as part of it — and the two are not merely different, they are on different axes
entirely.

Import that word into this book's vocabulary and the fence falls over from the other side. Write
*radiance and contraction* as the two phases of the fundamental rhythm and the last chapter of this book
instructs the reader to **oscillate between the good direction and the adversary's.** As a life
practice. Alternating.

That sentence is not a rhetorical flourish; it is what the words mean here, and they mean it four
chapters after they were defined.

The defect has a name and a history in this account: one word, two referents. It is worth noting that
it did not arrive here through carelessness. **It arrived by quotation.** The word is innocent
where it was carried in from — a vocabulary with no ethical polarity for it to collide with, which used
*contracted* in at least three unrelated senses across its own closing pages. Borrowing a term is borrowing whatever
else it is attached to at the other end, and the other end is not visible from here.

So the pair is struck. What replaces it is not a synonym.

---

### III. Two axes, not one

The reason no synonym will do is that **the oscillation and the polarity are not the same axis, and no
pair of words can be both.**

The oscillation is *rhythmic*. Being and doing, inhale and exhale, generating structure and letting it
go. It has no moral content whatsoever. It is a description of how a bounded consciousness operates,
and it would be true of a being that had never once faced an ethical choice.

The polarity is *ethical*. Radiant and contractive, individuation expressed through the whole or held
against it. It has no rhythmic content whatsoever. It is not a phase; it is an orientation, and you can
hold it steady for a decade.

Cross them and you get four things, all of which exist, none of which the single-axis version can even
state:

**Radiant doing.** The obvious good case, and the one every account of this gets right. Work that opens
the space it operates in — building something people can use, saying the true thing that makes the
conversation larger, the surgeon's whole attention down on one thing at 3am. Boundary-making in the
service of the whole.

**Contractive doing.** Also obvious once named, and it is most of what goes wrong in institutions.
Enormous activity, boundaries generated at speed, every one of them drawn to fortify a position. The
person who works eighty-hour weeks to be unassailable is doing, continuously, and every hour of it is
the fortress going up another course. Book VI's institutional half is a catalogue of this and did not
need this chapter's vocabulary to describe it.

**Radiant being.** The one the culture has almost no language for and this book owes a defence of, below.
Rest that is genuinely rest — the field allowed to be a field, the figures allowed to go. Nothing is
produced. Nothing is defended either. It is the phase in which what you have been maintaining gets to
stop being maintained, and it is available to someone who has done nothing at all that day.

**Contractive being.** Not-doing turned into a wall. Withdrawal that is a posture rather than a phase —
the retreat that is a refusal, the stillness that is a grudge, the serenity that is actually a boundary
being held against everyone outside it. This is the one the single-axis version cannot see, because on
one axis *stillness* and *goodness* are the same reading, and here they come apart cleanly.

**That cross is the last instruction.** Not *do more* and not *rest more*. **Run the rhythm, and check
the orientation separately** — because the rhythm will not tell you the orientation and the orientation
will not tell you the phase, and every account that collapses them into one dial produces advice that is
right half the time by accident.

---

### IV. Giving the rest-phase its innocence back

There is a specific reader this chapter owes something to, and it is the reader who has taken Book VII
seriously.

Seven chapters of ethics, worked without cushioning, with contraction named as the mechanism under most
of what goes wrong: the fortress, the shame that shrinks the chamber, the boundary made final. A reader
who has absorbed that has learned something correct and has also, almost certainly, learned something
that was never said — **that turning inward is where the failure starts.** That the inward movement is
the one to watch. That when you find yourself pulling back from the world, that is the beginning of the
thing the book spent a book warning about.

It is not. And the mistake matters more than a vocabulary problem, because the person most likely to
make it is the person doing the practice best: the one who reads their own states carefully, has learned
what the fortress feels like from the inside, and now cannot distinguish it from an ordinary exhale.

So, unhedged: **the turning back is half the breath.** The evening where nothing gets made. The
conversation you do not have because you have nothing to say and are not pretending otherwise. The week
after a hard thing where the world is at arm's length and you are not sealing it out, you are letting the
boundaries you built to survive it come down slowly. **None of that is the adversary's posture.** It
does not become the adversary's posture by lasting a while, either. The polarity is not a function of
duration.

The test is not *am I turning inward*. Book VII ruled on that question in as many words —
**contraction in itself is innocent**, and *"an ethics that read every drawing-in as wickedness would
have to indict every act of devotion ever made."* That ruling was written for the surgeon and the vow
and the sickbed. It covers the evening where nothing gets made.

What is left to ask is the orientation — **is the individuation being held against the whole, or
expressed as part of it?** — and that question does not answer itself, which is why Book VII never left
it to. It gave it a mark, at VII.3, and the mark is neither direction nor intensity: **does the thing
run *through* another navigator's navigation, or *over* it?**

Put a phase of your own under that and it stops being abstract, because a phase has to run through or
over somebody to be either. A person resting is not steering anyone. A person withdrawing to punish, to
be unreachable, to make a point, to keep an account open is steering someone — and steering them by
removing what they could do about it, which is *over* in a single word. The two look identical from
outside and often from inside, and they are not structurally the same act.

Which brings the difficulty into focus honestly rather than resolving it: **these two are hard to tell
apart in your own case, and the difficulty is not a failure of self-knowledge.** It is a feature of the
architecture. Reading your own filter stack was VIII.2's problem and it does not get easier here. What
helps is not more introspection. What helps is the thing VIII.6 established — the other players — because
contractive rest has a signature that is almost always visible to somebody else first: it *costs someone*.
Radiant rest costs nobody. If you cannot tell which one you are in, the question is not *how do I feel*;
it is *who is currently paying for this* — which is *over* asked in civilian words, and unlike a feeling
it has an answer you can go and find.[^4]

---

### V. Not a metronome

One more correction, and it is aimed at the reader who has accepted everything so far and is about to
turn it into a schedule.

**The oscillation is not a metronome.** It has no fixed period, the phases are not equal in length, and
nothing about the account implies they should be. Wednesday is not owed an inhale because Tuesday had an
exhale. A doing phase can run for a month and a being phase for an afternoon, and the reverse, and both
can be correct.

This matters because the schedule version fails in a specific and expensive way: it turns the being
phase into another thing to *do*. Rest at 6pm. Meditate for twenty minutes. The recovery week in the
calendar. All of these are doings wearing the other phase's clothes, and they are boundary-generation
right through the period that was supposed to be boundary-relaxation — which is why the rest so often
does not work, and why the person who took it feels obscurely cheated and concludes they need more of it.

More of a doing does not produce a being.

What the rhythm actually sounds like: **not a metronome — the sound wings make.** A wingbeat is an
oscillation and it is also nothing like a click track. It varies with load, with air, with what the bird
is doing; it is faster in the climb and it stops entirely in the glide; and the glide is not a failure of
the wingbeat, it is what the wingbeat was for. The rhythm is *in service of the flight*, and the flight
is the thing. Anything that makes the beat regular at the expense of the flight has inverted the whole
arrangement.

So the practical form of the instruction is negative, which is unusual for this book and is correct here:
**stop overriding it.** You do not have to build the oscillation. It is running. It has been running the
entire time, under whatever was on top of it — and what is usually on top of it is a doing that has been
extended past its own useful length because stopping felt like losing, or a being that has been extended
past its own because starting felt like exposure. The work is not to install a rhythm. The work is to
stop holding one phase open past the point where it went dead, which requires being able to feel that it
went dead, which is the practice the previous seven chapters were.

---

### VI. What the whole thing was, in one sentence

There is a claim this account has been organised around from the beginning and has, until now, been
stated only in its parts. It belongs here, because the oscillation is the form it takes in a life.

**Coherent multi-scale systems hold matters open until an informed measurement collapses them.**[^5]

Every load-bearing word in that sentence was bought earlier in this book, which is the reason it can be
said this way. *Open* is II.7's: a matter is open when more than one way it could go is still live, which
is not the same as not yet known. *Collapse* is II.7's as well, and II.7 is the one chapter with the
standing to have spent it, because a collapse is a thing the reader has already watched happen to a lung,
a bridge, a market and a folding chair — **a structure that was holding itself open stops, all at once,
and where there was an arrangement there is now one settled fact.** And *informed* is the grade: a
collapse happens at the grade of whatever it happened at, and there is no collapse from nowhere.

Held still, that same condition reads as a coupling, and the coupling is the form most of this book has
used it in. A system has a *structure* — what it is, its boundaries, its form, the distinctions that make
it that thing and not another. It has a *process* — what it does, how it moves, the way it goes. The
claim is that in anything that holds together, these are not two facts that need reconciling. They are
one fact approached from either end. The structure is the shape the process has settled into; the process is what
the structure is doing. Where they come apart — where a thing's form no longer matches how it moves, or
where its movement is no longer producing its form — that is where things fall apart, and the falling
apart is diagnosable before it is visible.

And the multi-scale part is not decoration. **The same condition holds, in the same form, at scales that
have nothing else in common** — the breath, the day, the life, the institution, the civilisation. A body
that has lost the coupling is ill. An institution that has lost it is the one whose stated form and
actual behaviour have separated, which Book VI worked at length. A life that has lost it is the one where
the person's account of who they are and the shape of their days no longer refer to each other.

Being and doing are that condition made temporal — and here the terminator is the whole of the point,
because the oscillation *is* the holding and the collapsing, running in a life. **Being is the holding
open. Doing is the informed measurement that collapses it.** Every distinction you make settles
something that was live until you made it, and settles it at the grade you made it from; every phase of
being is a matter left live a while longer. That is why §I could define the two the way it did without
having earned this sentence yet. **Structure is what doing produces and being perceives. Process is what
being permits and doing performs.** Run only one and the coupling breaks in the predictable direction: pure doing makes structure faster than anything can perceive it, which is how
a life fills with things that no longer mean anything; pure being perceives structure it is no longer
making, which is how a person becomes an excellent describer of a life they are not living.

Neither the word *balance* nor the word *harmony* is the right one, and both are refused deliberately.
Balance implies a static point, and there is not one. **The condition is dynamic — it is held by moving,
the way a bicycle is upright.**

---

### VII. The last instruction

There is no summary coming. Summarising was never available to this account: an account of a live ground
that could be closed off in a paragraph would be advertising its own falsity.

What can be given is the instruction, and it is smaller than the architecture behind it, which is how it
should be.

**Do. Then be. Then do.**

Make the distinction, take the action, draw the boundary, say the thing — and then let it go and let the
field be a field for a while, and then make the next distinction. Not because the alternation is
virtuous. Because it is what you are, and the suffering that is genuinely optional — the second arrow's
territory, not the first's — is very largely the suffering of a phase held open past its life by
somebody who thought stopping was losing.

And check the orientation, separately, on its own axis: *held against the whole, or expressed as part of
it?* — and where that will not answer itself, which in your own case is most of the time, use the mark
the ethics actually turns on and ask whether the thing runs *through* another's navigation or *over* it.
Ask both of the doing and ask both of the being. Both phases can be either. Neither phase answers for
the other.

That is all of it. The rest of what this book contains is the reason the instruction is not glib —
because *do, then be, then do* on its own is a fortune cookie, and it stops being one exactly when you
know what is doing the oscillating, what the boundaries are made of, why there is nobody above the game
to be doing this for, and what the other players are.

The last image is the right one to end on, and it is taken alone, severed from the
paragraphs around it, which this account has refused elsewhere and does not get to quietly re-adopt at
the close.[^6] The image survives the severance intact:

> **a symphony that does not end, but plays on, *do be do be do*, forever.**

Which is a way of saying the thing this book has been unable to stop saying: **it does not resolve, and
that is not a defect in it.** A resolved chord is a finished piece. The playing on *is* the music. You
are not waiting for the end of it and there is not going to be one, and the correct response to that is
not resignation. It is to take the next breath, which you were going to take anyway, and to notice —
once, and then get on with it — that the breathing was never the interruption of the work.

It was the work.
