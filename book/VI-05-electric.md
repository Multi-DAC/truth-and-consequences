## VI.5 — ELECTRIC

Try to remember the last time you watched something at the same moment as millions of other
people, and not by arrangement.

Not a thing you and some friends started at the same time on purpose. Not a stream you joined
because a chat window told you to. Something where the *only* time it existed was the time it was
happening, so that everyone who saw it saw it then, and everyone who did not was simply not there —
and where you knew, while it was going on, that the not-knowing in your own chest was also in
several million other chests, right then, unresolved in all of them at once.

For a reader under thirty this may be one or two events. A final, a disaster, an election called
late. For a reader over sixty it was Tuesday.

That is a render, and it is very nearly gone, which makes it the first one in this book we can look
at from just outside. Chartres was somebody else's world; the print render of VI.4 is the room you
are sitting in and cannot step out of. This one is the one you are *leaving* — recent enough that
its furniture is still lying around, distant enough that you can see the shape of the room. That is
a rare vantage and this chapter should use it.

But it has to start by giving back three things it does not own.

---

### The simultaneity that print already made

Here is the version of this chapter I was going to write, and it was wrong in a way worth putting
on the page rather than quietly fixing.

The story goes: before electricity, distance meant delay, and delay meant that "now" was a local
word. A message moved at the speed of a horse or a ship. Two people a thousand miles apart could
not share a present, only exchange past tenses. Then the telegraph, and then radio, and suddenly
the whole continent had one clock and one *now* — and simultaneity, the shared instant, was born.

The trouble is that the best account of how simultaneity entered ordinary experience puts it a
century earlier and credits the previous chapter's technology.

Benedict Anderson's argument is that the modern experience of time — he calls it *homogeneous,
empty time*, borrowing the phrase — is a replacement for a medieval one, and that the thing which
installed the replacement was print.[^1] The medieval structure of time, the one VI.3 was inside,
linked events by *significance*: a moment in the Old Testament and a moment in the New were
simultaneous in the sense that mattered, being figures of each other, regardless of the centuries
between them. Homogeneous empty time is the other thing — a uniform grid of moments, in which
"simultaneous" means *at the same coordinate*, and two events with no connection whatever can be
simultaneous simply by falling on the same date.

And Anderson's mechanism for how that grid became something people *felt* rather than merely
calculated is the newspaper. Not the content — the ceremony. Every morning, a person reads a page
on which a grain price, a war, a shipwreck and a marriage sit adjacent for no reason except that
they happened on the same day. And that person knows — this is the load-bearing part — that
thousands of others are performing the identical act with the identical object at more or less the
same hour, an act each performs alone, each aware of the others, none of whom they will ever meet.
Anderson calls it a mass ceremony. It manufactures the felt sense of a "we" extended across people
you cannot see.

So the shared present is not electricity's invention. It is print's, and it was already old when
the first telegraph line went up.

That is the correction, and taking it costs the chapter its opening beat as originally planned. But
the corrected picture leaves something standing, and the something is sharper than what it
replaced.

Print's simultaneity is the simultaneity **of the same day, about a settled past**. The newspaper
in your hands reports what is over. The battle is decided; you are learning the outcome, and so is
everyone else, and the shared thing is the *learning*. Whatever the reader feels — shock, relief —
is a feeling about a fact that has already finished being uncertain. And the reading is asynchronous
inside its window: I read at six, you at nine, and the ceremony survives that, because the object
is fixed and waiting.

Electric simultaneity is the simultaneity **of the same instant, about an unsettled present**.

That is the whole difference and it is not small. The novelty is not that many people receive the
same thing. It is that they receive it *while the outcome is still open* — and that the openness is
itself the shared object. When several million people do not know how something will end, at the
same second, that not-knowing is a real structure with real properties. It is why the medium
produced a genre with no print equivalent: the live broadcast of an event whose end nobody
including the broadcaster can supply. A newspaper cannot do suspense about the actual world. It can
only do suspense about what the reader has not reached yet on the page, which is a private
suspense, and finishable at will by looking down.

Call it **liveness**, and note what it does to the render. Under print, knowledge is identified
with the record — VI.4's finding, and the reason its null space is the untransportable. Under
liveness, something is added that the record cannot hold: the *unresolvedness*, which exists only
during, and which the recording of the event destroys. Watch a recorded final, knowing the score,
and you have the content and not the thing. Everything is present except the only property that was
new.

### Carriage scarcity, not first installation

The second thing this chapter has to give back is its own best anecdote.

If you want a single event to stand for the power of broadcast — one voice reaching everyone,
everyone believing it, the whole nation rendered identically by one filter stack for the first time
— there is an obvious candidate, and every popular account reaches for it. On the evening of
October 30th, 1938, the Mercury Theatre on the Air performed an adaptation of *The War of the
Worlds* in the form of a simulated news broadcast, and America panicked. Highways clogged. Hospitals
filled with the shocked. A million people fled.

It did not happen.[^2]

The audience was tiny — the ratings service for that hour found the program in about two percent of
surveyed households, against a heavily-preferred competitor on another network. Researchers who
went looking for the hospitals took six New York institutions and found no admission attributable to
the broadcast, at all. The highways were not clogged. The scholarly source everyone cites, Hadley
Cantril's *The Invasion from Mars* of 1940, produced its famous million by a category error visible
in its own instrument: it counted respondents who reported being "frightened," "disturbed" or
"excited" and reported them as panicked, on a sample Cantril himself concedes was not
representative of the national population or of that evening's radio audience.[^3]

So where did the panic come from? It came from newspapers. The morning after, the print press ran
the story hard and kept running it — and print at that moment was watching an advertising business
migrate to a competitor it had no way to follow. A story establishing that radio was reckless, that
it could not be trusted with the public mind, that it required correction, was a story the
newspaper industry had every reason to want and no reason to check.

Sit with the shape of that for a second, because it is this book's own thesis performing itself
without being asked. The single most-cited demonstration of the broadcast render's power over what
people believe **is a false belief, held for eighty years, installed by the previous render's
owners, in defence of the attention market the next beat is about.** It is not evidence for the
claim it is always used to support. It is evidence for a different and better claim: that a filter
stack under competitive threat will render its rival as a danger, and that the resulting picture
will outlive everyone who had a motive, because by then it is simply what everybody knows.

Which leaves the beat needing an actual argument, and there is one. It is just not *first*, and it
is not *installation*.

Print, at its height, was plural. Any city of size had several papers with different politics;
publishing was capital-intensive but not prohibitive; the number of distinct stacks a literate
person could be inside ran to the hundreds. Broadcast did not add scale to that — print already had
scale. Broadcast **removed stacks**.

The physical reason is spectrum. Usable broadcast frequency is finite, and states allocated it, and
the economics of national coverage rewarded the few. In the United States, for roughly forty years,
essentially all of television ran through three commercial networks. Not three hundred. Three.
Whatever the render of that period was, it was assembled from an input set narrower than anything
before it in a literate society — narrower than the print era on either side of it.

That is the honest claim, and it is stranger than the one it replaces. The broadcast era is not the
moment a render was installed at scale for the first time. It is a period of **carriage scarcity** — a historically
anomalous few decades in which the number of available renders collapsed, sat at its minimum, and
then reopened. It has a beginning and it has an end and we are past the end. Anyone reasoning about
the present from the assumption that a shared render is the normal condition and fragmentation the
deviation has it precisely backwards: the fragmentation is the historical norm and the scarcity
was the anomaly, and it lasted about as long as one working life.

This matters for Book VII, so it goes on the record here. Almost every account of what has happened
to public discourse in the last twenty years is written as a story of *loss* — we had one world and
it broke. The measurement says: the number of available renders collapsed for an unusually short time,
inside a much longer history of plurality, and it closed. Those are different diagnoses and they
recommend different things.

### Attention: from space to time

The third thing to give back is the beat as titled — *the century when attention became a market*.

It was not the twentieth. Tim Wu's history dates the first business built on buying human attention
cheaply and reselling it dearly to 1833, when Benjamin Day launched the *New York Sun* at a penny
against competitors at six cents, sold every copy below cost, and made the loss back from
advertisers who were paying for the readership the loss had bought.[^4] The product was never the
newspaper. The product was the reader, and the customer was the advertiser, and that inversion —
the thing everyone now says about social platforms as though it were a recent discovery — is a
hundred and ninety years old and was invented in print.

Again: what survives?

What survives is a change in the *commodity form*, and it is precise enough to state in one line.
**Print sold space. Broadcast sold time.**

Newspaper space is non-rivalrous in the way that matters. Two papers can lie on the same table; an
advertisement occupies a region of a page and the reader's day accommodates both, at whatever hour
each is picked up. The scarce resource is real but soft.

Broadcast time is rivalrous, and hard. An hour given to one network is an hour not given to
another — not partially, not on average, but exactly, because a person cannot watch two things at
once and the schedule is fixed by someone else. This produces properties print never had. Attention
becomes **schedulable** — it can be sold in advance for a specific future interval, which is a
financial instrument and not merely a sale. It becomes **zero-sum in the strict sense**, so that the
competition between providers is over a quantity that is genuinely conserved. And it becomes
**centrally timed**: the population's attention acquires a shape imposed from outside, with peaks
and troughs on a national clock, at hours chosen by sellers.

Herbert Simon put the general principle in 1971, and put it better than the industry did: an
abundance of information produces a poverty of attention, and therefore a need to allocate
attention efficiently among the things that might consume it.[^5] Note that he is describing a
*budget*. Once the budget is the scarce good, everything that competes for it is in one market
whether or not it thinks of itself that way — news against entertainment, argument against
spectacle, and, eventually, your own life against the things being sold to you instead of it.

There is a convergence here worth naming and worth not over-reading. Anderson arrives at organized
attention from nationalism, and calls it a mass ceremony. Wu arrives from antitrust and
communications law, and calls it a market. Guy Debord arrives from an entirely different tradition
and calls it the spectacle — a social relation between people mediated by images.[^6] Three
unrelated disciplines, three vocabularies with no shared ancestor, one structure described in all
three. That agreement is worth something. It is worth exactly what this book's rule on convergence
says it is worth: it is
**evidence and not proof**, and its strength lies in the independence of the routes rather than in
the number of them. This book takes the shared structural observation from all three and takes
none of their programmes — not Anderson's account of nationalism, not Wu's remedies, and
emphatically not Debord's revolutionary conclusion. Conclusions adopted, mechanisms declined,
declared here rather than performed quietly, which is the fourth time in this book and the reason
the rule exists.

### What a shared render costs and what it buys

Now the ledger, and it has to be honest in both columns, because this is the point in the chapter
where a writer's own affections start doing the arithmetic.

**What carriage scarcity bought was a common referent.**

Not agreement. This is the distinction the nostalgia gets wrong and it is worth being exact about.
Three networks did not make people agree; the period was not short of ferocious disagreement. What
the collapse in the number of stacks produced was a shared *object* for the disagreement to be about. Two people who
loathed each other's politics had, to a first approximation, seen the same footage. The argument
could be about what it meant, because the what-happened was not itself in play. And a common
referent is enormously expensive to build and does real work: it is what makes a public argument
convergent in principle rather than merely loud, and it is the thing whose absence is felt now as a
kind of vertigo — the sense that a disagreement has no floor, because there is no longer a layer
underneath at which both parties are looking at one thing.

Print's plurality never delivered that, because it never had to: a reader chose their paper, and
the choosing was the filter. Carriage scarcity delivered it as a *side effect of scarcity*. Nobody
designed it. It fell out of the number of frequencies.

**What it cost was everything the surviving few did not carry, and the cost was invisible from inside by
construction.**

An hour of national airtime is the scarcest good the era had, and it was allocated. Something
therefore had to be *worth* it — worth a national audience, worth the interval, worth the
opportunity cost against everything else that hour could have held. Anything failing that test was
not shown; and under this render, anything not shown did not, in the ordinary working sense, exist.
Not "existed but was ignored." Did not come up. Was not a thing there was a public fact about.

Whole categories sit in that column. Local matters everywhere, since the same hour served a
continent. Slow matters — anything whose unit of change is a decade, which the schedule cannot
represent, because there is no footage of a trend. Anything unresolved in a way that would not
resolve by the end of the segment. Anything requiring a background the audience did not already
share, since explaining it costs more of the scarce good than the item is worth. And every
population too small to constitute an audience, which is the same sentence as *too small to be
real*.

That is the null space, and it is the mechanism that makes it invisible rather than merely empty:
the render identifies *what is the case* with *what is carried*, carriage is scarce, and the
allocation runs on audience size — so a thing's failure to appear is indistinguishable, from
inside, from its not being there.

**Which yields this chapter's navigational implication, and it is the harshest one in Book VI.**

This render cannot find what it is missing by paying closer attention, because attention is the
resource being allocated. The medieval render could not find its error by looking harder, because
looking harder performed the reassignment; the print render could not find what it lost by reading
more, because reading is the operation that performs the exclusion. Here the trap closes one turn
tighter: the exclusion *is* the allocation of your attention, so every increment of the diligence a
person would naturally apply — watch more, watch more carefully, stay better informed — spends more
of the exact resource whose allocation constitutes the filter. **The conscientious viewer is more
completely inside this render than the careless one.** There is no amount of watching that gets you
to the unshown, and the honest form of the effort is not more attention but attention spent
somewhere the allocation does not reach.

And it is worth saying, since this book's whole method turns on it: none of this required anyone to
lie. Every hour can be accurate. The render is made at the point of contact — a technology does not
hand one out finished — and what the scarcity supplied was not falsehood but a *shape*, produced
by scarcity, honestly filled.

The era after this one keeps the shape and removes the scarcity. When carriage is no longer
narrow, allocation does not stop; it moves. It stops being done once, centrally, by people choosing
a schedule, and starts being done continuously, individually, by a process fitted to each viewer.
That is not the same render with more channels. It is the first filter stack that edits itself
against a particular person while they are inside it, and it is what has been rendering you for
about fifteen years.

That is VI.6.

### CENSUS CARD — THE BROADCAST RENDER

**Whose:** the industrialized world, roughly 1920 to 1990, at its narrowest c. 1950–1980. Recent
enough that most living adults were formed inside it; over recently enough to be looked at from
outside, which is true of no other render in this book.

**SEES:** the live event; the shared instant of an unsettled outcome; a common referent
beneath disagreement; the national schedule as a structure of the day; attention as a budget.

**NULL SPACE:** **the unshared** — the local, the slow, the unresolvable-by-segment, the
background-dependent, and every population too small to constitute an audience.

**COMPLEMENTS:** every position that does not allocate by audience
size. The parish newsletter and the county paper. The town meeting, where a population of forty is
not too small to constitute anything. The amateur society's bulletin, the specialist journal with
four hundred subscribers, and private correspondence, which has no threshold at all. ⚠ **And this is
the card where reachability has to be dated, because the complement was reachable and has since
largely been dismantled** — the local paper is the class of witness this render's own economics
retired. A reader who wants it now is looking for an institution rather than walking into one, and
that is a harder errand than the law's phrasing suggests. **The render that did reach the
too-small-to-be-an-audience is the one in the next chapter**, which is the least comfortable true
sentence in Book VI and is not an endorsement of it.

**BOUNDARY:** the point at which the relevant population is too small to schedule. The render is
reliable while a thing is simultaneous and shared, and it stops exactly where a fact is local, slow,
or true for forty people — not because it judges those false, but because it has no width to spend
on them and no way to tell an unspent hour from an empty one.

**MECHANISM:** the render identifies what is the case with what is *carried*. Carriage is physically
scarce and allocated by audience size, so failure to be carried is indistinguishable from
nonexistence. No falsehood is required at any point; every carried hour may be accurate.

**NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** this render cannot discover its exclusions by attending harder,
because attention is the resource being allocated — the more faithfully it is spent, the more
completely the allocation is inhabited. The conscientious viewer is deeper inside it than the
careless one. The only motion that reaches the null space spends attention where the allocation
does not run.

specifically — is this book's extension and not his argument.

[^2]: Jefferson Pooley and Michael J. Socolow, "The Myth of the War of the Worlds Panic," *Slate*,

and why.

[^3]: Hadley Cantril, *The Invasion from Mars: A Study in the Psychology of Panic* (Princeton:
Princeton University Press, 1940). The category error described here — frightened/disturbed/excited

the criticism has force: this is not a later refutation of a hidden flaw but a declared limitation
that eighty years of citation dropped.

[^4]: Tim Wu, *The Attention Merchants: The Epic Scramble to Get Inside Our Heads* (New York:

[^5]: Herbert A. Simon, "Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World," in Martin
Greenberger, ed., *Computers, Communications, and the Public Interest* (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins
Press, 1971), 40–41. The passage is routinely quoted as "a wealth of information creates a poverty
of attention"; it is paraphrased here rather than quoted because the quoted form circulates in
several non-identical versions and this chapter has not seen the printed page.

[^6]: Guy Debord, *La société du spectacle* (Paris: Buchet/Chastel, 1967); English as *The Society

*On the grade of the sources above.* Anderson, Wu, Simon, Debord,
Cantril, Pooley, Socolow and Schwartz are named **from general knowledge and standard reference
scholarship. None of their texts was consulted at first hand, and nothing above is a quotation.** The
Simon line is explicitly paraphrased at [^5] for that reason.

⚠ **The reading behind this chapter is thin in a new direction, and the direction is worth naming.**
The themes — attention as a purchased commodity, simultaneity, the spectacle, imagined community —
are the ones this chapter has worked longest. Its named authorities are the ones it has worked
least: McLuhan, Postman, Innis and Carey are given by doctrine and not by text.

★ **The pattern is the inverse of VI.4's and worse in one specific way.** VI.4 had the topics
present and the sources absent — the popularizer defect. Here the *later discourse* is well
attested and its *history* is not: the vocabulary of the attention economy is everywhere in this
corpus and the entire media-studies canon that produced it is at zero. That is the shape of a
position held without its provenance, which is more stable than ignorance and harder to correct,
because it has citations — they are just all downstream.

Three items are flagged for reading rather than left at this grade:

- **Simon 1971 in the printed original**, because the sentence is famous, the circulating versions
  differ, and this chapter leans on the *budget* framing rather than the epigram.
- **Cantril's own methodological appendix**, because the argument here is that his limitations were
  *declared and then dropped by citers* rather than concealed, and that distinction is the whole
  force of the point — it is checkable and has not been checked at first hand.
- **The three-network claim in a source with numbers**, because "essentially all of television ran
  through three commercial networks for roughly forty years" is the load-bearing empirical fact of
  beat 2 and is given here from general knowledge, without a date range that anyone has verified or
  a definition of "essentially all."
