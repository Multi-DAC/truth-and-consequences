## VI.6 — THE ALGORITHMIC TUNNEL

There is a story about this era that almost everyone reading this already believes, and this book
has to give it back before it can say anything true.

The story is the filter bubble. It goes like this: a machine watches what you click, learns what
holds you, and feeds you more of it. Over time the machine builds each person a private chamber of
the agreeable. Two neighbours open the same application and receive two incompatible worlds. The
chamber is sealed, it is getting tighter, and it explains the condition of public life.

Eli Pariser gave the frame its name in 2011 and it is one of the most successful pieces of
vocabulary of the century.[^1] It is also, in the strong form just stated, the most thoroughly
tested claim in the whole of media studies — and it has mostly failed the tests.

That is not a comfortable sentence to write in a chapter whose entire subject is that renders
differ. But this book has now taken three corrections in three chapters, and the discipline is the
same each time: when the popular account and the best measurement disagree, the measurement wins,
and what survives is usually sharper than what it replaced. It was sharper for the print era. It
was sharper for the broadcast era. It is sharper here — and here, unusually, the surviving version
is *worse news* than the story it replaces.

---

### What the measurements actually found

Start with the strongest early study, because it was run by the platform on its own data and
therefore had every incentive to find nothing.

Bakshy, Messing and Adamic looked at ten million American Facebook users and separated three
different things that the filter-bubble story runs together: what your friends share, what the
ranking algorithm puts in front of you, and what you click.[^2] The finding that mattered was the
ordering. Ideological segregation was real, and the largest contributor to it was not the
algorithm. It was who your friends are — and after that, what you yourself chose to open.
"Compared with algorithmic ranking," the paper says, "individuals' choices played a stronger role
in limiting exposure to cross-cutting content."

That result is regularly reported as *the algorithm is innocent*, which is not what it says. It
says the algorithm was a smaller term than homophily. The bubble was already there. It is made of
people you like, and it predates every machine in this chapter by the entire history of human
settlement.

Then, in 2023, the experiment. Andrew Guess and a large collaboration were given access to run
randomized interventions on Facebook and Instagram during the 2020 US election: take consenting
users, switch them out of the algorithmic feed into a reverse-chronological one, and leave them
there for three months.[^3] This is the thing the frame demands. Remove the filter and see what the
person believes when the chamber is opened.

The on-platform effects were large. Time on the platform dropped sharply. The composition of what
people saw changed a great deal — more political content, more content from moderate friends and
ideologically mixed sources, and, awkwardly for the reform position, *more* untrustworthy content
rather than less.

The attitude effects were nothing. Not small: nothing. Issue polarization, affective polarization,
political knowledge — none of it moved over three months.

If the filter bubble as popularly told were true, that is the single result that could not have
happened. And for about two and a half years it stood as the best evidence available, and the
reasonable conclusion from it was that the chamber does not work the way the story says.

### The experiment that ran the other direction

Here is where this chapter stops agreeing with the debunking and turns.

Every study above shares a design feature that is rarely treated as a limitation, because it does
not look like one. **They all switch the algorithm off.** They take people who have lived inside a
personalized feed for years, remove it, and measure what changes. When nothing changes, the
inference drawn is that the algorithm was not doing anything.

The direction has been run the other way, and by researchers this chapter has already cited.[^4]
It matters enough to state before the turn rather than after it, because the result points the
other way from the one this chapter is about to build on.

In February 2026, Germain Gauthier, Roland Hodler, Philine Widmer and Ekaterina Zhuravskaya
published a field experiment on X that ran *both* directions.[^5] Just under five thousand active
American users, seven weeks, randomized with equal probability to either feed, and the analysis
conditions throughout on which feed each participant was already using. So one arm took
chronological users and switched the algorithm **on**. The other took algorithmic users and
switched it **off**. Two experiments, opposite in sign, in one population, at one time.

The off-arm reproduced the earlier nulls. "Remarkably," the authors write, "none of the political
attitudes were affected by the reverse switch from the algorithmic to the chronological feed, that
is, by switching the algorithm off."

The on-arm did not.

Switching the algorithm on moved political opinion, in a consistent direction, across several
distinct measures: policy priorities became more conservative (+0.11 SD), views of the criminal
investigations into Donald Trump moved toward regarding them as unacceptable (+0.08 SD), views on
the war in Ukraine moved toward pro-Kremlin positions (+0.12 SD), and the combined
policies-and-current-events index moved +0.12 SD. Engagement rose. These are modest effects in a
seven-week window on a single platform, and they are effects.

So the algorithm's influence on belief is not zero and is not symmetric. **It runs one way.** You
can be moved into this render and you cannot be moved out of it by the removal of the thing that
moved you in.

And the authors found the mechanism, which is the part that makes this a chapter of this book
rather than a finding about a website. Users switched into the algorithmic feed started **following
more conservative accounts** — +0.17 SD for conservative accounts, +0.18 SD for conservative
political-activist accounts. And then: "when the algorithmic feed was switched off, users continued
to follow the accounts they had engaged with previously."

Read that slowly, because it is the whole chapter.

The algorithm's product is not a state of mind. It is a **structure** — a set of standing
connections that generate a world. Once that structure exists, the algorithm is no longer load-
bearing. It was the scaffolding. Remove the scaffolding and the building stands, because the
building is what was being built.

The authors say so explicitly, and extend it to explain the earlier nulls: "This mechanism may also
help to explain why previous research found no political effects of switching the algorithm off on
Meta platforms, even though it altered political content."

The debunking literature was not measuring whether the filter works. It was measuring **whether
turning it off restores what was there before.** It does not. That was reported as an absence of
effect for two and a half years, and it is very close to the opposite: it is the signature of an
effect that has finished writing itself into a substrate that does not need the writer any more.

### Selection, not persuasion — and the measurement that separates them

This book has claimed since Book II that renders differ by what they *contain*, not by what their
inhabitants have been talked into. That distinction has been an assertion. In this chapter it
becomes something closer to a measurement, and the evidence for it comes from the same experiment —
specifically, from what did *not* move.

Alongside the attitude shifts, Gauthier and colleagues report "precisely estimated null effects of
the algorithm on partisanship and affective polarization, whether the algorithm was switched on or
off." Precisely estimated is the operative phrase; this is a measured zero with tight bounds, not a
failure to detect.

So: policy priorities moved. Assessments of specific current events moved. Partisan
self-identification did not. Feeling toward the other side did not.

That is a very strange result under a persuasion model. If a machine is talking people into
conservatism, the thing that should move first and hardest is the tribal marker — what team you say
you are on, and how much you dislike the other one. Those are the most responsive quantities in all
of political psychology. They sat still while *what people thought was important* and *what people
took to be the case about particular events* moved.

Under a selection model this is exactly right, and it is nearly the definition. Nobody argued
anybody into anything. What changed was the contents of the world — which items existed, how often,
with what weight. Salience is not an opinion. It is a property of a render: the difference between
a fact you have met forty times this month and a fact you have never met is not a difference of
belief, and no amount of open-mindedness converts one into the other. You cannot weigh what was not
delivered.

Identity did not move because identity was not the object being edited. The **inventory** was.

### Why "misinformation" is the wrong frame

Almost the entire public and regulatory response to this era has organized itself around the
truth-value of individual items. Label the false ones. Downrank the false ones. Fact-check, flag,
demote, remove. The frame assumes that the harm is carried by falsehood, so that a sufficiently
good filter for falsehood would resolve it.

Two numbers say that programme cannot reach the mechanism.

First, the volume. Jennifer Allen and colleagues measured fake news against the whole information
diet — mobile, desktop and television, on a nationally representative sample — rather than against
the subset of the diet that lives on one platform. Fake news came to about **0.15% of Americans'
daily media consumption**.[^6] Their own conclusion is the point: "the origins of public
misinformedness and polarization are more likely to lie in the content of ordinary news or the
avoidance of news altogether than in overt fakery."

Second, the distribution. Nir Grinberg and colleagues matched Twitter accounts to voter records
during the 2016 election and found the exposure massively concentrated: **1% of users accounted for
80% of fake-news exposures, and 0.1% of users for 80% of the sharing.**[^7]

Put those together and the falsehood programme is aimed at a rounding error that is concentrated in
a tail already unreachable by it. Suppose it succeeded completely — every false item, gone. The
remaining 99.85% of the diet is true, and two people can still be handed two incompatible worlds
out of it, because the render is not built by lying. **It is built by choosing.**

This is the mechanism a truth-filter is structurally unable to see, and it needs saying without
hedging: *a selection over true items is sufficient to produce two irreconcilable pictures of
reality, with no false statement at any point.* Everything in both feeds happened. Everything in
both feeds is checkable and checks out. Two people who compare notes will each find the other's
items accurate and the other's world unrecognizable, and neither has been deceived by anyone.

That is the same structure VI.5 identified in the broadcast render — *no falsehood is required at
any point; every carried hour may be accurate* — arriving with the scarcity removed and the
allocation moved from a schedule to a person. It is not a new mechanism. It is the old one, fitted
individually.

Which brings us to the thing in this era that genuinely has no precedent, and it is not the one
the popular story names.

### The null space stops being public

Every render in this book has a null space: the region it cannot deliver, which its inhabitants
experience not as absence but as *nothing there*. What differs between eras is the shape of the
hole, and — this is the part that has gone unremarked until now — **who else has the same one.**

In the enchanted render of VI.3, the null space was shared by a civilization. In the print render
of VI.4, it was shared by everyone who reads. In the broadcast render of VI.5, it was shared by
everyone inside the carriage: three networks, one national schedule, and therefore *one set of
exclusions*, held in common by tens of millions of people simultaneously.

A common null space has a remarkable property that nobody noticed while it was in force. **It is
discoverable by comparison.** Not from inside — you cannot see your own hole by looking harder,
that was VI.3's finding, and looking is the operation that fills it. But because everyone's
exclusions were the same exclusions, the hole was a *public fact*. It could be named. Somebody who
had been to the excluded place could say *this is not on television* and be understood by every
listener, because every listener had the identical gap in the identical position. The dissident,
the specialist, the person from the small town that was too small to constitute an audience — each
of them could describe the missing thing to anyone, and the description landed, because the
audience's absence was congruent with the speaker's.

That is what this render removes, and it is the genuinely new thing.

When the filter is fitted per person, **the exclusions are no longer shared** — and comparison
stops working. Your neighbour's absence is a different absence. When you and they compare notes,
you do not discover the shape of a hole; you discover that your accounts differ, which is a fact
with a hundred available explanations, and the two you will reach for first are *they are misled*
and *they are lying*. Both are available immediately. Both are wrong. The true explanation —
*we are being handed different inventories of a world neither of us has misdescribed* — is the
only one that requires knowing something about the mechanism, and the mechanism is precisely what
the render does not deliver.

So the individuation of the filter does not primarily change what you see. It changes what
**disagreement means**. In a common render, two people who disagree are disagreeing about a shared
object, and the argument, however ferocious, has a floor. VI.5 called it the common referent, and
was careful to say that the era it belonged to had no shortage of ferocity — what it had was
something for the ferocity to be *about*. Remove the commonality of the null space and you do not
get more disagreement. You get disagreement with no floor under it, in which the ordinary,
correct-feeling move — *check what they said, it is false or they are dishonest* — returns a
confident answer that is wrong in a new way. The other person's items are true. You have simply
never been handed them, and neither of you can see that from where you are standing.

This is what VI.1 meant by *different worlds, not different opinions*, and this is the era in which
it stops being a philosophical position and becomes an operational description of a Tuesday.

### The ethics arrive here, one book early

This book files its ethics in Book VII. One piece of it has to arrive now, out of order, and the
reason is not thematic convenience: **the reader is inside this render while reading this
sentence**, which is true of no other chapter in Book VI except the print one, and the print one is
demonstrative where this one is urgent.

The ethical question in this era is not *is it wrong to lie to people*. That question is settled,
uninteresting, and — per the two numbers above — aimed at a rounding error. The live question is
harder and it has almost no philosophical literature under it:

**What is owed by a party that determines what exists for someone?**

Not what they believe. What *exists* — the inventory, the set of items that will ever be available
for consideration. A selection process that operates only over true things, deceives nobody, and
can defend every individual decision it makes, still stands in this position, and the position is
not the one that ethics of communication was built to evaluate. Every framework we have for the
morality of speech is a framework about **assertions**: their truth, their sincerity, the intent
behind them. Selection makes no assertion. It has no truth-value to be sincere about. The entire
apparatus slides off.

Shoshana Zuboff's account supplies the economics, and this chapter takes it for that and not for
its programme.[^8] Her point that matters here is about the commodity form, and it completes a
series this book has been running since VI.4. **Print sold space. Broadcast sold time. This era
sells prediction** — and the third term is different in kind from the first two, because a seller
of predicted behaviour has an interest that a seller of space or time does not: predictions improve
when the world becomes more predictable. The incentive does not stop at forecasting the person.
That is a structural fact about the arrangement, and it is available without accepting any
particular account of who intended it or what should be done about it.

But it does not answer the ethical question, and neither does anything else currently on offer,
which is why this chapter names it rather than resolving it. What is owed to a person by the party
holding the inventory is genuinely open, the frameworks that exist address the wrong act, and the
arrangement is running at global scale on approximately four billion people while the question sits
unformulated. VII.2 will take up what is owed to positions that cannot register that anything is
owed to them. This is the inverse and it is nearer: what is owed to a position that *can* register
it, is registering it right now, and cannot see the mechanism from inside.

### What this render cannot do, and the one thing it will not permit

The pattern of Book VI has been that each render forecloses its own escape, and each does it one
turn tighter than the last.

VI.3: you cannot find the error by looking harder, because looking performs the reassignment.
VI.4: you cannot find what was lost by reading more, because reading performs the exclusion.
VI.5: you cannot discover your exclusions by attending harder, because attention *is* the resource
being allocated, so ordinary diligence spends more of the exact good whose allocation constitutes
the filter.

VI.6 closes it further, and the closure is measured rather than argued: **you cannot exit by turning
it off.**

That is the finding of the off-arm, and it is worth being exact about what it does and does not say.
It does not say the filter cannot be edited; editability stands, and this book will spend it in VIII.3. It
says that **removing the mechanism is not the edit.** The mechanism's product outlived it in the
one experiment designed to check, because what it built was a set of standing connections, and the
connections generate the world whether or not the builder is still present. Delete the application
and the follow graph remains. Leave the platform and the people you now read, the topics you now
consider live, the shape of what strikes you as important — those came with you, and nothing about
your departure touches them.

The scaffolding comes down. The building is the point.

An edit that reaches this render has to operate on the accumulated selections rather than on the
selector — which is slow, deliberate, and unavailable as a settings change. There is no off switch
for a structure, because the structure was never the switch.

### Card: THE ALGORITHMIC RENDER

**Era:** roughly 2009 to now, still running — **the only render in this book that can be tested
from inside**, because it is live and people can be randomized into and out of it. Every prior
chapter reached its era through convergence across independent routes, because 1200 CE cannot be
assigned to a treatment arm. This one has an experiment, and the evidence grade in this chapter is
therefore different in kind from VI.3's, VI.4's and VI.5's. The convergence warrant is **not** taken
here and is not claimed; the argument does not rest on convergence, and saying so is the point.

**Renders:** the inventory fitted to the person; salience as a delivered quantity; the standing
connection as the real product; relevance experienced as the natural shape of the world.

**Complement — who sees what this render cannot:** ⚠ **this is the card where IV.1's stated cost
arrives in full, and it is recorded rather than smoothed.** A complement here must be a position that
sees a *distribution* rather than a person, because the exclusion is individuated and no single
viewer holds enough of it. Three exist. **The operator's own instrumentation** sees the whole surface
and is the best-placed witness in this book — and it is not consultable, by anybody, on request; it
is the clearest case in the volume of a complement that certainly exists and cannot be reached, which
is the distinction `IV.1` marks and this card pays for. **The audit literature** — sock-puppet
accounts, donated-data panels, algorithmic accountability work — reaches it partially, at
institutional cost, on a lag, and its findings are public. **And one move is available to the reader
this afternoon at no cost: put your feed beside another person's and read both.** That recovers the
*fact* of divergence, which is the thing this render most needs an outsider for, and it does not
recover the shape. **Two of the three witnesses are out of reach and the cheap one is partial** —
which is a true report about where the reader is standing and not a defect of the card.

**Null space:** **THE UNENGAGING — and, for the first time in this book, an unshared one.** Not
merely what a person is not shown, but the fact that the *shape* of what they are not shown differs
from their neighbour's, so the exclusion cannot be recovered by comparing notes. **The null space
stops being a public fact.** This is the era's actual novelty, and it is not personalization of
content — it is individuation of absence.

**Mechanism:** the render identifies what is the case with **what a model predicted this particular
person would engage with** — a proxy that is neither truth nor even preference, but forecast
behaviour, fitted per person and improved by being spent. No false item is required. A selection
over true items is sufficient to produce two irreconcilable worlds, both fully checkable, neither
misdescribed.

**Navigational implication:** the exit is not where it appears to be. The measured effect of
switching the mechanism off is nothing, because the mechanism's product is downstream of it and
persists without it. **What must be edited is what was accumulated, not what did the accumulating.**

⚠ **Seam to VI.7:** the natural defence against a render you cannot see out of is to hold no model
at all — to treat every picture as one arbitrary tunnel among others and decline to stand anywhere.
That posture has a name, a serious tradition behind it, and a price this book pays in full in the
next chapter. It is not the answer, and the reason it is not is more interesting than the posture.
