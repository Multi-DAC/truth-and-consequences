## VII.5 — SUFFERING

Something is happening to you that you did not choose and cannot stop. That is the first sentence and
it is not a hypothetical; some percentage of anyone reading this is reading it from inside that
sentence right now, and the rest have been there and will be again. This chapter is about what an
account like the one in this book is permitted to say to that, and — the harder half, and the reason
the chapter exists — what it is *forbidden* to say. There is more of the second than a reader might
expect from a work that has been fairly free with its explanations. The restriction is not modesty.
It is the one place where this book's explanatory habit would produce a falsehood, and the falsehood
would be attractive, and it would be told to the people least able to check it.

---

### The two arrows

The oldest useful thing anyone has said about suffering is a distinction, and it is about two and a
half thousand years old.

A man is struck by an arrow. That is the first arrow, and it hurts, and there is nothing interesting
to say about it: a body was in the path of a thing and the thing arrived. Then — and this is the
observation — the man is struck by a second arrow, and the second one he fires himself. It is the
recoil: the *why me*, the rehearsal of how it could have gone otherwise, the resistance to the fact
that it did not, the story assembling itself around the wound about what the wound means concerning
his life. The first arrow was fired by the world. The second was fired from inside, at a target that
was already hit.[^1]

The claim is not that the second arrow is imaginary. It is a real wound and it hurts in the same
currency. The claim is that it is *added* — that a quantity of the suffering in a life is generated
by the sufferer, downstream of the event, and that the two quantities have different origins and
therefore different handles. You have no purchase on the archer. You have some purchase on yourself.

In this book's terms the distinction is not a metaphor, and it is not an exhortation. It is a
description of what is happening to a position. Suffering is felt distance from coherence: the
condition of a perspective whose situation and whose structure are not fitting each other, registered
from inside as pain. The first arrow is a change in the situation. The second is a change in the
structure — a further drawing-in of the perspective around the injury, which narrows what it can
reach and so *increases* the distance it was already registering. That is why the recoil makes things
worse in a way that is not merely psychological bookkeeping. It is the same variable, moved twice, the
second time by you and in the wrong direction.

Everything the practical traditions have built on this is downstream of one observation about
leverage. The Stoics reached it independently and stated it as a dichotomy of control. The cognitive
therapies rediscovered it in the twentieth century and made it a clinical procedure. It travels well
because it is true, and because it is the rarest kind of practical truth: one that identifies a real
handle without pretending the thing being handled is small.

---

### Suffering fills whatever it is put in

There is a second observation, less famous, and it does more work in practice than the first.

Suffering is not proportional to the objective severity of its cause. It is proportional to the volume
it is in. A prisoner grieving his freedom and an executive grieving a bonus are not suffering equally
— there are moral distinctions and this book is not about to pretend otherwise — but they are each
suffering *totally*, in the sense that suffering expands to occupy whatever interior it finds. There
is no reservoir it fails to fill. A perspective is bounded and therefore has a volume, and pain
behaves in it the way a gas behaves in a sealed room: not settling at the bottom in proportion to its
cause, but distributing until it is everywhere at once.

Most people know this about themselves and treat it as a defect of character. It is not a defect of
character. It is the thermodynamics of being someone in particular, and it follows from the structure
this book spent four books establishing: you are a focusing, the focusing is bounded, and a bounded
volume does not get to have a small feeling about a large thing and a proportionally smaller feeling
about a smaller one. The scale you feel on is the scale of the perspective you are, always,
regardless of what is being felt.

Knowing this does not reduce the suffering by one unit. What it does is take away the second arrow of
shame — *I should not hurt this much over something this small* — which is the most common of all the
self-fired arrows and among the most costly, because the shame is itself a contraction, and a
contraction makes the chamber smaller, and a smaller chamber makes the same quantity of pain more
concentrated. The person who berates himself for suffering disproportionately is, by the act of
berating, making the disproportion worse. That loop is closed by the observation and by nothing else.
It is a small mercy and it is a real one, and it is available to anyone, which is more than can be
said for most of what gets offered here.

### The census entry

The two-arrow analysis is an instrument, and like every instrument in this book it earns a card by
having a boundary. The method chapter defined that field as the place where a render does not stop
but goes on returning ordinary-looking results about nothing, and warned that the condition is
invisible from inside by construction. This card is that definition's worst case in the whole work,
and the reason it is the worst is not that the readings become meaningless past the line. It is that
they stay meaningful, and invert.

**Whose:** the Buddhist analysis of the two arrows, and everything downstream of it — the Stoic
dichotomy of control, the cognitive therapies, and very nearly all of the practical advice about pain
that a person in a difficult year is likely to be handed.

**Renders:** what it renders superbly. The added suffering, isolated and made actionable. Nothing
else in the human record separates the event from the elaboration this cleanly, and the separation is
what makes the elaboration workable — you cannot stop doing a thing you have not noticed you are
doing. It renders the second arrow with a precision that has survived every reformulation and every
translation into every idiom that has picked it up, which is the signature of an instrument reading
something real. It is also, and this matters for how the rest of this section should be read, *right
about the overwhelming majority of the suffering in an ordinary life.*

**Complement — who sees the affliction:** whoever is in the room, and it has to be somebody else.
This is the one card in the atlas where the complement is *structurally* prohibited from being the
subject: the instrument fails exactly where the self that would have reported is gone, so a witness
who is not that self is not a convenience here, it is the only available channel. The palliative
nurse. The psychiatric ward. The person who sat with someone through the year and can say what they
watched, in sentences, without a scale. The traditions that debrief the far side after a return —
Book V holds several, and their reports agree with each other more than the literature expects. **All
of them are reachable and none of them are asked**, because the practical advice this render supplies
is addressed to the sufferer, and the one person who by construction cannot supply the missing
reading is the sufferer. ⚠ **A card that says *ask someone else* to a reader in affliction has
already failed. It is written for the person beside them, and that is the only form in which this
line can be delivered.**

**Null space:** what it structurally cannot render. Affliction — suffering that destroys the archer.
The instrument requires that there be someone in there to fire or not fire the second arrow, and its
entire output is a statement about what that someone is doing. Where the first arrow has been total
enough to take out the self that would have done the recoiling, the instrument has nothing left to
measure and does not report that it has nothing left to measure.

**Boundary:** where it goes from reliable to unreliable. It is reliable wherever a self survives its
pain intact enough to stand in a relation to it — which is most pain, most of the time, including
nearly all of the pain that gets described as unbearable. It becomes unreliable at exactly the point
where the pain stops being something a person is having and becomes the thing that is dismantling the
person. That point is not a matter of intensity, and this is the part that is consistently got wrong:
it is not the top of a scale that begins with a stubbed toe. It is a different phenomenon, reached by
a route the scale does not run along.

**Mechanism of the exclusion:** what the render identifies with what. It identifies suffering with a
**relation** — between someone and their pain — and its measurement is a measurement of that
relation's condition. A relation needs two terms. When affliction has removed one of them, the
instrument does what any instrument does with a missing relatum: it returns the null reading. And the
null reading for *second arrow* is the same null reading returned by mastery. **The person who has
been most thoroughly destroyed and the person who has most completely stopped adding to their own
suffering produce identical readings on this instrument.** That is not a rare edge case a careful user
can steer around. It is the shape of the device. Anyone who has sat with someone very far gone and
found them strangely calm, and has felt the calm as a good sign, has read this instrument at the point
where its output inverts.

**Navigational implication:** what to do about the other five. Use it, everywhere it is reliable,
which is most places and most days — it is the best single thing anyone has to offer about ordinary
suffering and this book is not proposing a replacement. But its output is never a **grade.** It
reports on a mechanism, not on a person's worth or progress or depth, and the one use to which it must
never be put is ranking two sufferers, because at the bottom of its range the ranking is not merely
uncertain, it is reversed.

### The floor

Simone Weil drew a line that most treatments blur, and this book takes the line as its floor.

There is suffering — pain, loss, fear, disappointment, the daily friction of being someone — and then
there is *malheur*, affliction, which is not more of that. Weil's criterion is a conjunction and the
conjunction is the whole content: physical pain, psychological anguish, and social degradation, all
three present and converging at once. Any two of them is a very bad time. All three is a different
category, and her point is that the third term is what makes it different, because social degradation
is what removes the position from which a person could be someone-suffering rather than a thing being
damaged.[^2]

Affliction does not hurt the self. It destroys the self, from the outside. Weil's phrase for what it
leaves is the mark of slavery — a stamp on the soul, put there by an agency that was never in
negotiation with it. A person in affliction does not suffer and then recover. They are unmade, and
what recovers, if something does, is not the same construction resuming.

In this book's terms the mechanism is exact and does not require any softening to fit. Affliction is
compression from outside, so extreme that the perspective can no longer generate the attentional
quality that any navigational act whatsoever requires. Not a second arrow. Not a contraction the
sufferer performed and could in principle stop performing. A crushing, done from outside, of the
apparatus by which a stopping could be chosen. The focusing is not tightened by it. It is shut.

**So the floor is this, stated once and without qualification: not all suffering discloses, and not
all suffering can be navigated through. Some suffering annihilates the navigator.**[^4] No clause in this
book overrides that sentence. Not the plenum, not the completeness of configuration space, not the
oscillation, not the account of meaning that arrives three chapters from now. If any of them appears
to, the appearance is a mistake in the reading or a mistake in the writing, and this sentence is the
one that stands.

That is not a caveat attached to an otherwise triumphant account. It is a load-bearing member. A
framework that could not say it would be a framework that had never looked at the thing it was
theorising, and this one has looked.

---

### The altitude rule

Everything above raises a question about permissions that this book has been circling since Book I
and now has to state as a rule, because from here to the end of the work it is the rule that does the
most restraining.

**A truth is not an answer unless it is true at the altitude the question was asked from.**[^7]

The propositions in this book are, as far as I can argue them, true. They are true about the
structure of things — about grounds, plenums, positions, the completeness of what is realizable. That
is an altitude. Almost nothing that anyone actually asks is asked from there. *Why is this happening
to me* is asked from inside a room, by a particular person, at a particular hour, and the fact that a
sentence about the totality can be constructed which is formally responsive to it does not make that
sentence an answer. It makes it a change of subject performed in the grammar of a reply, which is
worse than a change of subject, because it cannot be identified as one.

The rule has a test and the test is cheap. Take the consoling sentence and ask what altitude it is
true at, and then ask whether the person it is being said to is standing there. *Every configuration
is realized* is true at the altitude of the plenum. A woman whose child has died is not standing at
the altitude of the plenum; she is standing in a kitchen. Said to her, that sentence does not console
and does not fail to console — it does something worse than either, which is to arrive as an
explanation and thereby to imply that the situation was the kind of thing an explanation was owed
for, and that having been given one she has been attended to. She has not been attended to. She has
been addressed by a fact.

This is why the sustained metaphor that runs through most of this work is barred here and was barred
in the chapter on death. Not because the metaphor is inaccurate — it has been accurate for six books
— but because its altitude is fixed, and there are two subjects in this work whose altitude is a room:
irreversible loss, and affliction. A device that clarifies the metaphysics cannot be pointed at those
without converting a person into an illustration. The rule protects the reader from the book, and the
book has needed protecting from at nearly every point where it was going well.

### No theodicy

Now the prohibition, and it has to be stated with a specific target or it will not hold, because the
temptation it forbids is not an outside temptation. It is this book's own, and the source these books
were built from succumbed to it in its final section.

Here is the move, quoted in full so that it cannot be softened by paraphrase:

> This provides a powerful theodicy: a perfect, undifferentiated unity has no internal narrative, no
> drama, no growth. The "fall" into perspectival limitation is what creates the necessary conditions
> for the drama of choice, striving, and self-creation. **Every finite experience, no matter how
> painful or contracted, possesses ultimate meaning and value.** It contributes a unique and
> irreplaceable quality to the cosmic totality, imbuing all of existence with profound and inalienable
> purpose.[^5]

I am refusing that, and refusing it here rather than quietly declining to repeat it, because it is not
a stray sentence. It is a culmination — the last thing said before the argument turns to evidence, and
it is welded to the most load-bearing image in the whole account. That is precisely the position from
which a false sentence does the most damage, and the reason a reader would accept it is that
everything around it is true.

The refusal is not that the metaphysics is wrong. Take the metaphysics as given: the plenum, the
differentiation, the contribution of each perspective's quality to a whole that would be poorer
without it. Grant all of it. **The lie is in one conversion.** The passage says something about the
totality's accounts — that they balance, that nothing in them is waste — and then converts it, in the
space of a comma, into something about a *person's* accounts. Those are two claims at two altitudes,
and the second does not follow from the first. That the whole is enriched by a quality does not make
the quality's bearer compensated. There is no rate of exchange between a contribution to a totality
and a life ruined, because a totality does not have the kind of interior in which a debt could be
settled, and the person does, and it is the person's interior in which the settling would have to be
felt. It is not felt. Nothing arrives.

And the second failure, which is the one the classical theodicies at least had the honesty to be
embarrassed about: *no matter how painful or contracted* is a universal quantifier, and a universal
quantifier ranges over the cases in the floor above. It says of affliction that it possesses ultimate
meaning and value. But affliction is defined by the destruction of the one who would hold a meaning.
The sentence assigns a possession to a bearer it has already been established there is nothing left of.
That is not a hard case for the doctrine. It is the doctrine asserting something of an entity that
does not exist by the doctrine's own account of what happened to it.

Two things need saying about why this book is unusually exposed here rather than unusually safe.

The first is that we have already refused theodicy once, in Book V, and the refusal there was
structural and free: a ground with no preferences has nothing to justify, so the classical problem —
reconciling an omnipotent benevolence with a world containing children's cancer wards — does not
arise for us, because we have no omnipotent benevolence to defend. That is true. **It is also a
narrower alibi than it looks, and mistaking its scope is how a book like this ends up with a theodicy
it thinks it is immune to.** The Book V argument dissolves the *God-shaped* theodicy. It says nothing
whatever about a *mechanical* one — a theodicy with no deity in it at all, which justifies suffering
not by an author's purposes but by the structure's own productivity. That is what the quoted passage
is. There is no God in it. It is a theodicy anyway, and Book V's argument does not touch it.

The second is worse and is the reason C21 is a standing prohibition rather than a note. **This book is
better equipped to build a mechanical theodicy than a theist is to build a classical one.** The theist
has to attribute intentions to a being he admits he cannot read. We have a mechanism, in working
order, that genuinely does explain how differentiation produces the conditions for everything that
has value in it — and every step of that explanation is one I have argued and would defend. A
theodicy assembled out of true steps is not less dangerous than one assembled out of false ones. It
is more dangerous, because there is no step at which the reader can get off. The only place to refuse
it is at the conversion, and the conversion is a comma, and nobody argues with a comma.

So it is refused by rule rather than by argument-in-the-moment, because in the moment the argument
will be available and it will be good. **The framework does not make affliction meaningful. It is not
permitted to try.** A book that made affliction meaningful would contain the one provable lie in it —
provable because there is someone who could check it, and they are the one person the sentence was
constructed to be unfalsifiable to.

---

### What is left, which is not nothing

The prohibition is narrow, and its narrowness is not a loophole being kept in reserve. It has an
exact boundary and the boundary is worth stating, because a reader who takes the prohibition too
wide will conclude that this book has nothing to say about living through anything, and that is not
the position.

Meaning is available. It is found by traversal — that is the account, and the chapters after this one
make it — and the traversal is a real thing a real person does, including a person in a very bad year.
What is forbidden is the manufacture of meaning **on affliction's behalf.** The distinction is one
clause wide and it is the whole of the ethics here.

A person can find meaning on a path that runs through a catastrophe. That happens; it is common; it
is not an illusion and no one gets to tell them it was. What no one gets to do — including this book,
and especially this book, which has the vocabulary to do it elegantly — is to run the inference
backwards: from *meaning was found afterward* to *the catastrophe was for that.* The first is a
report from a position, made by the only person entitled to make it. The second is an explanation
offered from outside about an inside, and it is the same grammatical violation this work forbade on
its first page, wearing the clothes of comfort instead of the clothes of metaphysics.

Grief gets handled as itself. So does affliction. Not as a problem the metaphysics dissolves, not as
an appearance that a wide enough view corrects, not as a contribution. As what it is, at the altitude
it happened at, by someone who is willing to be in the room while it is true.

---

### Sometimes the focusing is the care

Which brings this chapter to the thing it has been walking toward, and it has to be entered from the
hard side or it is sentimental.

The previous chapter drew the line between the metaphysical act and the ethical stance, and set it
out in a sentence that does not need saying twice. It is inherited here, used and not re-earned. What
this chapter owes is the consequence, which the previous one deliberately held back: a positive claim
rather than a permission.

**The full focusing of a perspective onto one other perspective, with nothing withheld and nothing
gained by it, is not a diminishment of the person doing it. It is what care is made of.**[^6] Attention is
not a preliminary to caring, a thing you do in order to then do something helpful. In the cases that
matter most it is the entire act, because there is nothing helpful to do.

Consider where that is hardest. Someone is dying and there is no intervention left. Someone is in the
middle of a loss that has no remedy. Someone is in affliction, and by the account above there is no
navigational move available to them and no technique that will meet the situation. Every instrumental
justification for attention has been removed: you are not attending in order to fix, or to advise, or
to reframe, or to extract a lesson that will be useful later. The whole payoff structure is gone.

And attention is still the thing to do. Not as a consolation prize for having nothing better. It is
categorically the correct act, and the reason is structural rather than sentimental. Affliction works
by social degradation — it is a third of the definition — which means it works by removing a person
from the space of things that are attended to, converting them from someone in the world into a thing
in it. A perspective focused wholly and without instrumental purpose onto an afflicted person is
doing the one operation that runs directly against the mechanism of the affliction. It does not cure
it. It is not offered here as a cure and this chapter has spent four sections foreclosing that reading.
But it is the only act in the entire book that touches the mechanism at all, and it is available to
anyone, and it costs exactly what it looks like it costs.

Iris Murdoch built a whole moral philosophy on the observation and gave the operation its name:
attention is the moral act, and its difficulty is the difficulty of getting the self out of the way
long enough to see something that is not the self. She called that unselfing, and she thought — I
think rightly — that most moral failure is not a failure of will at the moment of choice but a failure
of attention long beforehand, such that by the time the choice arrives the person is choosing between
options a poorer looking has already generated.[^3] Weil is upstream of her and put it more starkly: the
capacity to give one's attention to a sufferer is rare to the point of being nearly miraculous, and
almost everyone who believes they possess it does not.

This is where the two of them and this book converge, from three directions, on one claim: **attention
is not the preparation for love. In the cases where nothing can be done, it is love, entire, with no
remainder unaccounted for.**

And it is bounded, like everything else here. Attention does not undo the affliction. It does not
make the affliction mean anything — that is still forbidden, and offering attention *as* the meaning
would be the prohibition breached at the last moment by the softest available route. It does not
guarantee that the person can receive it, because affliction can take that too. It is a real act with
a real mechanism, aimed precisely at the one thing it can reach, and claiming nothing beyond.

---

### Where this leaves the book

Two arrows. The world fires one and you fire the other, and the entire practical content of this
chapter concerns the second, because the second is the only one anybody has a handle on. That is a
small thing to be able to say after six books of cosmology and it is said small on purpose. The
alternative — a large thing, a sentence that made the first arrow all right — was available, was
generated by this book's own machinery in its own source, was quoted above, and has been refused.

What the account gives, it gives here: an explanation of why suffering is disproportionate that
removes the shame from the disproportion; a clean separation of what was done to you from what you are
doing to yourself; a named boundary past which none of it applies; and one act that works at the
bottom, where nothing else does.

What it withholds, it withholds here too, and the withholding is the more important half. It does not
tell you what your suffering is for. It does not have that information. Nobody does, and the accounts
that claim to are making a conversion between two altitudes that cannot be made, and doing it to
people who are in no condition to audit the step.

The next chapter is about love, and it arrives having already had to say the hardest true thing about
what love is for — which is that in the place where it matters most, it is not *for* anything.
