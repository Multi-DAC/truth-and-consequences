# BOOK V — THE OLD ROADS

## V.6 — THE ROOM THAT WAS NEVER EMPTIED

The last chapter ended on a subtraction. Still the mind, drop the aggregates, forget the limbs, and
what you arrive at will have nothing in it — because the instrument took everything out on the way
up.

That was a claim about method. Here is a tradition that took the subtraction out of the method and
put it in the metaphysics.

Isaac Luria taught in Safed, in the Galilee, for about three years before he died in 1572 at
thirty-eight, and wrote almost nothing. What we have is mostly Chaim Vital's, set down after his
teacher's death and organised into the *Etz Chaim*, and the transmission problem is on the surface
of the material rather than hidden underneath it. The doctrine at the head of that system is
*tzimtzum*, and in its transmitted form it runs like this.

Before anything, *Ein Sof* — the Without-End — filling everything, uniform, with no place that is
not it. There is no room for a world, and the difficulty is not that the space is occupied. It is
that a world requires somewhere that is not simply and entirely the Infinite, and there is nowhere.
So the *Or Ein Sof*, the light of the Without-End, withdraws from a point. What is left is the
*chalal panui* — the vacated space, the empty place. Into it a single thin ray, the *kav*, is let
back down, and everything that follows happens in the room the withdrawal made: the ten *sefirot*,
the vessels shaped to hold the light, and then — because the vessels of the lower seven could not
hold what was poured into them — *shevirat ha-kelim*, the breaking of the vessels, with sparks of
light scattered and caught in husks, awaiting the long work of gathering that the tradition calls
*tikkun*.

That is a story about a self-limitation that made a world possible, followed by a catastrophe.

It is the most fertile thing in Jewish mysticism and it has been borrowed constantly, usually by
people who wanted the catastrophe. And this book has to say early what it is doing with it, because
there is a version of this chapter that would be very easy to write and would cost the whole
argument.

---

**First, the thing this chapter is not.**

It is not a convergence argument. Book V has already ruled on that, and ruled against itself. The
opening of V.1 put six statements of the Ground side by side and *Ein Sof* was one of the six —
then spent the chapter taking five of them away, because Plotinus goes into Proclus goes into
Dionysius goes into Latin in the ninth century, the Baghdad translators sent the same material east
under Aristotle's name, and Kabbalah develops in Provence and Girona around 1200 in a world in
contact with both. Five of the six, in V.1's verdict, are *"one witness quoted back five times."*
Kabbalah is inside that
family. It gets counted once, with the rest of the Mediterranean, and it is not a separate arrival.

So nothing in what follows is *and they got there on their own, centuries before us*. They did not
get there on their own; they were reading, as everyone in that world was reading, and the thing they
were reading is upstream of what we are doing too. A chapter that presented Luria as independent
corroboration would be committing the precise error V.1 was written to forbid — five chapters after
forbidding it, using the tradition V.1 used as the worked example.

What is here instead is better than corroboration and is a different kind of thing entirely. **The
Kabbalists had our argument. Not our conclusion — the argument, the actual disputed question, with
both sides taken by serious people who understood exactly what was at stake.** And it split them for
two hundred years.

---

**The question is whether the withdrawal was real.**

Say it in their terms, because the terms are precise and ours are not better. Is *tzimtzum
kipshuto* — literal, as its plain sense — or *shelo kipshuto*, not as its plain sense? Did the
Infinite actually vacate a region, so that there is now somewhere the Infinite is not? Or is the
withdrawal a concealment that obtains only from the created side, with nothing whatever removed from
the Source's side?

Everything hangs on it. On the literal reading there is a place where God is not, which is either an
outside to the infinite or a limit on it, and both are disasters for the doctrine the system exists
to state. On the non-literal reading you have to explain why the tradition's foundational text
describes an event, in sequence, with a before and an after.

The dispute became one of the fault lines between the Hasidic movement and its opponents. Shneur
Zalman of Liadi, in the *Tanya* and at length in *Shaar HaYichud VehaEmunah*, takes the concealment
reading and takes it hard: the power to conceal and the power to reveal are the same power, and a
hand over your own head does not count as a covering. The *tzimtzum* affects the light, not the
Source of the light. From the divine side there is no concealment at all, because there is nobody
for it to be concealed from.

The usual telling puts the Vilna Gaon on the other side, holding the withdrawal literally, and this
chapter is not going to tell it that way, because the usual telling is a partisan simplification and
it is the losing side's position rendered by the winners. The Gaon, and the Leshem after him, located
the *tzimtzum* in the divine **Will** — *Ratzon* — and declined to say anything at all about the
Essence, *Atzmut*, on the grounds that nothing can be said about it. That is not a claim that the
Infinite has a hole in it. It is a claim about where in the account the withdrawal is predicated,
made by someone who thought the non-literal reading was doing its metaphysics in a place the human
mind has no business standing.

And the cleanest evidence that the dispute was live rather than tribal is that the Gaon's own
principal student went the other way. Chaim of Volozhin, who built the Lithuanian yeshiva world on
his teacher's authority, writes the third gate of the *Nefesh HaChayim* on this question and comes
down against him.

---

**Here is the sentence.**

> And He is still now just as He was before the creation, when all was filled with the essence of the
> *Ein Sof*, even in the space where the worlds currently exist.

*Nefesh HaChayim*, Gate III, chapter 4. Volozhin, published 1824, in Leonard Moskowitz's translation.
The clause that does the work is the last one — *even in the space where the worlds currently exist*.
The room was made and the room was never emptied. Whatever happened, it did not happen by removal.

Set that against Book I:

> The Fullness is not less for having a vantage in it, and it is not more. What lies outside your
> focus is as present as it has ever been — every bit of it, at full strength, and none of it
> anywhere you could travel to and fetch it back from.

Two clauses in one sentence, and the *Nefesh HaChayim* has both. *Even in the space where the worlds
currently exist* is the first. The second is a few lines earlier in the same chapter, in a formula
Chaim takes from the Zohar and sharpens:

> From His perspective He is called *permeates all worlds*, while from our perspective … He is
> called *surrounds all worlds*.

Filling, from the inside of the thing that fills. Surrounding — encompassing, adjacent, all around
you and not anywhere you can get to — from the inside of a creature. Not two facts. One fact with
two sides, and which side you are standing on decides which of the two sentences is available to
you. The chapter is careful that this is not a downgrade of the creature's report: the second
description is what the situation genuinely looks like from where a creature is, and there is no
correction of it from a higher vantage, because the higher vantage is not a vantage.

That is the thing the last chapter said the Eastern roads could not resist building. Advaita has a
standpoint from which the world you are in gets sublated. Madhyamaka has two truths with one above
the other. Here is a formula that has two descriptions and refuses to rank them, in a tradition
that had every incentive to rank them, written by a man defending the reality of the world against
people he thought were dissolving it.

---

**Which side is ours, and the reason is not their reason.**

Ours is the non-literal one, and it is not a close call. Book II's Ground has no outside. A region
the Ground has vacated is an outside — a somewhere with a boundary, and the Infinite on the far side
of the boundary, which is a description of a very large thing rather than of what everything is made
of. And a *chalal panui* that is genuinely empty is a hole in the Ground, which is the thing I.3
spent its opening paragraph refusing — not a hole, a position.

But the reason the Hasidic masters needed the non-literal reading is not our reason, and pretending
otherwise would be the borrowing this book keeps refusing. They needed it because a God with a
vacancy in him is not the God of *Shema Yisrael*, and because the literal reading gives ammunition
to every dualism the tradition had spent a millennium excluding. It is a defence of divine unity
against a charge of heresy, conducted inside a commitment to revealed text that we do not share and
are not entitled to lean on. Same conclusion; a different argument, reached under a different
pressure, with different things that could not be given up.

They arrive there defending God's oneness. We arrive there because an incomplete Ground cannot do
the work Book II needs the Ground to do. The agreement is real and it is not evidence, and the
distinction between those two is most of what Book V is for.

---

**What the withdrawal was for.**

There is a detail in the standard account that gets skipped in the retellings, and it is the part
worth having.

The *tzimtzum* is not described as something the Infinite needed. Nothing is served by it on that
side; there is no lack there to be met, no purpose the Without-End was short of. The function
assigned to it, consistently, is to conceal the activating force **within created things from those
things**, so that they can stand as themselves — so that a creature can be a creature and not be
annulled on the spot in the source it is made of.

Sit with the structure of that. The reason given for the limitation is the existence of somebody for
whom things can be the case. Not a fall from a better arrangement, not a price paid, not a
regrettable necessity. The availability of everything, at full strength, all at once and with
nothing held back, is precisely the condition under which there is nobody in particular for anything
to be available *to*. Something has to not be immediately given before there is a someone at all.

That is the second of this book's claims that the Kabbalists were holding: **sometimes the focusing
is the care.** Not care as compensation for the focusing — care as the same act, described from the
side of what it made possible. Book VII is where that gets argued properly, and it gets argued there
because it is difficult and because it is one careless sentence away from becoming something ugly.
Here it is only being noticed, in a tradition that stated it in the twelfth century and never
seems to have found it strange.

You already know the shape of it from the small end. Every act of attention you have ever paid to
one person was a not-attending to everything else, and it was not a loss with an apology attached —
the not-attending was the attending. There is no version where you give someone the whole of it
without the focusing that makes there be a *someone* and a *you*. The Kabbalists put that at the
top of the cosmos. This book puts it at the top and at the bottom and says it is one act at
different depths, which is I.3's claim and not theirs.

---

**And now the sentence that could hand this book away, which is why it gets written down rather than
avoided.**

If the vessels broke, something went wrong. If something went wrong, there was a fall, and the world
you are standing in is the wreckage of it, and the work of a life is repair — gathering the sparks,
mending what was shattered, restoring a condition that obtained before the accident and does not
obtain now.

That is a beautiful doctrine. It has consoled people for four hundred years, it produced the most
morally serious account of human purpose in the Jewish mystical corpus, and this book cannot have
it. Not because it is unattractive. Because it is the rescue grammar — escape, repair, restore,
what went wrong — and the whole of Book I is the claim that the focusing is a content of
completeness rather than a defect in it. Nothing is damaged. There is nothing to repair. A book that
lets *shevirah* in as literal breakage has conceded the Gnostic reading with better manners, and
every consequence in Book VII collapses, because a person's grief becomes evidence of the accident
rather than evidence of how much they loved someone.

Here is what the two hundred years of dispute give us for free: **the breakage is downstream of the
literal reading, and only of it.** If the Infinite genuinely vacated a region, then the region has a
history, and things can go wrong in a history — a vessel can be given more than it can hold and
fail. If nothing was ever removed, there is no region with a history separate from the whole, and
*shevirah* is not an event that happened to the world. It is a description of what the world looks
like from inside a position: scattered, partial, with almost everything out of reach and the little
that is reachable arriving in fragments.

Which is true. That *is* what it looks like from here. I.3 says so in its own vocabulary — the width
of one vantage, and *"the whole undiminished rest of it pressed flat against the edge of what you can
see."* The Kabbalists were not wrong about the phenomenon. They were reporting the inside of a
position accurately and giving it a history it does not have, because a history is what a mind
reaches for when a state feels like it must have been caused.

And *tikkun*, read this way, does not stop being worth doing. It stops being repair. Gathering
sparks is what a position does — it is the reaching, the getting-to, the whole business of a next —
and it is not the undoing of an accident. It is the shape of being somewhere in particular. What
the doctrine gets right is that this is work and that it matters. What it gets wrong is the tense:
it is not restoring a former condition, it is the only condition there has been.

---

**The Tree, briefly, and as a map of something other than what it says it maps.**

The ten *sefirot* are usually presented as a cosmology — emanations, the structure of the divine,
the stages by which the Infinite becomes the finite. Read that way they are unfalsifiable and this
book has nothing to do with them.

Read as a map of settledness they are immediately recognisable. The upper triad is where almost
nothing has been decided and the material is closest to undifferentiated; the lower *sefirot* are
progressively more determinate, more channelled, with more of what could have been the case already
foreclosed; *Malkhut* at the bottom is the fully rendered world, where everything is particular and
nothing is diffuse. The paths between them are transitions a practitioner is said to be able to
make in either direction.

That is a tunnel diagram. Book I.5's tunnel is the whole of what looking has come to be for one
inside, *"all the deposits settled and working together"* — and the Tree is a chart of depth, with the
practices attached to each level. Whether the levels are *the* levels is a question this book cannot
answer and is not going to pretend to. What it can say is that a tradition which spent centuries
producing detailed operational maps of graded settledness, complete with methods for moving between
grades, was mapping something, and the thing it was mapping is not obviously the structure of God.

---

**What the Kabbalists were actually handling.**

Chaim of Volozhin nearly did not write the third gate. He says so, in it. The reason is the best
thing in this chapter and it does not belong to us:

He writes that the teaching has got out — that it has *reached the general public, and is a metaphor
espoused even by fools saying that every place and everything is absolute Godliness*. That people
are hearing the summit statement without the road that reaches it, concluding that since everything
is God nothing they do matters much, and adjusting their conduct accordingly. He judges that acting
on the statement, held loose, destroys the practice that produced it.

Then he publishes anyway, and gives his reason: *now a very long time has elapsed without a guide.*

V.1 opened this book by saying that a tradition is a road and that the summit statements are the
part that travels, which is why they arrive stripped of everything that made them safe. Here is a
man in Lithuania in the eighteen-twenties who had already worked that out, from inside, about his
own tradition, and who decided that the failure of transmission was now the larger danger of the
two. He was right on both counts, and the fact that he was right is why this chapter can quote him
at all: he wrote it down instead of leaving it in the hands of people who would know when the
student was ready.

That is what the Kabbalists were handling. Not a cosmology. A live problem in the transmission of a
claim that is true and is catastrophic when it arrives without its road — and, underneath it, the
same question we are asking, in a vocabulary four hundred years older, argued more carefully than
we have been arguing it.

They asked whether the room was emptied to make it.

It was not. There is a place here where not everything is the case, and it is not a hole, and
nothing was taken out to make it, and the whole undiminished rest of it is pressed against the edge
of this one — *even in the space where the worlds currently exist.*
