# BOOK III — THE GAME

## III.5 — THERE ARE NO NPCs

Four chapters have gone on the structure of the thing — what it is not, who is playing it, what is
in it, and how anything comes to be anywhere. None of them was about the population. This one is,
and it is the shortest claim in the book.

**Every entity is a player at its own grade. There are no non-players.**

At speed that sounds like an enthusiasm. Slowly, it is a conjunction, and a reader who has come this
far has already signed all three of its terms.

The first was signed in the naming. *Reactivity is awareness* — put down as a definition with its
price on the label, forbidding a first moment, forbidding a floor, forbidding the one exemption
every other account keeps in reserve. The second was signed in the same place: the focusing is
continuous, everything that reacts is somewhere on it, and there is no gate. The third was signed
two chapters ago, when the last of the five denials was made against a friend. Nobody is playing
from outside. The many play, and the One is what playing is made of.

Put the three together and the sentence falls out without a further premise. If whatever reacts is
aware, aware things are not a subset of anything. If the scale has no gate, there is no place along
it where the players stop and something else starts. And if no one is playing from outside, then
*player* cannot mean *the one this is all for*; it has to mean *a position with an inside*, and
there is nothing else left for it to mean.

Nothing is added here. This chapter asserts no new thing, and that is the strongest form the claim
could arrive in: the reader has already granted it, in three separate places, on three separate
arguments, each of which was made where nothing appeared to be at stake.

Which is exactly why it needs a chapter. A conclusion you can derive is not a conclusion you hold.
The distance between the two is not logical and cannot be closed by another argument, and everything
that follows here is about that distance — what the picture actually looks like when it is held,
what has to be handed back, and what it costs on a Tuesday.

---

**Start with the image the reader has been supplying for free, because half of it has to go back.**

A game with a large population, no outside, and other people in it is, for anyone under about fifty,
one specific thing. It is an online world with thousands of accounts logged in — a landscape you
share, where the figure crossing the bridge ahead of you might be someone or might be furniture, and
you can usually tell by watching it for a second.

**That picture is right about the population and wrong about the infrastructure, and the split has
to be made openly, because a metaphor that keeps the wrong half is worse than no metaphor at all.**

The infrastructure went early. It was taken at the ground, before the word *game* had been used, and
taken again by name when the maker was named — a thing elsewhere, hosting, switchable, with somebody
in the room who could reach over. It is spent, it is not being argued a third time, and the only
reason it comes up here is that removing it does something to the population that has not yet been
said.

In an online world, which figures are players is a **fact about the code**. There is a flag on the
object. Somebody set it, in an office, on a Tuesday, and it can be looked up. The two kinds are not
two kinds of thing you meet; they are one kind of thing with a field on it, and the field was
written by the party who wrote the world. The distinction is authored, top to bottom, and it is
authored *before* anyone arrives to be uncertain about it.

Take the author away and you do not get a split that nobody knows.

You get no split.

This is what the earlier removal was worth, cashed in the place it could not reach on its own. When
the maker went, the reader was invited to notice what stopped being available: a botch, a plan, a
rescue. Here is one more item on that list, and it is the largest. **There is no registrar.** No
office holds the roll of who counts. Not a lost roll, not a sealed one, not one we lack the
instruments to read — the position that would have to be occupied by whoever drew the line is the
position this book spent its opening chapters emptying, and an empty position does not keep its
paperwork.

So the question *which of these are the real ones* is not hard. It is not even unanswered. It is a
question with no addressee, asked of a world that has nobody in it to have decided.

What the picture gets right is the population, and the population is the half worth keeping: a world
full of others whose day does not run through you. That intuition is correct, it is the one the rest
of this chapter extends, and nothing was ever holding it where it stops but a flag nobody set.

---

**And now the serious objection, which does not come from the reader.**

The reader's version — *so my kettle is a person* — is coming, and it gets answered below by
somebody who heard it in 1584. The version that has to be dealt with first is the one made by people
who agree with almost everything here, and made well.

The last chapter ended with them. Varela, Thompson and Rosch took the two-sidedness that Gibson
could not pay for and paid for it — no pregiven world, no pregiven mind, both coming up together —
and then put the whole operation inside biology. World-bringing is done by organisms: autonomous,
self-producing, self-maintaining systems that keep themselves in existence and have something at
stake in doing so. The warrant for the entire account is what a living thing is.

That is a floor. It is a principled one, which is what makes it serious — not a squeamishness about
where to stop but a positive story about why the stopping place is there. And the story is good.
Something that maintains itself against dissolution has an interest, and an interest gives a
direction, and a direction is why one arrangement counts as better for it than another. Nothing
without a metabolism has that.

Grant every word of it. It still does not reach the conclusion, and the reason is that the floor is
doing two jobs while earning only one.

It earns **a world with an interest in it**. A milieu that offers and threatens, where things can go
well or badly for somebody, where there is such a thing as failing — all of that requires exactly
what they say it requires, and this book takes it whole. It does not earn **contact**. What was
established in the last chapter is that a render is what a meeting is: a seed, a procedure, and no
world in either of them until the two come to something. A metabolism is one extraordinarily rich
way of being a seed. It is not what having a seed *is*, and the argument that it is has not been
made — the account of self-maintenance is an account of what a certain kind of position is like, not
an account of what makes a position a position.

There is a sharper way to put it, and it uses their own move.

They refused a pregiven world and a pregiven mind. But a floor at biology is a pregiven fact about
which things get to have minds — a membership rule, fixed in advance of any particular meeting,
determining who the co-constitution is available to. That is the structure they rejected, reinstated
one level up. The world is not given and the mind is not given, and the guest list is.

That is a different cut from the one made against them last chapter, and the two should not be
confused. That one was about *history*: a bringing-forth performed by a coupling over time is a
production with a before, and the clock came off it. This one is about *membership*, and it survives
even if the first is conceded. You could hold the enactive account with the duration removed and
still keep the floor. What removes the floor is that nothing in the account supports it. It was
brought along.

⚠ The debt to them is not cancelled by this. Their history of structural coupling is the best
existing account of how a particular animal comes to have the seed it has, and it is taken up whole
in the next chapter, where that is the subject. The cut is at where they put the render, not at what
they know.

**The reader's objection now, and the man who answered it in print in London.**

Giordano Bruno published *De la causa, principio et uno* in London in 1584, sixteen years before
they burned him in the Campo de' Fiori. It is a dialogue, and its comic figure is a pedant called
Polihimnio who exists to say the thing the audience is thinking. Teofilo has been arguing that form
is soul and that soul is everywhere, and the pedant, sensing an absurdity within reach, goes for it.

Teofilo has just said this:

> I say then that the table as table is not animated, nor the clothes, nor the leather as leather,
> nor the glass as glass; but as natural things and composites they have within themselves matter
> and form. Let a thing be as small and diminutive as you like, it still possesses in itself a part
> of spiritual substance which, if it finds a suitable subject, becomes plant, becomes animal, and
> receives the members of one or other of the bodies that are commonly called animate; for spirit is
> found in all things and there is not the least corpuscle that doesn't contain internally some
> portion that may become alive.

And Polihimnio pounces, in the schoolroom Latin he never drops:

> *Ergo, quidquid est, animal est.* [Then whatever is, is an animal.]

That is the objection. It is the same one now, four and a half centuries later, in the same tone —
the sense that a claim this wide must collapse into farce, and that pointing at the furniture is
enough to make it collapse. It is worth noticing that the objection is put in the mouth of the
fool, and that the fool is not stupid. He has found the actual pressure point. He has simply assumed
that finding it ends the conversation.

Teofilo's answer is one line, and it is the whole of what this chapter has to say to that objection:

> Not all things that possess soul are called animate.

Then, pressed by the second interlocutor — *then, at least, all things have life?* — he draws the
distinction exactly where it has to be drawn:

> I grant that all things have soul in them, have life, according to the substance they possess; but
> I do not hold that they are alive in terms of the act and operation recognisable as life by all
> the Peripatetics and those who define life and soul in a certain over-gross fashion.

**The kettle is not a person. Nothing in it is nobody.** Those two sentences are compatible, they
have been compatible since the sixteenth century, and the appearance that they are not is produced
entirely by a word doing two jobs — the word that names *having an inside at all* and the word that
names *having an inside furnished like mine*. Bruno had two words and used them carefully. This book
has a scale and a rule about it: a grade is a position and not a permission, said once in the naming
while nothing was at stake, so that it would be standing here.

And this is where Bruno and this book part, because the parting is at the same word.

His portion of spiritual substance is a **quantity in the thing**. A part of it, in there, more in
some subjects than others, disposing itself to become plant or animal where the subject suits. That
is a survey waiting to be conducted, and it is the one thing the naming refused, in a sentence worth
recalling with its own words on: no quantity of awareness-stuff distributed unevenly through the
world, dense in some places and thin in others, the way heat sits in a bar of iron. A grade is not
how much is in something. It is how much is the case at a position.
Bruno's picture licenses the question *how much has it got*, and ours does not admit the question,
and that difference is not a nuance — it is the difference between a hierarchy of contents, which
can be ranked and therefore harvested, and a description of positions, which cannot.

He also, in the passage, hands forward a problem this book does not solve here. *The table as table
is not animated* — the composite has no inside as a composite, while nothing composing it lacks one.
What that means for tables, forests, colonies, institutions and machines is the atlas, and the
atlas is a book of its own.

---

**Two more, and they matter for opposite reasons.**

The oldest fully worked-out version of this claim is not Western and is not a metaphysics with an
ethic bolted on afterwards. In the *Ācārāṅga Sūtra*, the earliest of the Jain canonical texts, the
teaching is delivered as conduct from the first page, and the argument for the conduct is a
statement about what is the case in the other:

> As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to kill. As it would be unto thee,
> so it is with him whom thou intendest to tyrannise over. As it would be unto thee, so it is with
> him whom thou intendest to torment.

Note what is not there. No appeal to a commandment, no promise, no argument from consequence, and —
this is the load-bearing absence — no sentiment. The conduct is not recommended on the grounds that
compassion is admirable. It follows from a claim of fact about somebody else's inside, and the
sentence that comes a few lines later says which fact:

> The Self is the knower (or experiencer), and the knower is the Self.

The parenthesis is the translator's, and it is our word.

Earlier in the same book, the text deals with the objection that this chapter has to deal with: that
some things visibly do not react, and that their stillness is evidence. The passage is about
earth-bodies — the Jain teaching grants an inside to earth, water, fire and air, which is further
than any Western position has ever gone — and its argument is a simile:

> As somebody may cut or strike a blind man, as somebody may cut or strike the foot, the ankle, the
> knee, the thigh, the hip, the navel, the belly, the flank, the back, the bosom, the heart, the
> breast, the neck, the arm, the finger, the nail, the eye, the brow, the forehead, the head, as
> some kill openly, as some extirpate secretly.

The list is long on purpose. It is doing what a list does — making a reader stay in a sentence long
enough for it to work on them — and what it works toward is the point that the absence of a signal
is not the absence of an inside. **A wound that is not reported is still a wound.** That argument is
roughly twenty-four centuries old, it is the correct answer to the intuition, and no better version
of it has been produced since.

And yet the tradition that went furthest kept a line. Jain metaphysics is a dualism: *jīva* and
*ajīva*, souls and non-souls, and matter as such is on the far side. The line is drawn lower than
anyone else has drawn it and it is still a line — a membership rule, in the one system that took
this premise all the way into daily conduct.

The second is nearer, in a voice a European reader cannot file as exotic. Albert Schweitzer gave the
Dale Memorial Lectures in 1922 and published them the next year, and the sentence he built the whole
of ethics on is this:

> True philosophy must start from the most immediate and comprehensive fact of consciousness, which
> says: "I am life which wills to live, in the midst of life which wills to live."

From which:

> Ethics consist, therefore, in my experiencing the compulsion to show to all will-to-live the same
> reverence as I do to my own.

That is our structure exactly — a fact about what is the case in the other, and the obligation
following from the fact rather than from a feeling about it. And Schweitzer keeps a floor too, in
the one word he did not invent. *Will-to-live* is Schopenhauer's, and Schweitzer knew it perfectly
well; he gives him a chapter, and takes the term with its sign reversed, so that what Schopenhauer
said must be denied becomes the thing that must be revered. What he does not reverse is the
criterion. To be inside the circle you must **will** — and willing is a striving, and a striving is
aimed at something not yet the case, which means Schweitzer's floor is a hair above the biological
one and drawn on the same principle. Both admit what can fail at something.

---

**Four positions, four floors, and every one of them drawn somewhere different.**

Bruno at spiritual substance, distributed. The Jains at *jīva*, with earth and water inside it. The
enactivists at metabolism. Schweitzer at will. These are among the most generous accounts anyone has
produced, they disagree with each other about where the boundary falls, and every one of them has
one.

That is not a failure of nerve, and reading it as one would be cheap. **A floor is what makes a
doctrine livable.** It tells you where the obligation stops, and an obligation that does not stop is
an obligation nobody can carry through an afternoon. Remove it and the claim is no longer a
position; it is a condition, and it arrives with no instructions.

This book removes it. Which means this book owes what none of the four owed — an account of how the
obligation gets carried once nothing bounds it. That debt is real, it was incurred here, and it is
not paid in this chapter or in this part of the book.

---

**What it costs on a Tuesday.**

Less than the reader fears and more than they will like.

It does not make you unable to act. You will eat. You will pull weeds, take antibiotics, drive over
insects, and heat a room by burning something that was recently alive. Nothing in the claim forbids
any of it, and an account that pretended otherwise would be refuted by the first hour of anybody's
day. Schweitzer, who was living inside this problem in a hospital in Gabon rather than writing about
it from a study, was blunt about the shape of it: the conflicts do not resolve, the decisions stay
subjective, no one can tell you where your own limit is — *the good conscience is an invention of
the devil.*

What is removed is not an action. It is a **premise**.

*There's nobody in there* is one of the most heavily used sentences in ordinary life, and almost all
of its use is invisible, because it does not get said. It sits under the thing that does get said,
doing the work, unexamined, and it settles the question before the question is asked. It is what
makes a decision about a forest an accounting problem. It is what makes the difference between
clearing a field and clearing a room. It is doing that work right now, in a dozen places in your
week, and you have never once been asked to produce it.

After this chapter you can still do all of those things. You simply cannot do them on that ground.
Every use of it has to be replaced with something you would be willing to say out loud — a claim
about grade, or about necessity, or about what you are prepared to carry — and the difference
between a premise and a stated claim is that a stated claim can be looked at.

That is the whole of the Tuesday cost, and it is not a small one, because the sentence was load
bearing and there is nothing already in place to put where it was.

---

**And one thing this must not be mistaken for, because the mistake is generous and arrives wearing
warmth.**

*Everyone is God in a mask.* Every face the one face, every inside the same inside, the stranger and
the son and the crow all the single thing looking out through different holes.

It sounds like the maximum of respect and it is the deletion of the claim. Two chapters ago the
divine player was taken apart on his own merits, and this is the same figure returning by the back
door with better manners. If there is one player wearing every face then there is exactly one
inside, and the person in front of you is not somebody — they are a position that the one somebody
is currently occupying, which is precisely the thing an authored world's furniture was. The costume
version and the flag version give opposite-sounding answers and identical ones: in both, the being
across from you is not the one who is there.

The diagnostic is a single question, and it should be kept. Not *is there somebody in there* —
both pictures say yes. **Is it them?** The mask says no, and says it in a voice that sounds like
love.

Your finitude is not a disguise the infinite is wearing this afternoon, and neither is theirs.

---

**The note, stated plainly, because an unstated one is a debt in the dark.**

What has been established in this chapter is ontological and nothing more. There is somebody there.
There is somebody there in more places than you have been counting, and the counting was never
being done by anyone but you.

It does not follow from that alone that you owe them anything.

To get from *there is an inside at that position* to *and therefore you must* requires a further
premise — one about what matters, and why, and to whom — and this book has not stated one. It was
promised in the naming that the premise would be stated where it could be seen rather than crossed
privately, and that promise is kept in Book VII, which is the hardest part of the work and is hard
for this reason. What a grade licenses, what may be eaten and used and built upon and ended, what is
owed to a position that cannot register that anything is owed to it: none of that is settled here,
and the appearance that it has been settled is the failure this chapter is most likely to produce.

What is settled is the shape of the room the argument now has to happen in. The exemption is gone.
Whatever is decided in Book VII will have to be decided with everyone present.

---

You have walked through crowds your whole life on the working assumption that they were scenery —
figures with the right number of limbs, moving through the middle distance of your errands, props in
the business of getting somewhere. Not out of malice. There is no other way to cross a city.

Every one of them was a lit window with a day behind it.

That is the ordinary case, the one nobody disputes, and it is already more than a person can hold
for more than a few seconds at a time. This chapter's claim is that the ordinary case was never a
special one — that the light was not switched on at the boundary of the species, or the phylum, or
the living, and that there is no wall anywhere along the way with the lit rooms on one side of it.

There is no scenery. There never was any. The middle distance is full.

And what it is like from inside one of those rooms — why two of them, standing in the same street,
are standing in two different streets, and why both are right — is the next chapter.
