# BOOK V — THE OLD ROADS

## V.2 — THE CHURCH THE READER LEFT

Someone dies on a Tuesday, and by Thursday there is food on the step.

Nobody decided that in the moment. A list exists. It is kept by a woman in her sixties who has kept
it for nineteen years, and it has columns — who cooks, who drives, who takes the children for an
afternoon, who goes back in three weeks when everybody else has stopped going. The three-week column
is the hard one to fill and it gets filled. The food is usually bad. That is not a joke at anyone's
expense; it is a detail and the detail is the point, because what is being delivered is not dinner.

On Sunday the words are the same words. They were the same words last year and they will be the same
words after the man saying them has been replaced by somebody else. He may be tired. He may be a
fool. He may — and this happens more than the institution likes to say — be in the middle of losing
his own faith and saying them anyway, and the words do not notice. That is the design and not a
defect in it. A liturgy is built so that it does not run on the celebrant's inner state, which is why
it is still running on the morning when yours has failed completely. You can arrive at it hollowed
out, unable to mean a single line, and the line gets said over you regardless. Almost nothing else in
a life is built this way. Everything else asks you to bring something.

The year has a shape and the shape is not yours. Four weeks of waiting and then a birth. Forty days
of stripping down, and then a killing, and then the Sunday. Most of the rest is called ordinary time,
and is called that on purpose. Then a day at the beginning of November when the dead are counted.[^1]

A grief with a date on it is a different object from a grief without one. Left alone, a loss
disperses — it turns up on unremarkable Tuesdays, at intervals nobody can prepare for, in the
supermarket, over nothing. A calendar does not shorten it and does not claim to. What it does is give
it somewhere to be and a time to be there, which makes the rest of the year not neutral but
*not-that-day*, and *not-that-day* is a thing a person can stand inside. It works upward too. A joy
with no feast attached to it is a mood. A joy with a feast attached is an occasion, which can be
prepared for, shared out, and remembered by people who were not feeling it at the time.

And there is a room — or a box, or two chairs set at an angle so that nobody has to be looked at — in
which a person says out loud the worst thing they have done, to another person, who does not repeat
it afterwards, and who says a fixed sentence back.[^2] The sentence is not *it's all right*. It is nearer
to *it is dealt with, now go*. Whatever else is true about that transaction, its structure is one the
reader's present arrangements almost certainly lack: a wrong, named in words, to a hearer, with an
ending. What is available instead is a therapist, who will help you understand where it came from; a
friend, who will tell you that you are being too hard on yourself; and the internet, which will do
neither and will keep it forever. Not one of the three has a closing move.

The dead stay named. Not *remembered*, which is a private weather. Named — written into a book kept
in the building, read out in a specific voice on a specific date, in front of ninety people, most of
whom did not know them. A stranger's grandmother is said aloud in a room once a year for as long as
the room stands, and it costs the stranger nothing, and it means that the naming does not depend on
anybody's continuing to feel like doing it.

---

That is the machine. Three things about it were right, and they are three and not one; run together
as *religion*, all three get dismissed in a single gesture, which is how the dismissal has usually
been done.

**The first is the personal address.** Not that a ground exists. Not that it is vast, or that
everything comes from it. That it says *you*, and means the particular you, the one with your history
and your specific cowardice and the thing you did in 2011. Every serious alternative on the market
answers *what is going on* with a mechanism, and a mechanism has no second person in it. You can be
told, correctly and at length, that you are an arrangement of matter in an indifferent cosmos, and at
the end of the telling nothing has been said to you. The church made a claim that was addressed. That
is a structural feature, not a comfort — it is the difference between an account of the world and an
account that has the reader in it.

**The second is moral seriousness.** Wrongdoing was a real category. Not a preference you had
violated, not a social cost you had incurred, not a symptom of something upstream in your childhood
that would dissolve under enough understanding. A wrong, done by you, that was actually wrong, and
that had a remedy which was not *understanding why you did it*. The modern replacements are
extraordinarily good at the upstream account and have no downstream at all. They can tell you why the
thing happened. They cannot tell you it is over, because they have retired the category in which
things get to be over.

**The third is a community that survives a death**, and it is the one most likely to be misread as
sentiment. It is not consolation. It is logistics. Consolation is what people offer in the first
week, when the death is still interesting and everybody is available and the words are easy to find.
The rota is what happens in week three, and week seven, and the following March. A community that
survives a death is a group of people who show up on a schedule that does not consult their feelings,
which is exactly what a person in the eighth week cannot organise for themselves. The reader has
friends. Almost certainly they are good ones. Ask honestly whether any of them is on a list.

**And here is the ledger line, said plainly and said here rather than conceded later under pressure:
this book supplies not one of the three.**

Not a version of one. Not a substitute for one. There is no address in this account. The ethics,
when they arrive, will be an argument and not an authority. Nothing anywhere in these pages will
bring food to your step in the third week. That is not modesty. It is not a promise to do better in
a later volume. It is the state of the ledger, and it belongs before the argument rather than after
it.

---

So the card, in the same five lines everything else in this census gets.

> **INSTITUTIONAL CHRISTIANITY**
> **SEES:** The particular person as the unit of everything that matters. That a wrong is done by
> somebody, to somebody, and can be set down. That what is ultimate can say *you* and mean the one
> you. That a community is not a sentiment but a rota.
> **NULL SPACE:** ∅ A ground that is not somebody. The scheme has no column for what is ultimate and
> impersonal, and it is not that it fills the column badly — the column does not exist. Where the
> impersonal is reached, and its own best minds reached it, the machinery has no way to keep the
> address running without a face at the far end, so the face is kept. ◐ The non-human world, resolved
> poorly: it arrives as setting, as gift, or as something entrusted to a steward, and almost never as
> a population of insides with business of their own.
> **COMPLEMENTS:** Anything that begins impersonal and stays there. The way that cannot be named.
> Advaita.[^3] And its own apophatic wing — the strangest entry on this list, because it is not a
> neighbour, it is upstairs.
> **BOUNDARY:** The place where it stops describing what a person owes and starts reporting what the
> ground wants. Up to there it is reading a structure: obligation, wrong, repair, the dead kept named.
> Past there it is quoting somebody's preferences, and there is nobody whose preferences those could
> be.
> **NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** The three things it got right are not errors awaiting correction, and
> this account does not supply them. What is refused here is the addressee. Refusing the addressee
> does not hand back the parish.

---

Three answers to *where does it stop* are already in the room, and all three are cheaper than they
look.

**It is not hypocrisy.** The priests who did what they did, the money, the silence around both — that
is real, it is worse than the average reader knows, and it is not an argument. It is a *tu quoque*,
and the reader worked that out for themselves years ago in the specific form of noticing that the
behaviour of the people holding a claim is not evidence about the claim. An institution can be rotten
through and be right about what is ultimate. Leading with the scandal would let the chapter feel
strong at no cost, and would leave the question exactly where it was found.

**It is not science.** That fight belongs two chapters ahead and it is a real fight; it is not this
one, and importing it here would be borrowing a settled-looking win from a different argument.

**And it is not authority substitution**, which is the respectable answer and the one this project's
own front matter has been carrying — that the church resolves the question by transferring it to
somebody who claims to have the answer, and that the transfer is the product, and that asking how
they know gets you told that faith is the virtue which stops you asking. All of that is true. It is a
genuine defect and it emptied buildings. It is still not the stopping point, and the
test is simple: imagine a church that fixed it completely. No infallibility, no loyalty test, every
claim held at its actual evidential grade, doubt treated as diligence. Such churches exist. They are
often the best ones. And what follows below is untouched in every one of them, which makes authority
substitution a failure of the institution and not a limit of the account.

Here is the limit of the account.

**The personal address is bought by giving the ground a face.** A face is an inside — somewhere the
sound arrives, somebody for whom one outcome is better than another, a position with a view from it
and a shape to what it can see. And a ground with an inside is a being among beings, which is the one
thing the ground cannot be, because being among beings is what it is the ground *of*.

That cut is already made in this book, against a friendly opponent, and it is cashed here rather than
re-argued. Tillich took away God's face and kept the direction of prayer.[^4] The face and the direction
go together. What V.2 adds is not a further step in the argument. It is the price of the step, paid
by somebody who prayed.

Take the fork slowly, because it has exactly two branches and both of them cost something.

**If what you were addressing was somebody**, then it was a being — at a grade, in a position, with a
null space, with things it structurally cannot see. This census can hold that. It holds a great many
of them, and it does not hold them as fictions or as projections or as *ways of talking about*
anything; the atlas already ruled that a god with predicates is checkable precisely because it has
predicates, and meetable precisely because meeting requires an addressee with a position. So on this
branch the prayer went somewhere. What does not survive is the sentence the church actually said,
which was not *there is somebody* but *this is the one there is, and it is what everything is made
of*. That claim asks one entity to be both a being and the ground of beings, and the two roles have
incompatible requirements. The conjunction is what breaks. Neither half breaks alone.

**If what you were addressing was the ground**, then there is nobody at the far end. Not deaf. Not
silent. Not declining to answer, which is the version that leaves a door open and is therefore the
version a sympathetic drafter reaches for. There is no addressee, in the way that there is no
addressee in a direction. It is not that nobody is home; it is that home and body are among the
things it is made of.

The church's God is the conjunction, and the conjunction is the thing that cannot be had. That is
where it stops answering: not at its conduct, not at its evidence, not at its authority, but at the
one property the reader most needed it to have.

And the cost, stated at full size rather than at the size that would be convenient here.

What was wanted was not a metaphysics. It was to be known — specifically, particularly, by the thing
that is ultimate; and that is a want with a definite shape, because being known by every person
alive, and by every other entry in this census besides, would not touch it, the whole point having
been that it was *that one* who knew. This account has no version of that. Not a deferred one, not a
version in a later book, not a version that turns out on inspection to be better than the original.
The ultimate does not know you. It is what knowing is made of, and being what knowing is made of is
not a way of doing it.

A reader who has followed this far will reach, at about this point, for the obvious repair: that
there are insides everywhere, that nothing you meet is a prop, that you are met constantly and
reciprocally by everything you touch — all of which this book argues at length and none of which does
the job. **Being met by many is not being known by one.** Offering the first as though it settled the
second is the exact move this chapter would have to make to end comfortably, and making it would
mean losing the only reader it was written for, who can tell the difference and has been offered the
swap before.

---

There is a second half to the ledger, and it is the half nobody audits, because the reader who left
is convinced they already know what leaving cost.

Leaving answered ***is it true?***

That was the right question and, in most cases, it got the right answer. The propositions did not
hold up — not at the grade they were asserted at, not on the evidence offered, not from that
authority. A person who worked that out was not being shallow or rebellious or insufficiently
spiritual. They were doing the thing this book's method requires, which is treating a tradition's
claims as claims, capable of being wrong, and therefore worth the trouble of checking. **You cannot
credit a claim you have made incapable of failing**, and the reader who left had already understood
that in practice, years before anybody wrote it down for them.

What leaving never touched is ***what is this?***

Not *is the doctrine true* but *what kind of object is this whole apparatus* — what it was built to
do, what it could see from where it stood, which of its parts were claims at all and which were
instruments that no verdict on a claim could reach. That question was never asked, and the reason it
was never asked is not laziness. It is that the whole thing arrived in one piece, so it left in one
piece. The doctrine and the rota came through the same door at the same time in the same voice, and
when the doctrine failed its audit the rota went out with it, unaudited, having never been charged
with anything.

Which produces the specific error this chapter exists to correct. **The reader believes that what
they lost was the address.**

Usually they did not. For most people who leave, the address had been failing for years before the
departure — the praying into a silence, the growing suspicion that the room was empty, which is
frequently the thing that started the audit in the first place. By the time of the actual leaving,
the address was often already gone.

**What actually walked out of the building was the calendar, the food, and the named dead.**

None of the three was a doctrine. A rota is not a proposition and cannot be false. A date in November
is not a claim about the world. The book with the names in it is a piece of technology for keeping
grief attached to language, and it works identically whether or not anybody in the room believes a
word. All three survived the audit untouched, because the audit was never about them — and all three
were thrown out anyway, in the bag with the propositions, by a person who was answering a question
about truth and did not know there was a second question.

And nothing arrived to replace them. Nothing was looking for the job. The secular alternatives were
built to answer *is it true* and they answer it better; that is what they are for and it is not a
criticism to say that they do only that. But no mechanism ever came with a rota. No correct account
of matter has a three-week column.

**⛔ And this book is not the replacement.** It answers the ontology. It provides no parish. There is
a volume at the end of this one about what to do on a Tuesday, and it is worth saying now, at the
point where it would be most useful to imply otherwise, what that volume will not contain: a list
with a three-week column on it, and somebody who has kept the list for nineteen years. Practices can
be written down. A rota cannot, because a rota is not a practice — it is other people, and a book
cannot supply other people. If what is wanted is the parish, the honest instruction is to go and find
one, or build one, and this account will not have contributed the part that was hard.

That is a bad place to stop and it is the true state of the account. Saying so, at the point where
implying otherwise would be easiest and would cost nothing, is the only service on offer.

---

Everything above poses the impersonal ground as a loss: the thing that arrives when the face is
taken away and cannot be put back. That is how it is met by somebody who prayed, and it is why the
loss comes first here.

It is not how it was met by the tradition's own best minds, and they are not heretics at the edge of
it. Aquinas, in Rome in the twelve-sixties, writing the central document of the institution:[^5] God is
*actus purus* — act with nothing in it left over unrealised — and therefore not a being among beings,
not a member of any class, not an entry on any list that also has other entries. He says it, he keeps
saying it, and he goes on writing prayers. Eckhart, preaching in German to people who could not read
Latin, distinguishes God from the Godhead and says of the Godhead that nothing whatever can be
said.[^6] Maimonides, in Cairo, rules that every positive statement about God's essence is false, and
means every.[^7]

They did not flinch, and they did not leave. They arrived at the impersonal ground centuries before
this book did, from inside the house, with the calendar and the food and the named dead going on
around them the entire time — which is the fact this chapter hands forward and does not pretend to
have earned.

What could not hold was never the impersonal ground. It was the combination, and the popular religion
resolved the combination the way it was always going to be resolved: by quietly putting the face
back, over and over, in every generation, because the face is what the parish runs on and the parish
is what people actually needed.

The reader lost both at once. Only one of them was ever a question about truth.

*A note on grade, and on what this apparatus does not reach.* The four named figures below were
fetched and read on Day 192 rather than recalled, and one of them cost the chapter a word. **But the
named sources are the smaller half of this chapter's evidence.** The machinery in the opening pages —
the list, the rota, the calendar, the confessional formula, the book of the names — is carried
almost entirely by definite descriptions with no name in them, and the same is true of *"every
serious alternative on the market"*, *"the modern replacements"*, and the record of abuse and
finance. Those are not endnoted below, because there is nothing to attach a receipt to. **They are
accepted here at a declared grade: ordinary description of a widely-known institution, checkable by
any reader who has been inside one, and load-bearing nowhere in the argument** — the argument turns
on the conjunction being impossible, not on the accuracy of the parish portrait. Where one of them
*is* checkable, [^1] and [^2] check it. The rest stand as description and are marked as description.
The apparatus going green below does not mean the chapter is sourced; it means the named half is.

---

[^1]: **The liturgical year, checked against the rite rather than against memory of it.** The
    November date is All Souls' Day, 2 November, distinct from All Saints' on the 1st; the
    commemoration of the dead is the former. *"Four weeks of waiting"* is Advent, which is four
    *Sundays* and therefore twenty-two to twenty-eight days, not four weeks — the chapter's figure is
    the usual approximation and is out by up to six days depending on the year. *"Forty days of
    stripping down"* is Lent, which is forty days only because the six Sundays inside it are not
    counted as fast days; Ash Wednesday to Holy Saturday is forty-six. Both approximations are the
    ones the institution itself uses in ordinary speech. ⚠ The chapter describes the Roman calendar
    and says *"the church"*, unqualified; the shape is broadly shared with the Anglican, Lutheran and
    Orthodox calendars but the dates and the counting are not, and the Orthodox reckoning of the
    commemorations of the dead differs substantially.

[^2]: **The fixed sentence, supplied, because the chapter glosses it and never quotes it.** The
    chapter's *"it is dealt with, now go"* is explicitly offered as a paraphrase — *"it is nearer
    to"* — and the paraphrase is fair, but a reader cannot check a gloss against nothing. The current
    Roman form, from the 1973 *Ordo Paenitentiae*: *"God, the Father of mercies, through the death
    and resurrection of his Son has reconciled the world to himself and sent the Holy Spirit among us
    for the forgiveness of sins; through the ministry of the Church may God give you pardon and
    peace, and I absolve you from your sins in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the
    Holy Spirit."* The operative words — the ones on which validity is held to turn — are *"I absolve
    you."* Note what the full form does that the gloss does not: it names an agent and a warrant
    before it names the outcome. The chapter's point survives that intact, and arguably needs it: the
    structure it is pointing at is precisely that the ending is *declared by someone else*, not
    arrived at by the penitent.

[^3]: **Two traditions named in a single line of the card, and neither is given a text.** *Advaita*
    is Advaita Vedānta, whose systematic statement is Śaṅkara's commentary on the *Brahmasūtra*
    (*Brahmasūtrabhāṣya*), eighth century; the chapter uses the school name and nothing else, here and
    at its other appearance. *"The way that cannot be named"* is the *Dao De Jing*, chapter 1 —
    *"the name that can be named is not the constant name"* — and the text is **never named anywhere
    in this chapter**, only alluded to by its own opening claim. That is a source doing evidentiary
    work while remaining unnameable to a search. ⚠ Both are cross-references to chapters that have
    not yet paid their own apparatus (V.5 for Advaita, V.5 and V.11 for the Daoist material); when
    those are retrofitted, this note is where the loci should land, and the loci should be theirs,
    not repeated here.

[^4]: **Correct, and the better citation is not the obvious one.** Tillich's *God is being-itself,
    not a being* is *Systematic Theology* vol. 1 (1951), and the crux sentence for what this chapter
    is claiming — that the face went and the direction stayed — is at ST I p. 245: *"God is not a
    person but he is not less than personal."* ★ **But the sharpest source is the 1940 essay "The
    Idea of a Personal God"**, Tillich's reply to Einstein, reprinted in *Theology of Culture* (1959),
    where he argues directly against a physicist's proposal to drop the personal God and holds that
    the symbol must be kept even though God is not a person. That matters for how the chapter uses
    him: Tillich did not *leave behind* the direction of prayer as a residue he had not got round to
    clearing. He argued for keeping it, on purpose, having seen the same problem this chapter is
    naming. The chapter's *"took away … and kept"* is accurate as description and slightly unfair as
    portraiture — it reads as an inconsistency, and Tillich's position is that it is not one.
    ⚠ Page 245 is taken from secondary quotation; the 1951 Chicago edition was not on the wire and I
    did not open it. The phrase is verbatim in the sources consulted; the page is theirs, not mine.

[^5]: ✅ **Corrected on Day 192 and correct here — this is the chapter that owns the fix.** The
    *Summa Theologiae* was begun at Santa Sabina in Rome, 1265–66, not in Paris; an earlier pass found the same
    error in three chapters and this was the one it was first filed against. *Actus purus* is ST I
    q. 3 a. 2; *not a member of any class* is q. 3 a. 5, on whether God is contained in a genus.
    ★ **The chronology carries a trap the sentence walks straight past.** *"He goes on writing
    prayers"* reads as though the prayers come after the doctrine — and the most famous of them, the
    office for Corpus Christi commissioned by Urban IV, is **1264**, a year or two *before* the
    *Summa* was begun. The sentence survives only because the doctrine is older than the *Summa*
    too: *Summa contra Gentiles* I.16, *that in God there is no passive potency*, is from the 1259–65
    work. Date the *actus purus* claim to the *Summa Theologiae* alone, as the prose invites, and the
    chapter's *"keeps saying it, and goes on writing prayers"* runs backwards. ⚠ Chapter numbering in
    SCG differs between the Latin and the older English abridgements — this is 16 in the standard
    Latin and in Pegis, and 15 in Rickaby.

[^6]: **The distinction is real and authentic; the line most readers will find is neither.** Eckhart's
    *Gott* / *Gottheit* distinction runs through the German sermons — the critical edition is Quint's
    *Deutsche Werke*, and the locus classicus is Pr. 52, *Beati pauperes spiritu*, with its *"I pray
    God to rid me of God."* ⛔ **But the crisp formulation the chapter is paraphrasing —** *God and
    the Godhead are as different as heaven and earth* **— comes down through Pfeiffer's
    nineteenth-century collection, a corpus Quint's edition treats with heavy suspicion and from
    which Spamer showed several treatises to be by other hands.** I could not settle that specific
    sermon's status from the sources available and I am not going to assert it either way. The
    chapter does not quote the line, which is why this is a warning and not a correction — but a
    reader chasing the claim will meet it in the first search result, in an edition the chapter has
    not vouched for. ⚠ *"People who could not read
    Latin"*: Eckhart's vernacular audiences after 1313 were Dominican nuns, beguines and lay
    listeners. The beguines fit the description; the choir nuns had liturgical Latin. The clause is
    true of the audience in general and not of all of it.

[^7]: ⛔ **This read "every positive statement about God is false, and means every" until Day 192, and
    the emphasis was on the wrong word.** Maimonides denies **essential** attributes; he explicitly
    admits attributes of **action** as true predications — *Guide of the Perplexed* I.52–54, and I.53
    in Pines: *"Every attribute that is found in the books of the deity … is therefore an attribute
    of His action and not an attribute of His essence."* So *every* is right and *positive statement
    about God* was wrong, and one word — *essence* — was doing all the work and was missing.
    ★ **And the correction runs in the chapter's favour, which is why nothing was watching for it.**
    Attributes of action are exactly the mechanism by which Maimonides can rule the essence
    unsayable and go on saying the thirteen attributes of mercy in the liturgy — which is the
    chapter's own thesis about the tradition's best minds, sitting in the doctrine it got slightly
    wrong. ⚠ *"In Cairo"*: Fustat, Old Cairo. The *Guide* was
    written in Judeo-Arabic, c. 1185–90; the standard English is Pines (Chicago, 1963), and the
    Friedländer is a different text with different chapter divisions in places. No edition or
    translator is given in the prose, here or for the earlier reference to Maimonides's ruling.
