# BOOK II — THE NAMING

## II.7 — THE COLLAPSE

This is the word this book cannot afford, and is going to spend anyway.

Every other borrowing so far has been from a philosopher, a mystic, a poet, or ordinary speech, and
the worst a bad one could do was confuse somebody. This one is different. *Collapse* is owned, in
the reader's mind and in most of the last century's, by physics — by the thing that is supposed to
happen to a particle when somebody looks at it. The people who own it have equipment. And the
reader has met this exact borrowing before, on the covers of books with *quantum* in the title, and
it was a con, and they were right to stop reading.

So the order of what follows is itself the argument. Measurement gets defined first, with no physics
in it at all — and if the definition needs the physics to stand up, it will be visible immediately,
because there will be nothing holding it. Only then does the subatomic case arrive, as an instance
and not as a source, and get handled honestly, including the part nobody selling the paperbacks
mentions: the one serious physicist who ever held the view they are selling withdrew it, in print,
and said his own earlier position should be criticised as solipsism.

Then the ban. This chapter is the one place in the book with standing to state it, because by then
it will have paid for it.

---

**Something is open when more than one way it could go is still live.**

That is not the same as *not yet known*. The contents of a sealed envelope are not open; they are
settled and hidden, and opening it changes you and not the envelope. Openness is the matter itself
not having gone one way yet — the outcome not sitting somewhere finished, waiting to be uncovered.

Whether anything is *really* open, independently of everybody, is a question this book declines,
and declines on principle rather than out of nerves. A fact settled for no perspective whatever is a
fact for nobody, and there is no position from which such a thing could be checked, including this
one. What can be said, and it is enough to build on, is that matters are open *for* perspectives,
and that they close.

**A measurement is a contact that closes one.** Three things have to be true of it, and each rules
out a counterfeit the reader will recognise from their own life.

**It has to be contact.** Something not already inside you has to reach you. This is the condition
that disqualifies most of what people call finding out. The second opinion sought from the friend
whose answer was predictable before the question finished. The test designed so that it cannot come
out wrong. The question asked in the tone that supplies its own reply. None of these are
measurements. They are echoes, and an echo can be repeated indefinitely without one thing being
decided, which is exactly why it is so restful and why people do so much of it.

**It could have come out otherwise.** If only one result was available, nothing was closed, because
nothing was open. This is the condition that makes a measurement expensive: to take one, you have to
put something at risk that you were not already sure of. It is also why a system cannot certify
itself. Certification asks a question whose answer was fixed by whoever wrote the question.

**It has to land.** The contact has to change what is now the case for the one who took it. A
result received and set aside is not a measurement; it is a rumour. This is the condition most often
failed by people who are, in every other respect, being scrupulous — they go and look, honestly, and
then carry on as before, and afterwards believe they have checked.

There is one exception to the first condition and it matters more than the rule. The thing that
reaches you does not have to come from outside *you*. It has to come from outside the part of you
that is asking. A body's hunger is news to the one making plans. A grief that arrives on a Tuesday
for no reason is one part of a person telling another part something it did not have. The levels of
a thing are genuinely distinct — that was the first of the four conditions, and it is what makes
this possible — so one of them can be a real other to another, and the contact between them is a
real measurement with a real outcome. What is *not* possible is the whole of you consulting the
whole of you. That is the closed loop, and it is the most comfortable room in the world.

---

**The possible does not become actual. It becomes actual for somebody.**

Aristotle owns this ground and should be paid for it before anything is taken. Potentiality and
actuality — *dynamis* and *energeia* — are his, and the half he got right is the half most moderns
get wrong: that possibility is a real feature of the world and not merely a name for our ignorance.
The acorn's oak is not a fact about what we have failed to find out. It is a fact about the acorn.
An age that hears *possible* and reaches straight for *unknown* has lost something he had.

And in one place he had the whole of it. In the psychology, not the metaphysics: the activity of the
sensible object and the activity of the percipient sense, he says, are one and the same activity,
though what it is to be each of them stays distinct. The sounding of the thing and the hearing of
the animal are not two events that meet. They are one event described from its two ends. That is a
measurement in this book's sense, exactly, stated twenty-three centuries early.

**The cut is that he never let it out of the room.** In the metaphysics, actualisation is a thing's
own form arriving — the potential is a power *in* the acorn, and the oak is what happens when it
finishes. There is no second party anywhere in that account, and no *for whom*. The oak is actual
whether or not anything has ever met it. He had the two-sided structure in his hand while writing
about perception and did not carry it back, and so the doctrine that governs everything else in his
system is the one-sided one, where the world unfolds its own possibilities on its own and being met
is incidental.

Here it is not incidental. It is the mechanism. Nothing goes from open to settled on its own,
because *settled* is not a property a thing can have by itself — it is a relation between a matter
and a place where that matter has now come out one way. Every perspective has a grade, and so every
collapse has one: what settles when a photon meets an electron settles at that grade, and it is a
genuine settling, and nobody is watching.

Which forces a consequence people balk at. The same matter can be closed for one perspective and
still open for another, and neither is mistaken. This is not the polite relativism the word
*perspective* keeps trying to smuggle in — there is no suggestion here that they merely see it
differently, or that it is a matter of taste which is right. The settling is an event, events happen
somewhere, and these two are somewhere else from each other. What has actually happened is a fact.
*Where* it has happened is also a fact, and it is the one that keeps getting dropped.

---

**Now the physics, and it arrives fourth on purpose.**

Von Neumann, in 1932, proved something that is still the honest starting point: the mathematics
permits the collapse to be placed anywhere along the chain. Particle to apparatus, apparatus to
photographic plate, plate to retina, retina to whatever comes after — the formalism gives the same
predictions wherever the cut is made, and offers no way to find where it truly goes. He drew no
conclusion about minds. He reported that the location was unfixed.

That result is usually presented as an embarrassment. Read it once more with this chapter's claim in
hand and it stops being one. A cut whose position the equations cannot fix is precisely what you
would expect if the position were set by something the equations do not mention — namely *whose*
settling is being asked about. The formalism is silent about the location of the cut in the same way
a map is silent about who is holding it.

Wigner, in 1961, took the unfixed cut and fixed it: consciousness. Where the chain reaches an aware
being, he proposed, the possibilities become one outcome, and the awareness is what does it. Credit
where it is owed, because he saw the real problem — the chain has to stop somewhere and the stopping
is not in the mathematics. **The cut is that he made the perspective a cause.** A special power,
reaching into physics from outside it and forcing a result. This account makes the perspective a
*place*, which is the opposite kind of thing. His version needs mind to be extraordinary; this one
needs it to be utterly ordinary, one grade among all the others, doing nothing that a photon does
not also do.

And his version failed, on the record, by his own hand. In a 1982 lecture he said his earlier view
should be criticised as solipsism. In 1984 he wrote that he had been argued out of it by Dieter
Zeh's 1970 work on decoherence — the demonstration that a system with several live outcomes stops
having them by leaking into its surroundings, continuously, everywhere, with nobody watching and
nothing aware anywhere near it.

**That is the whole shape of the objection this chapter exists to kill.** *It's all just quantum* is
not too speculative. It is too old. The one distinguished physicist who ever held the position was
convinced out of it more than forty years ago, in public, for stated reasons, and said the thing he
had held was close to solipsism — and the marketing layer is still selling the 1961 version to
people who have never been told there was a 1984.

Two pieces of honesty are owed here, and they cost nothing. First, decoherence does not close the
measurement problem; it explains why nobody ever sees the smear, and there is live, serious
disagreement about whether it explains why exactly one outcome is the one that happened. That
argument is open and this book is not going to pretend to settle it. Second, and more important:
none of the above is evidence for anything claimed here. The claim of this chapter is that the
settling of the open is one structure wherever it happens, and the subatomic case is its most
studied instance rather than its source. That is a claim, offered as one. If it were withdrawn
tomorrow, everything else in this book would stand exactly where it is, because none of it was ever
resting there. Anyone who needs the physics to be true for their metaphysics to work has already
told you their metaphysics does not work.

---

**The ban, and the one word it costs us.**

A short list of words does not appear in this book, and the reason is a single sentence: **what is
banned is a vague word, never a bold claim.** Nothing on the list was excluded for being too
strange, too mystical, or too far from respectable opinion; this book is going to say things far
stranger than any of them. They are excluded for being imprecise in a way that *sounds* precise —
for arriving with the tone of a technical term and the content of a mood. The roster itself, with
each retirement's one-line reason, belongs to the next chapter, where it can be read as the closed
inventory it is.

What belongs here is the test, applied once, to the word that costs us something.

*Collapse* is kept. *Superposition* is refused. That looks arbitrary — both are physics'
vocabulary, both were reached for by this account because they were vivid — and it is not.

A collapse is a thing the reader has already seen. A lung, a bridge, a market, a negotiation, a
folding chair. The word already means: a structure that was holding itself open stops, all at once,
and where there was an arrangement there is now one settled fact. That is very nearly the whole of
what is meant here, available free, in a word every reader owns outright and no physicist can
repossess.

*Superposition* has no life outside the formalism. To almost every reader it means nothing at all —
which is precisely why it is tempting, and precisely why it must go. **An analogy has to be made of
something the reader already has.** A word that is unfamiliar and impressive is not an analogy; it
is a credential, and borrowing it would not explain this claim to anyone. It would lend the claim
somebody else's authority, which is the exact transaction the ban exists to refuse. Refusing the
word we would most enjoy having is how the ban stops being a posture.

So: *open*. A matter is open when more than one way it could go is still live. It costs a syllable
and it borrows nothing.

In the game, the country past the draw distance is not sitting somewhere finished and hidden, the
way the envelope's contents are. It is generated at the point of contact — not stored, not fetched.
Riding over the hill is not the uncovering of a valley. It is the closing of one.

---

One question this chapter has raised and will not answer: *when*.

If holding a matter open has a cost, and closing it early has a different cost, and looking longer
sometimes reduces error and sometimes does not, then there is a right moment to settle, and it is a
determinate one — computable from the situation, not a counsel of patience dressed as wisdom. That
machinery is real and it is not needed yet. It belongs where the cost of being wrong has already
been established, and that is Book VIII.

What this chapter settles is what a measurement is. It is a contact that could have come out
otherwise, arriving from outside the part that is asking, landing hard enough to change what is now
the case — and closing, for one perspective and at its grade, a matter that had been open. The
possible becoming actual is not a thing the world does by itself in the dark. It is a thing that
happens at a place, and the places are everywhere, and there are nothing but places.

The vocabulary is now complete. What remains is to say plainly what this book will not do with it,
and which words it has declined to use on the way here — because a reader who has been sold this
territory before is owed the list, and owed it before the arguments start rather than after.
