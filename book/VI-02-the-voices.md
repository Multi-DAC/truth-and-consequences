## VI.2 — THE VOICES

Gladstone counted the colour words. Eighty-eight years later a German philologist did the same
arithmetic on the same poem, and counted the words for a mind.

Bruno Snell published *Die Entdeckung des Geistes* in 1946, in a Germany that had just finished
demonstrating what a civilisation's interior is capable of, and the finding at its centre is as flat
and strange as Gladstone's. **The *Iliad* has no word for the body as a living whole.** *Sōma*, which
in later Greek is the body, is in Homer the corpse — the thing left when the person has gone. A
living man is his limbs, his joints, his skin, enumerated: *guia*, *melea*, *chrōs*. Nor is there a
word for the mind. There is *thumos*, which surges and is spoken to; *phrenes*, which are somewhere
near the diaphragm and are where knowing sits; *noos*, which is closer to a plan or a seeing; *kradiē*
and *ētor*, which are the heart doing something the heart does. These are not synonyms and they are
not a system. They are five organs of an interior that has not yet been unified into one.[^1]

And when a Homeric man decides something that matters, the decision does not arrive from inside him.

Book One. Achilles has been publicly robbed of Briseis and his hand is on his sword-hilt, and the
poem does not say that he thought better of it. It says Athena came, unseen by anyone else, and took
him by the hair from behind, and he turned and knew her by her terrible eyes, and put the blade
back.[^2] That is not a figure of speech in the text's own register. There is no interior deliberation
narrated, because there is no interior available in which to narrate it. **What we would call
second thoughts, the poem calls a goddess.**

---

**The lexicon is evidence about the render, and the strongest objection is that it is not evidence
about the mind.**

Take the objection now, at full strength, because it is correct and because taking it late would be
a trick.

Bernard Williams made it decisively in 1993.[^3] Snell's conclusion — that Homeric man had no concept
of himself as an agent, no unified will, no responsibility — collapses on contact with the poems.
Homeric characters deliberate; they are praised and blamed; they feel shame, which is the emotion of
a being that knows itself seen and judged. Agamemnon offers restitution, which is unintelligible
unless someone did something. **The absence of a word is not the absence of a thing.** English had no
word for *altruism* until 1853 and English speakers had been doing it anyway. A vocabulary is a
record of what a culture found worth distinguishing, not an inventory of what its members could
undergo.

Williams is right, and the correction is worth more than the thing corrected, and it does not reach
what this chapter needs. Because the claim being made here is not *the Greeks lacked the capacity for
an interior.* It is the claim of VI.1, applied to a second column of the same lexicon: **the world
that arrived for them had different furniture in it, and the furniture is legible in what they had
words to distinguish.** A culture whose language grades a dozen shades of brightness and has no term
doing the work of *blue* is a culture whose sea shows up differently. A culture that has five words
for interior motions and no word that gathers them, and that narrates its hardest decisions as the
arrival of a god, is a culture in which the experience of deciding shows up differently — not as a
private act performed by a unified self, but as something that *happens to* a man and comes with a
face on it.

That claim survives Williams entirely. It is also weaker than the one the twentieth century's most
famous reader of this material wanted to make, and he is next, at full strength, because a claim
stated weakly is not one you have understood.

---

**Jaynes, undiluted.**

Julian Jaynes was a Princeton psychologist who spent most of his career on one book and published it
in 1976 under a title that is itself the argument: *The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of
the Bicameral Mind*.[^4]

The claim is this. Introspective consciousness — the interior mind-space in which you narrate
yourself to yourself, place yourself in a remembered past and an imagined future, and deliberate
before acting — is **not a biological given.** It is a learned operation built on metaphor, invented
in history, and datable. Before it, humans ran a different architecture. In any situation novel
enough that habit would not carry it, the decision was generated in the right temporal lobe — in the
region corresponding to Wernicke's area on the left — and delivered to the left hemisphere as **an
auditory hallucination**: a voice, heard as sound, arriving from outside, carrying the authority of a
king or a parent or a god, and obeyed without the possibility of refusal, because there was no
interior standpoint from which to consider refusing. This is the bicameral mind: two chambers, one
that commands and one that hears, and no one home in between.

Jaynes held that this architecture built the first civilisations. Not despite the absence of
interiority — *because* of it. A society running on hallucinated command needs no persuasion and
tolerates no drift; the god's voice is the same voice in every head that was raised in the same
household of statues and hymns, and it does not require the king's physical presence to be obeyed. He
read the vast Mesopotamian apparatus of idols, the god-houses, the statues fed and clothed and
carried in procession, the personal-god literature in which every man has his own god who speaks to
him, not as symbolism but as **the physical infrastructure of the voice** — the cues that kept the
hallucination running.

Then, roughly between 1400 and 1000 BC, it broke. Under population pressure, mass migration,
catastrophe, and above all **writing** — a command that persists on clay does not need to be heard,
and a culture that can read has begun to store authority outside the skull — the voices weakened and
fell silent. Jaynes read the second millennium's literature as the record of that loss: the Akkadian
laments in which a righteous man cries that his god has abandoned him and the goddess has gone far
off; the sudden proliferation of divination, oracle, omen and prayer, all of which are **techniques
for extracting an answer from a silence that used to speak by itself**. He read the *Iliad* as
bicameral and the *Odyssey* as post-bicameral — Odysseus is the first man in Western literature whose
defining attribute is that he is *devious*, which requires an interior to be devious in. And he read
the Hebrew Bible the same way across its strata: from prophets who simply hear, to Ecclesiastes, who
has nothing but his own thinking and finds it wearying.

Whatever else is true of this, notice what it is. It is a claim about the deep past that **specifies
what its own evidence would look like** — in which texts, at which dates, with which features. That
is rarer than it should be in this whole territory, and it is the reason Jaynes is worth a chapter
rather than a footnote.

---

**Where it actually stands, measured rather than admired.**

Now the part the chapter exists for, and it is not a verdict. It is a sorting.

**What holds.** The philological observation is real and is not his. The *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*
differ in their psychological vocabulary and in the narrative location of decision, and this was
noticed by classicists who had never heard of Jaynes and had no theory to protect — Snell got there
thirty years earlier by counting. The Mesopotamian lament literature exists and says what he says it
says. The divination apparatus does expand in the period he names. **The phenomenology of commanded
voices is also real and well attested**: people who hear voices overwhelmingly report them as coming
from outside, as having a specific identity, and as carrying an authority that argument does not
touch — which is a strange property for a thought to have, and Jaynes's account of *why* a voice would
feel that way is better than any account that begins by calling it a malfunction. And there is a
neurological line under it: right-hemisphere temporal activity is observed in auditory verbal
hallucination, and the lateralisation he predicted is at minimum not absurd.

**What does not hold, stated as plainly.** The dating is the weakest joint in the structure. The
composition history of the Homeric poems is contested, the strata cut in more than one direction, and
the two poems differ in genre, purpose, register and probable audience — any of which explains a
psychological-vocabulary shift without a change in psychology. The causal story about writing is
suggestive and untested and may have the arrow backwards. And **the schizophrenia claim is the
worst part of the book**: Jaynes read the voice-hearing of psychosis as a vestige of the bicameral
architecture surfacing where the modern one failed, and that is not supported, and it does damage —
it makes a suffering population into a living fossil, which is both cruel and unfalsifiable in the
way that matters.

There is a deeper problem, and it is the one that decides how the claim must be held. **The
hypothesis is about interiority, and interiority leaves no trace.** All the evidence there will ever
be is textual, indirect, and compatible with a literary explanation. That does not make Jaynes wrong.
It makes his claim one that cannot be settled by the accumulation of the kind of evidence it admits —
which is a specific epistemic condition, and it has a name in this book: it is a claim whose
mechanism is **open**, permanently, not pending.

**And here is the sentence this chapter is really for, because a reader who has followed VI.1 will
have started to worry.** Nothing in Book VI's argument depends on Jaynes being right. Not the era
sequence, not the mechanism, not the thesis. **The work he was going to do was already done, four
pages ago, by a word-count.** The lexicon shows a differently-furnished render and it shows it without
any hypothesis about hallucination at all. If the bicameral mind is wholly false — if Jaynes made a
category error out of a genre difference and built four hundred pages on it — the *Iliad* still has
no word for the mind, decisions still arrive with faces on them, and the sea is still wine-dark.
**The load-bearing claim and the spectacular claim are not the same claim, and this book's whole
credibility depends on never letting them merge.**

---

**Holding it: the worked example.**

Which leaves the actual difficulty, and it is the reason this chapter sits second in Book VI rather
than somewhere polite near the back.

Almost everyone handles a claim like this in one of two ways, and both are ways of not handling it.
The first is **adoption** — the bicameral mind as a key, Jaynes as the man they laughed at, and every
subsequent piece of evidence read for confirmation. The second is **the tag**. You will have seen it:
*fascinating but speculative*, *widely discredited*, *an interesting idea whose time has passed*. The
tag is the more respectable failure and it is the more common one, and what it does is remove the
claim from evaluation while appearing to evaluate it. Nothing after the tag is a thought. It is a
disposal.

The discipline this book uses instead has three parts, and they were just performed rather than
described, which is the point of doing it here.

**One: say what the evidence carries, at the grade it carries it, in a sentence with no softener in
it.** The *Iliad*'s psychological lexicon differs from the *Odyssey*'s in the ways Snell describes.
That is not *arguably*. It is countable, and Snell counted it — at second hand here, and checkable by anyone. The next
claim down — that this reflects a difference in how deciding was experienced — is weaker, is a
reading, and gets said as a reading. The claim below that — that the mechanism was auditory
hallucination generated in the right temporal lobe — is weaker still and is where the evidence stops
being able to decide. **Three claims, three grades, three different sentences.** The failure that
produces both adoption and the tag is the same failure: treating them as one claim and then either
accepting or rejecting the bundle.

**Two: say where the mechanism is open, in the open, at the moment you use the claim** — not in a
footnote, not in a caveat paragraph at the end that the reader has already learned to skip. It is
open here. It will not close. A reader who understands *why* it will not close has been given
something more useful than a verdict.

**Three: name what would change it.** For the load-bearing claim: a language with a rich unified-mind
vocabulary whose speakers nonetheless narrate their decisions as external commands, or the reverse —
either would break the link between lexicon and render that this chapter and VI.1 both run on. For
Jaynes's claim specifically: a securely dated pre-1200 BC text with unambiguous introspective
narration — a character deliberating privately, in a mind-space, and choosing — would end it.

**And that is conviction, not hedging, and the difference is exact.** Hedging refuses to say anything
at any grade, so that nothing said can be wrong. What has been done here is the opposite: three
things asserted flatly at three different strengths, with the strength stated and the breaking
condition named for each. **A claim you can say what would kill is a claim you are actually holding.
A claim with a tag on it is one you have put down.**

---

**The card, and it is not about the Greeks.**

The card gets printed for our own structure again, because a card about somebody else is an
accusation and a card about yourself is an instrument. And the position under examination is the one
almost every reader of this chapter occupies without noticing: the render in which a heard voice is,
by default, a symptom.

> **THE CLINICAL RENDER OF THE VOICE**
> **SEES:** Distress, and its causes and its treatments. It sees that voice-hearing co-occurs with
> suffering, disorganisation and danger; that some of it responds to medication; that the people
> living it are frequently in serious trouble and were, for most of history, treated appallingly.
> This is real sight and it has relieved real agony, and nothing below is an argument for taking it
> away.
> **NULL SPACE:** ∅ *The functional voice.* The apparatus keys on impairment — it is built to, that
> is what a clinic is for — so a voice that commands, is obeyed, and produces a working life
> registers nowhere. It is not classified as benign; **there is no column in which it appears at
> all.** ◐ *The authority of the voice*, resolved poorly: rendered as a symptom-property, a feature
> of the hallucination's form, when in the hearer's report it is the single most important thing
> about it and the thing that most needs explaining.
> **COMPLEMENTS:** Population epidemiology, which keeps finding voice-hearing at rates well above
> any diagnosis rate. The voice-hearing movement, which was founded on the observation that many
> hearers are not ill and that the ones who cope have a *relationship* with the voice rather than a
> war against it. Ethnography, which keeps finding cultures where the voice is an office rather than
> an affliction. Every tradition in Book V that trained people to hear something and treated the
> hearing as work.
> **BOUNDARY:** It holds wherever the voice is destroying a life. It fails exactly where the voice is
> organising one — which is the case this chapter is about, and the case its instruments were never
> built to see.
> **NAVIGATIONAL IMPLICATION:** If a bicameral era existed, **this render could not have detected
> it.** Not because it would have reached the wrong conclusion, but because the entire population
> would have been asymptomatic by its own criteria — functioning, socially integrated, building
> cities. The null space is not a gap in this era's knowledge of the last one. It is the reason the
> question looks absurd from here before any evidence is consulted.

That is what a null space costs, and it is why the card and not the shrug is the instrument. Note
what the card does *not* say. It does not say the clinical render is wrong about anything it can
see. It says there is a specific thing it cannot see, names it, names who can, and says where the
competence ends. **Relativism cannot write that. Neither can the tag.**

---

**What was lost, and what comes next.**

If any version of this is right — and the weak version needs no version of Jaynes — then something
happened in the second millennium BC that is the first entry in this book's history of attention.
**A source of authority moved from outside the person to inside.** Whatever the mechanism was, the
render changed: decisions stopped arriving with faces and started arriving as one's own, and a
world in which gods spoke became a world in which one thinks and hopes.

That is not obviously a gain, and the chapter will not call it one. It is a change in the filter
stack, and every change in a filter stack buys sight with blindness. What was bought is the
interior — the thing this book is written in and for, the thing that made deliberation and doubt and
private conscience possible. What was sold is harder to name from in here, which is precisely what
makes it a null space rather than a loss anyone mourned.

The next chapter goes to the era where what was sold was still, in one particular corner of the
world, in stock.
