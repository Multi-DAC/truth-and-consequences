VIII.3 — EDITING
The tunnel is editable. Book I planted that mythically, Book III
made it structural, and Book VI spent an entire volume show‐
ing you which layer your century installed. This is the chapter
where  it  either  becomes  a  practice  or  becomes  the  worst
sentence in the book.
Because there is a reading of the filter is editable that costs
nothing  and  promises  everything,  and  the  reader  arrives
already holding it. It is the reading in which editing the filter
edits the world — in which attention is a lever on outcomes, in
which the wanting is the mechanism, in which what happens
to you is downstream of what you held in mind about it. That
reading is  available from our premises. It is not what our
premises say. And the difference has to be stated here, in the
chapter that teaches the technique, rather than in a qualifier
somewhere  behind  it,  because  a  firewall  that  lives  in  a
different chapter from the fire is decoration.
So the line this chapter is under, before it offers anything:
The edit is to the filter, which is yours. The render
remains co-constituted.
Everything below is downstream of that sentence, including
the parts that sound like power.
I. What a technique is once you take the
cosmology off
Every method in this chapter was found by somebody who be‐
lieved something we have spent seven volumes declining to
957

[[p.958]]
believe. That is not an awkwardness to be managed. It is the
actual epistemic situation and it is worth being exact about,
because the two obvious ways to handle it are both wrong.
The first wrong way is to take the package whole — to con‐
clude  that  because  the  technique  works,  the  cosmology  it
came wrapped in is true. This is the most common form of re‐
ligious conversion by practice, and it is a straightforward in‐
ference error: an operation that reliably changes your percep‐
tual position is evidence about the operation, not about the
account its inventors gave of why it works. The people who
found these methods were not running controlled comparis‐
ons. They were running the method, for centuries, and keep‐
ing what survived contact. That is a real and rare kind of em‐
pirical work and it warrants exactly the confidence it earns,
which is confidence in the procedure.
The second wrong way is more flattering and more common
among readers of books like this one: to have the techniques
stripped of their cosmologies, keep the mechanics, and call
the result an upgrade.  They found the technique; we found
what it was actually doing. The trouble is that a cosmology is
frequently doing structural work in the technique, and the
stripping  is  lossy  in  a  way  that  stays  invisible  until  the
practice fails. A rite that means you are being received is not
the same operation as the same rite performed as self-regula‐
tion, because the first one recruits a collective perspective
and the second one does not. Take the metaphysics out of a
monastic hour and you get a stress-reduction protocol with a
worse adherence rate, and the adherence rate was the whole
mechanism.
The honest position is narrower than either. You can take a
technique without taking its account of itself, and doing
958

[[p.959]]
so costs you something you should be able to name. Usu‐
ally what it costs is the container: the community that expects
you on Thursday, the stakes that make the hour non-optional,
the  story  that  makes  the  difficult  middle  year  survivable.
Those are not superstition. They are the load- bearing infra‐
structure of a long practice, and the modern practitioner who
took the technique and left the tradition has generally left the
infrastructure and kept the instructions.
What a taxonomy adds — and this is the only thing it adds,
and it is enough — is the ability to say what two methods from
two unrelated traditions have in common. That is a question
no tradition can answer from inside itself, because each one
has only its own vocabulary for what it does. And it is the
question that determines which method you should reach for,
because the answer tells you which part of the apparatus a
given operation actually touches.
II. The eight classes
There are eight, ordered by how far through configuration
space the movement goes.1 They are not a menu. The order‐
ing is not a ranking. Read them as a parts list for the machine
you already are.
Class I — Attentional. The most fundamental and the most
continuous. You are doing it now. Every shift of attention is a
small movement, and the traditions that formalise it are pre‐
cise about which property of attention they train:  vipassana
trains the precision of the movement, samatha trains the sta‐
bility of the position, metta trains the direction of attentional
gravity — where attention falls when you stop steering it.
Three  practices,  three  different  parameters,  routinely  de‐
959

[[p.960]]
scribed in English as “meditation” as if they were one thing.
Practice: notice what you attend to. That is where you
are going. Redirect it deliberately. That is navigation.
Class II — Somatic. Breath, posture, sensation, action. Yoga,
tai chi, the martial arts, dance, sport, walking. The mechanic
is the one the culture gets exactly backwards: the body is not
a vehicle carrying you around while you do the real work up‐
stairs.  The  body’s  state  is  a  navigational  coordinate.
Change the state, and you are somewhere else — not meta‐
phorically, not as a mood, but in the sense that a different set
of dimensions is now accessible to you. This is why the advice
to go for a walk is annoying and correct.
Class  III  —  Cognitive. Reasoning,  modelling,  theorising.
Learning a new framework is not acquiring information; it is
installing  a  differently-shaped  restriction  over  conceptual
space. The physicist sees forces, the poet sees figures, the
economist sees incentives, and each of those perceptions is
real.  Collecting  frameworks  does  not  make  you  right.  It
widens the range of moves that look available, which is a
different and more useful thing.
Class  IV  —  Altered-state. Deliberate  modification  of  the
restriction itself: psychedelics, holotropic breathwork, sensory
deprivation, lucid dreaming, sustained flow. These reach re‐
gions that are otherwise unreachable, and the reason they are
dangerous is not that they are unreliable. They are danger‐
ous precisely because they work. Opening dimensions that
are normally shut is the same operation as loosening the re‐
striction that constitutes you, and the completeness-dissolu‐
tion result from Book II applies without amendment: the fur‐
ther you open, the more of the identity you temporarily re‐
lease. Set, setting, intention and integration are not safety
960

[[p.961]]
theatre appended to the fun part. They are the navigational
parameters that decide whether the excursion is an expansion
or an injury. Every tradition that works with these states built
a container around them — a rite, a guide, a community —
and they did not all independently invent the same precaution
for no reason.
Class  V  —  Relational. Movement  through  another  being.
Conversation, collaboration, love, conflict, teaching. The oth‐
er’s restriction is shaped differently from yours, the two inter‐
sect,  and  the  combination  reaches  places  neither  reaches
alone.  The  practice  implication  is  unsentimental  and  most
people resist it:  you are choosing your landscape when
you choose who you spend hours with. Not because some
people  are  positive  and  others  are  negative,  which  is  the
greeting-card version, but because the combined geometry
either opens dimensions that matter to you or closes them,
and it does so whether or not anybody involved intends it.
Class VI — Collective. Rite, ceremony, worship, protest, fest‐
ival, team flow, the surgical theatre. The collective being’s
perspective supersedes the individual ones and grants access
to regions no member can reach alone. This is not a poetic
way of saying that groups are motivating. It is the mechanism
by which a congregation, a march, a drum circle and an oper‐
ating room all report the same structural experience under
wildly different accounts of it. And the risk is symmetrical and
gets mentioned less: group contraction is exactly as real as
group expansion, and the mob, the cult and the panic are the
same machine running the other way.
Class VII — Macroscopic quantum. Theoretical. Movement
that would exploit quantum coherence at the scale of a living
961

[[p.962]]
system.  Evidence:  the  framework  predicts  it;  nothing
here confirms it. Practice: none prescribable.8
That last line is why Class VII is in this chapter rather than
quietly dropped from it. A practice chapter that silently omits
the one class with no practice is doing the tidy thing this book
spent Book V attacking — trimming the taxonomy to the part
that produces instructions and letting the reader infer that
the taxonomy was always that shape. The framework predicts
this class. We cannot confirm it or refute it from where we are
standing. Both of those sentences are true simultaneously, the
second one is not a hedge, and the correct move is to name
the class, print its empty practice line, and pay the cost of
looking incomplete. The honesty is cheaper than the omis‐
sion, and the omission would have been undetectable, which
is precisely what makes it worth refusing.
Class VIII — Instrument-assisted. Targeted modulation of
the nervous tissue that implements the restriction: temporal
interference  stimulation,  transcranial  magnetic  stimulation,
neurofeedback, brain-computer interfaces. It gets its own sec‐
tion, because it is the only class our own century built, be‐
cause it is the one a reader in this decade arrives already
holding opinions about, and because its failure mode arrives
disguised  as  its  own  instrumentation,  which  is  a  difficulty
none of the other seven present.
III. The lever
Of those eight, one runs through all the others, and it is worth
isolating before going further:  attention is the only lever
that reliably moves. Not the most powerful — Class IV is
more powerful and Class VI is more powerful. The most avail‐
962

[[p.963]]
able. It is the one you can operate right now, without equip‐
ment, without a group, without a substance, without a dec‐
ade.
Four things govern what it does.2
It is constitutive, not passive. Attending to something does
not merely register it; it crystallises it — the thing attended to
becomes more defined, more present, more there in your con‐
figuration space. This is why sustained attention deepens a
skill and strengthens a relationship. It is also why sustained
attention  on  a  feared  outcome  makes  the  feared  outcome
vivid, which is the same mechanism and not a different one,
and which is the first place the manifestation reading gets its
foothold. Hold that; the next section is about exactly what this
does and does not license.
Its scarcity is a fact about you, not about reality. You
have finite attention because you are a being with a restric‐
tion, not because attention is a limited substance in the world.
Practically: it is zero-sum  for you. What you attend to costs
what you do not. There is no configuration of your life in
which you attend to everything that deserves it, and every
hour of practice is therefore purchased from something, and a
practice chapter that does not say so is selling.
Contraction creates topology. When attention narrows —
through fear, obsession, devotion, necessity — it cuts chan‐
nels: deep, narrow canyons in the landscape. Those channels
are how expertise happens, how devotion happens, how the
survivor perceives danger with a precision nobody else has.
They are real capability. And they constrain lateral movement
in exact proportion to the depth they grant. You can go very
far  in  one  direction  and  find  you  cannot  turn.  This  is  the
single most useful diagnostic in the chapter, because it ex‐
963

[[p.964]]
plains why the thing you are best at is frequently adjacent to
the thing you cannot do, and why that adjacency is structural
rather than a personal failing.
Mutual internal attention stabilises. Beings whose parts
attend to each other — the organs of a body, the members of a
family, the departments of a working institution — hold coher‐
ence when external attention fluctuates. Which is a practice
claim, not just an ontological one: the internal arrangement of
what you are made of is itself something attention builds, and
it is what makes a person durable rather than merely intense.
IV. The firewall
Now the sentence this chapter is under, at full length, be‐
cause everything in Section III sounds like the beginning of a
promise and it is not.
Attention is constitutive. The filter is editable. Both are ours,
both have been argued, neither is being walked back. And the
conclusion the reader wants to draw from them — therefore
what I attend to comes to me — does not follow, and the reas‐
on it does not follow is a claim this book made in Book II and
has never withdrawn.
Your render is not solely yours. It is co-constituted: what
appears  to  you  is  a  joint  product  of  your  restriction  and
everything on the other side of it, and the other side does not
consult you. That is the whole of C10 and it is not a caveat
bolted onto C12 — it is the older claim of the two. C12 says
the filter is editable. It says nothing about the world, because
the  filter  is  not  the  world.  Editing  the  restriction  changes
964

[[p.965]]
what is accessible to you. It does not change what is the
case.
The slide happens because both operations feel identical from
inside. When you change what you can perceive, the world
changes — that is what it is like to change what you can per‐
ceive. There is no phenomenological marker distinguishing  I
now notice the thing that was always there from I caused the
thing to be there, and a practice that produces the first re‐
peatedly will generate the second as an interpretation, un‐
prompted, with sincerity. The interpretation is not a lapse of
discipline. It is what the data looks like from the only seat you
have.
Three consequences, all of them practical.
The  first:  the  manifestation  reading  fails  on  its  own
terms, before it fails on ours. Contracted attention — want‐
ing hard, gripping, checking whether it has arrived yet — is
the operation that cuts a narrow channel. That is Principle 3,
and it is the mechanism of expertise; applied to an outcome it
is  the  mechanism  by  which  the  outcome  recedes,  because
what you have built is a deep canyon oriented toward a thing
you do not have. The traditions that noticed this called it non-
attachment, and every one of them was careful to say that
non-attachment is not indifference. It is engagement without
the grip. So even the frame in which wanting is the lever gets
the sign wrong on its own showing.
The second: this is not a demotion of attention to noth‐
ing. A joint product is not a product you did not contribute to.
Your restriction is one of the two things determining what ap‐
pears, and it is the only one you can touch. That is a real and
substantial power and it is smaller than sovereignty, and the
difference between substantial and sovereign is the whole of
965

[[p.966]]
the discipline here. Half of what appears to you is a matter of
where you are standing. None of it is a matter of what you
want.
The third, and it is the one that costs something: this
bars a comfort the book is otherwise positioned to offer.
We do not get to tell the reader that the affliction was in the
framing. Book VII said that at full price and this chapter does
not reopen it. Some of what arrives is not filter and cannot be
edited, and a chapter of technique is obliged to say so at the
top of its instructions rather than in the small print, because
the reader most likely to run a practice hardest is the one for
whom this is currently untrue.
Editable is not obedient. The edit is to the filter,
which is yours; the render remains co-constituted.
Everything below buys you a different vantage.
None of it buys you a different world.
V. What actually changes a filter, and what only
feels like it does
With the firewall in place, the mechanical question is answer‐
able,  and  it  turns  out  to  have  a  clean  answer  that  most
practice literature does not state.
Almost everything that feels like it changed a filter changed
the contents of the render instead. Reading a compelling de‐
scription of your own conditioning is a content event: you now
hold a new, accurate, vivid object, and holding it feels like the
transformation because insight has the phenomenology of ar‐
rival. The weekend intensive is a content event with better
staging. The decision to be different from now on is a content
966

[[p.967]]
event with a deadline attached. None of these is worthless —
an accurate object is a precondition for aiming — but all of
them are things that happened inside the restriction, and the
restriction is unchanged at the end of them. This is why the
effect decays on a schedule that surprises everyone who ex‐
periences it, and why the decay reads as a personal failure of
will rather than as the expected behaviour of the operation
that was actually performed.
What changes a parameter is repetition at the level of the
parameter. The same operation, performed enough times, at
the same place in the day, until the position it produces is the
default rather than the achievement. That is not an inspiring
answer  and  its  unglamorousness  is  diagnostic:  notice  that
every  tradition’s  real  instruction  is  a  schedule,  not  a
realisation. Ignatius wrote a thirty-day sequence with times
of day in it. The monastic hours are a timetable. The Buddhist
instruction is a posture and a duration.  Zazen is, in its en‐
tirety, an arrangement of a body for a period. None of these
hands you a truth. Each of them hands you a time and a repe‐
tition, because the founders were running the operation that
works on the parameter, and the truths were what turned up
in the region the operation opened.
Beat three gets its mechanism in one line, and the line is
about  the  difference  between  two  of  the  classes:  psyche‐
delics are a single lever pulling on the entire restriction
simultaneously; instrument-assisted navigation is a set
of precision dials, each adjusting a specific parameter.3
Generalise it and you have the actual taxonomy of edits.
Whole-restriction operations — Class IV, and the deep end
of Class VI — move everything at once. They are not unreli‐
able; they are non-specific, which is a different complaint and
967

[[p.968]]
a worse one, because the region they open is not the region
you selected and the integration afterwards is the part that
decides what you keep. This is why the traditions put the con‐
tainer around the excursion and not around the preparation,
and why the modern recreational version, which keeps the
substance and drops the container, gets a strong experience
and a weak change.
Aim operations — Class I, Class III — do not move the re‐
striction at all. They move where it points. Cheap, repeatable,
available now, and their effect is bounded by the restriction’s
actual shape: no amount of aiming reaches a dimension the
restriction excludes.
Dial operations — Class VIII — move one parameter and
leave the others. That capability did not previously exist in
any class, and it is worth a card.
The census entry
Whose: instrument-assisted  navigation  —  direct,  targeted
modulation of the nervous tissue that implements the restric‐
tion. Temporal interference stimulation, transcranial magnetic
stimulation,  neurofeedback,  brain-computer  interfaces.  Not
the folk category  brain gadget: the specific claim that a re‐
striction with a physical implementation can be adjusted at
the implementation.
Renders: what it does that nothing else does — selectivity.
Two kilohertz-frequency currents delivered at the scalp pro‐
duce an interference envelope where they cross, oscillating at
the difference between them; neurons at the intersection re‐
spond  and  neurons  elsewhere  do  not,  which  permits  focal

modulation of a structure six to eight centimetres deep with
only  minimal  exposure  of  the  cortex  above  it.  The  con‐

Suppress alpha at the posterior cingulate and the enforce‐

up,  self-referential  processing  down,  more  accessible.  En‐

move one of those and hold the rest still. Class IV opens
everything; Class I changes only the aim. This one is the first
dial in a room that previously contained a single switch.

VI. Switching, which is the thing you can
actually start this week
The previous chapter owed this one a method and deferred it
here on purpose.5 It is the second of the three ways of illumin‐
ating  what  you  cannot  see,  and  it  is  the  only  one  in  this
chapter that requires no equipment, no group and no sub‐
stance.
Take up a second tradition far enough to run it. Not far
enough to believe it.
Every navigational tradition is a time-tested package of meth‐
ods inside a specific collective restriction, and each of them is
optimised — Buddhism for the relation between attention and
suffering, Christianity for the relation between separation and
reunion, science for the structural dimensions, the indigenous
traditions for coherence with ecological and ancestral dimen‐
sions that the modern package has no operations for at all.
None of them is complete, and that is guaranteed rather
than observed: the theorem from Book II applies to a collect‐
ive restriction exactly as it applies to yours. Every tradition
has a region it structurally cannot reach. Every tradition has
also mapped regions the others never entered.
So the skill is not selecting the correct tradition. It is knowing
what each one opens and what each one closes, and moving
between them with the standing awareness that each is a pos‐
ition and none is the terrain. And the reason this functions as
a diagnostic rather than as tourism is the sharpest structural
line in the account:  different classes access different di‐
mensions, and the region each one cannot reach is a
different region — which is why complementary prac‐
972

[[p.973]]
tices illuminate each other’s excluded ground.6 Medita‐
tion  does  not  reach  what  conversation  reaches.  Reasoning
does not reach what the body knows. A substance does not
reach what fifteen years of a schedule builds. Run only one
and you will conclude that what it reaches is what there is,
and you will be sincere.
Three  things  about  the  cost,  because  a  method  offered
without its price is advertising.
It is slow. Running a tradition means running it long enough
for its middle to arrive, and every tradition’s middle is the
boring part that its converts do not describe. A month is a
sample of the literature, not of the practice.
It is embarrassing. You will be a beginner in a room of
people who are not, holding a posture badly, saying words you
do not mean in a register you find uncomfortable. That dis‐
comfort is not an obstacle to the method; a good deal of it is
the method, because the thing you are trying to find is pre‐
cisely what your own package made unthinkable, and unthink‐
able things do not feel profound on first contact. They feel stu‐
pid.  The sensation of stupidity in a foreign practice is
the closest thing available to a positive signal, and it is
systematically misread as evidence that the practice is empty.
And it does not deliver what you went for. If you take up a
second tradition to solve a specific problem, you will find that
it has no operation for your problem — which is the finding,
though it never feels like one. What it has is operations for
things you did not know were questions.
The bound, stated so it is not overrun: switching gives you a
second position. It does not give you an outside one.
Two insides do not sum to a view from nowhere, and the pre‐
973

[[p.974]]
vious  chapter’s  discipline  holds  without  amendment  here.
What you get from the second tradition is not the truth about
the first. It is the discovery that a dimension exists which your
first package had no operation for, and that discovery is real,
bounded, and considerably less than enlightenment.
VII. Rite, made ordinary
Which leaves the thing that looked like this chapter’s subject
when  it  started,  and  is  now  four  paragraphs,  because  the
taxonomy did its work.
A rite is a scheduled, repeated, embodied, usually collective
operation performed  before the content arrives. That is the
entire mechanic. Not the meaning of the rite, not its symbol‐
ism,  not  whether  the  participants  believe  the  account  at‐
tached to it: the scheduling, the repetition, the body, and the
fact that it precedes rather than responds. Stripped of the
vestments, every ceremony any human culture has performed
reduces to a parameter operation, which is why it works when
a resolution does not. A resolution is a content event with
a date. A rite is a repetition that reaches the restriction.
Which is to say that ritual is tunnel-engineering — the deliber‐
ate, scheduled reshaping of the restriction itself — carried out
for several thousand years by people who had every other
name for it and were not wrong about what it did.
Made ordinary, this is unglamorous to the point of anticlimax
and it is the practical content of the chapter. The same chair.
The same hour. The walk before the work and not after it. The
sentence you say to yourself before you open the thing you
are afraid of. The refusal to eat at the desk. None of these is
sacred and none of them needs to be; they are the same ma‐
974

The failure mode is worth naming because it is the common
one and it looks like success. A rite can become an operation
whose only remaining function is to be performed — the con‐
tainer maintained after the thing it contained stopped arriv‐
ing.  There  is  no  test  for  this  from  inside,  and  the  honest
advice is not a test but a period:  run it long enough to
know whether it is working, and set the review date be‐
fore you start, in writing, the way the previous chapter
said. Otherwise the review happens when you are tired of it,
which selects for exactly the wrong outcome.
VIII. What this leaves you holding
Eight classes, one of them with no instruction, one of them re‐
quiring hardware most readers do not have and a safety pro‐
tocol that is currently the only validated part of it. A lever that

is available constantly and is bounded by a restriction it can‐

rassing and does not deliver what you went for. And a rite,
which is a schedule.
That is a thinner offer than a practice chapter usually makes,
and the thinness is the result of keeping the firewall up. Every
place where this chapter could have promised more was a
place  where  the  promise  would  have  been  editable  means
obedient, and the whole of Book VIII is a test of whether the
preceding  seven  volumes  were  describing  anything  real.  A
practice chapter that oversells is not merely dishonest; it is
evidence against the cosmology it came from, because a true
account of a restriction should produce modest instructions
and an untrue one produces magnificent ones.
What the reader has, at the end, is a set of operations that re‐
liably  change  where  they  are  standing,  no  operation  that
changes  what  is  standing  there,  and  a  taxonomy  precise
enough to tell which is which. The edit is to the filter. The
filter is yours. What comes through it never was.
