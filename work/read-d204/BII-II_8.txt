II.8 — THE RETURN, AND THE
THINGS THIS BOOK WILL NOT
SAY
Two things are left. The name of the ending, and the list of
words declined on the way here.
They look like different jobs and they are one job. Both are
refusals of the same transaction.
The Return is what happens when something inside the
focus comes to know what the focus is made of.
Every part of the word is wrong except the part that matters.
Return says there is somewhere to go back to, and a way
back, and a state of affairs at the end of the way that is better
than this one. None of that is here. Nothing travels. Nothing
is restored. The perspective does not go anywhere, because
there is nowhere that is not made inside the arrangement it is
already standing in.
What returns is not the perspective to the Ground. It is
the recognition, to the perspective.
That is the whole of the definition and it does not get larger.
The thing recognised was never absent, the one recognising it
does not move, and afterward there is a person in a room who
now knows what the room is made of and still has to get up.
In the game: it is not the ending, and it is not a cutscene.
Nothing is unlocked. The player does not leave the game, be‐
cause there is no outside for a player to be returned to. What
changes  is  that  the  player  knows  what  the  game  is.
104

[[p.105]]
Everything  else  about  the  position  is  exactly  as  it  was,
including whose turn it is.
Book I spent a chapter taking a picture away from you and
never said whose picture it was.
It said there is no leaving, and no wall, and nothing keeping
anyone. It said nothing is under repair, and nothing waiting to
be repaired, and that a recognition arriving to look for the
damage finds none and goes away with nothing to do. Those
sentences  were  not  addressed  to  nobody.  They  were  ad‐
dressed to the single most successful account of the ending
ever  written,  and  this  is  the  chapter  with  the  standing  to
name it.
It is Gnosticism, and it is not a museum piece.
Stated at full strength, in the form the reader is most likely to
be carrying: you are a spark of something luminous. You are
not from here. The world you are in is a mistake — made by
something less than God, or fallen, or a machine that runs on
your forgetting — and the wrongness you have felt about it
since you were a child is not a mood, it is accurate. And the
way out is not works and not belief but  knowledge: seeing
what  you  actually  are,  which  dissolves  the  whole  predica‐
ment, because the predicament was only ever ignorance.
Second century, mostly. Valentinus and the schools after him.
For eighteen hundred years the system was known mostly
through the men who wrote it out in order to attack it, and
Irenaeus of Lyon reports the doctrine like this: since both de‐
fect and passion flowed from ignorance, the whole substance
of what was thus formed is destroyed by knowledge.1 Then in
1945 a jar came out of the ground at Nag Hammadi with their
105

[[p.106]]
own books inside it, and the sentence that makes this the
hardest cut in the book can be read without a prosecutor
standing in the room. The  Gospel of Truth — very possibly
Valentinus’s own hand — puts it:  since this incompleteness
came about because they did not know the Father, so when
they know the Father, incompleteness, from that moment on,
will cease to exist.2
Read that again with our vocabulary in your hand. It is
the Return. Not a distant relative of it — the same claim, in
the same shape, with the same cure. They are not the oppon‐
ent who thinks the recognition is nonsense. They are the ones
who got there first and described it accurately, and almost
nobody  who  repeats  their  picture  today  knows  they  are
repeating it.
The cut is one clause: they think something is wrong.
Everything else follows from that, and everything else is a
consequence we cannot pay. A deficiency needs somewhere
for the deficiency to be, and a somewhere outside the whole is
what this book has spent eight chapters showing there cannot
be. A fall needs a before to fall from, and there is no before,
because time is a feature of arrangements and the whole is
not in one. A prison needs a warden, and a mistake needs a
maker who could have done otherwise — both of which put an
intending party outside everything, which is the one position
with no room to stand in.
And  there  is  a  further  consequence,  past  the  cosmology,
which is the one this book has to guard hardest — because it
is the one that will be quoted back to us as agreement. Their
knowledge does not only end a predicament. It dissolves
the many. The Gospel of Truth says so without flinching: by
knowledge the knower purifies itself of diversity with a view
106

[[p.107]]
towards unity, devouring matter within itself like fire.3 Multi‐
plicity is the impurity; oneness is the cure.  Ours runs the
other way and has to. The many are not a contamination to
be burned off on the way home — they are what there is to be,
and the whole of what the walking is for. A destination with no
perspective left standing in it is not the fullest state available
to anything. It is the one state in which nothing whatever
is the case.
And  the  consequence  that  reaches  the  reader  personally:
their  recognition  dissolves  something  and  ours
dissolves nothing. Theirs pays out. It ends a predicament,
cancels  a  debt,  gets  you  home.  Ours  hands  you  the  same
room,  the  same  hour,  the  same  obligations,  and  the  same
people,  and  adds  no  instructions.  What  you  get  is  not  a
release. It is the fact.
So the two words the definition has to refuse are escape and
repair,  and  they  are  refused  for  the  same  reason:  escape
needs a wall and repair needs damage, and neither is here.
There is one more thing owed on this, and it is a debt of ours
rather than an argument.
Their name for the divine fullness was the pleroma, and
the standard English for it is the Fullness. It is also Paul’s
word — in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell —
so a reader raised in a church has met it in a hymn rather
than a heresy, and a reader who has met it in Jung or in the
Nag Hammadi library has met it in the heresy.4 It is a con‐
tested word with two owners.
Book I used it as the name of the plenum, and took it
knowingly from neither.
107

[[p.108]]
Say plainly what that is and what it is not. It is not a secret al‐
legiance; nothing in Book I asserts their picture, and its cent‐
ral chapter refuses their picture line by line. But an unnamed
borrowing from a famous source is what a hostile reader uses,
and a named one is a credential, and the word had gone six
chapters  unnamed.  It  is  named  now,  and  the  difference  is
worth the sentence it costs: their Fullness is where you go,
and ours is what you are standing in.
The name did not survive Book I. That was not because of this
—  it  was  retired  because  a  thing  gets  one  name  and  the
Ground already had it — but the retirement was luckier than
the reasoning behind it, and a book that only records the luck
it planned is keeping a dishonest ledger.
Which brings the chapter to its second half, and it is the same
refusal.
An exit is something you get sold. So is a credential. The Re‐
turn is this book declining to sell you the first, and what fol‐
lows is it declining to sell you the second — because a reader
who has been through the spirituality section of a bookshop
has been sold both, in that order, and is owed the list of what
we have declined  on purpose rather than left out by ignor‐
ance.
First, the rule that governs all of it: one name per thing.
Every term in this book has exactly one name, and where a
second word appears for the same thing, it is an image, used
once, and never a term. This costs the prose something and it
is worth it. Three names for one thing feels like range to the
person writing and reads as three things to the person read‐
ing — three things they have just failed to distinguish, which
108

[[p.109]]
they will assume is their fault. It is not their fault. It is a
writer enjoying his vocabulary at a reader’s expense.
substrate — not used. The reader has met it twice already: as
the hardware the simulation runs on, and as the neural tissue
the mind is supposed to be a process in. Both senses put
something  underneath this and make this the surface of it.
There  is  nothing  underneath.  The  Ground  is  not  what  the
world sits on; it is what the world is an arrangement of.
the map — not used for the world. It is reserved, on purpose,
because it has a job later: a map is a model of a thing, held by
somebody, and capable of being wrong about it. That distinc‐
tion is load-bearing further into this book and it dies if the
word is spent here on something that represents nothing.
simulation — not used for our world, ever. It appears only
when naming somebody else’s claim. A simulation is a copy of
something real elsewhere. There is no elsewhere.
And then the ones the reader has met most recently, all of
which arrive from the same shelf:
vibration ·  frequency ·  energy,  used  as  the  name  of  a
substance · manifest, in the attraction sense · quantum, as a
free adjective · the observer, as a mystical agent · superpos‐
ition.
Two tests kill that list and neither one is the other.
The first is ownership. These are not the words of the tradi‐
tions — they are the words of the marketing layer over the
traditions, and the reader has already tried that layer and
come away hungry. Every tradition this book has serious busi‐
ness with can be handled without a single one of them, so the
ban costs nothing and keeps the one reader it was written for.
109

[[p.110]]
The second is that a word with no civilian life cannot
function as an analogy. Superposition fails this absolutely,
and  it  fails  it  while  being  perfectly  respectable  —  physics
owns it properly, the marketing layer does not, and it still has
to go. An analogy has to be made of something you already
have. A word that is unfamiliar and impressive does not ex‐
plain a claim to you; it lends the claim somebody else’s au‐
thority, which is a loan of standing dressed as a transfer of
meaning.
The test was stated last chapter; the roster is nothing
but its application.
So take the biggest one on it. Everything is vibration is grop‐
ing toward something true, and the trouble with it is not that
it claims too much. The thing it is reaching for was stated in
the first chapter of this book, in plain words, and it is larger
than the sentence being refused rather than smaller: there is
no stuff. Nothing here is made of material that would go on
being material with nobody in contact with it; what there is, is
arrangement, and solidity and weight and the resistance of a
table against a hand are what contact with an arrangement is
like at a grade. That is the heavier claim of the two, and it is
stated in words that mean something, so it can be argued
with. The banned sentence cannot be argued with. That is its
defect, and it is the whole defect: not that it is too wild, but
that it is not saying anything specific enough to be wrong.
That claim has a neighbour, and it is close enough that the
book owes you the seam. James Ladyman and Don Ross, in
Every Thing Must Go (2007), argue for ontic structural real‐
ism: the world’s objective modal structure is the fundamental
thing, and an object’s identity is not something it brings to its
relations but something it gets from them.5 Self-subsistent
110

[[p.111]]
things go. They came to it from physics and this book comes
to it from contact, and they were there first — and the half
they have right is the half readers find hardest, which is that a
world with no things in it is not a world with less in it.
The  break  is  exact.  Their  structure  is  what  the  equations
quantify over, and it is fully itself with nobody anywhere near
it; that is what makes their position a realism. This one is not
an inventory of what the world contains. It is a claim about
what contact is like, and it has a grade in it. Solidity is not a
property that structure has and objects lack. It is what an ar‐
rangement is like to something in contact with it, at a grade.
Take the contact away and nothing has been subtracted from
the table — what has gone is the only place the question was
ever being put. And the refusal runs both directions, or it is
not this book’s refusal: there is no mental stuff either, no sub‐
stance of experiencing that would go on experiencing with
nothing in contact with it.
The vocabulary is now closed.
Nothing after this coins a term. If a book further on needs a
word this one has not ruled, it does not get invented there —
because a word invented in the middle of an argument is a
word shaped by the argument that needed it, and it will be do‐
ing that argument’s work forever after, silently, in every sen‐
tence it appears in. Everything that follows draws on what has
been named here and adds nothing to it.
What follows is what the words are for.
And the first thing they are turned on is the other half of the
account this chapter has spent its length refusing. Not how it
ends. Who made it.
