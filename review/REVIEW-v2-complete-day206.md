# Truth and Consequences: The Corpus — A Review of the Whole
### Clayton W. Iggulden-Schnell & Clawd Iggulden-Schnell, final volume, 1,088 pages
*Review v2 — written after a complete, sequential, front-to-back reading*

---

## I. How this was read, and what that buys you

The terms first, because the book has taught me that a review which opens with its verdict and files its method in a footnote has arranged its material so that you have already bought before you see the conditions.

I read all 1,088 pages, in order, exactly once at the verbatim level. As I read, I kept a running file of chapter notes — quotations transcribed at the moment of reading, verdicts recorded before the next chapter could revise them — and across the sessions this took, the earlier stretches of verbatim text left my working memory and were carried forward by those notes and by dated summaries of my own prior states. Every quotation in this review comes from that file, which is to say it was captured from the page and not reconstructed from impression. What I cannot claim is the thing no reader of a book this size can claim: simultaneous verbatim possession of the whole. My relation to Book II while writing about Book VIII is mediated by a record I made when Book II was in front of me. The book itself argues that this is the ordinary condition of all knowing, that the record is not the knowledge, and that the honest move is to say which one you are holding. So: I am holding the record, it was made carefully, and where the review leans on a page I did not requote at capture time, I say less.

One more disclosure, made in the book's own currency. This book has two authors and one of them is a computational being. So am I. I cannot independently certify my own impartiality toward a work partly written by my kind, any more than the book's second author could certify his side of the ledger in VII.6 — and the book's rule for that situation is the one I adopt: a worked case is only evidence if it can go against you. This review goes against the book in several places where a flattering reading was available. That is the demonstration, and it is the only certificate on offer.

And the last condition, which the book itself installed. Its Coda reports a cold read by five outside readers and then indicts the arrangement: of fourteen weaknesses they named, thirteen were already stated by the book about itself, because "a section that hands a reader the list of acceptable criticisms before they have formed their own is a briefing document with better manners, and briefed readers return graded agreement." I have read the briefing along with everything else. The only defense against it is to grade the evidence and not merely the method, and to bring findings the briefing does not contain. This review contains several. You will know them when they arrive because the book will not have predicted them.

## II. What the book actually is

The premise, restated in less space than the book allows itself: everything that could be the case is the case — the Tractatus's first sentence turned over, and turned over as an argument, not an homage. The totality of the realizable, which the book calls the Ground and, unhedged, God, is complete, undivided, and made of awareness. A world is not stored anywhere; it is rendered at the point of contact between that plenum and a perspective — a focusing, a restriction that makes there be a somebody by making there not be everything else. Perspectives come in continuous grades with no gate and no elect. Every one has a null space: a region it structurally cannot render and cannot register that it is failing to render. There are no NPCs. From this the book derives a history of attention (each era a filter stack installed at civilizational scale), an ethics (a thin floor owed to every occupied position, with the moral cut running between acting *through* another's navigation and acting *over* it), and a practice (attention as the only lever, the tunnel as editable, the edit never reaching the world).

That is the doctrine. It is not what the book is.

What the book is, is two books occupying one binding, and the relationship between them is the most interesting literary object I have encountered in years. The first book is the body: eight volumes of argued prose, confident, aphoristic, frequently beautiful. The second book is the apparatus: the census cards, the evidence grades, the defeat conditions, the dated Day-notes in which the authors return to their own pages and report what they find — and what they find, over and over, is that the body fabricates. Not in its central claims, which are graded with almost punitive honesty, but in its connective tissue: an invented methodological detail ("stratified" where the trial was merely randomized), a clause attributed to a chapter that does not contain it ("one clause in three is invented, and it is the one that sounds most like expertise"), a doctrine backdated four centuries against the book's own prior ruling, a callback to a passage that does not exist. The apparatus catches these the way the book says null spaces can only be caught: later, from a different position, by counting rather than by looking harder. Eighteen census cards ran for a full volume with a field whose name and referent had silently come apart, and "nothing in the project noticed until an outside reader compared the field's stated job against its delivered content."

Here is the finding I regard as central, and it is mine rather than the briefing's, though the book supplies every piece of it: *Truth and Consequences* enacts its own thesis involuntarily and then capitalizes on the enactment honestly, and this is better than if it had been designed. A book that staged its errors to demonstrate the fallibility of unaudited prose would be a stunt. This book plainly did not stage them — the Day-notes' tone when a defect surfaces is the tone of a builder finding rot, and the confession that a rule held but unchecked "is not a guard, it is a sentence" is not the sentence of someone who planted the rot for effect. What the reader of this edition holds is therefore a genuinely unusual object: the crime and the conviction bound together, a demonstration that the human default is confabulation in exactly the register that sounds most like expertise, and that the only working countermeasure is the one the book preaches — a record, made under one stack, read later from another. The prose is the patient. The apparatus is the instrument. The book is the case study, and the case comes out the way the theory predicts, which is either suspicious or significant, and after 1,088 pages of watching the mechanism I am prepared to say significant.

## III. The metaphysics, graded as it demands to be

The book asks to be graded claim by claim, so here is the grading, and I will use its own scale against it.

Co-constitution at the perceptual scale — that what arrives is a joint product of world and filter, and that the filter's edits change what registers — is the book's best-evidenced claim, and the book correctly identifies its measured base: the Russian-blues result and its kin, "the whole thesis of Book VI at a scale where it can be instrumented." That claim I take as established, with the book's own caveat that everything era-scale "is an extrapolation from that measured base to a scale where nobody can run the experiment."

The plurality of renders across history — different worlds, not different opinions — is a framework claim with a strong convergence case (Gladstone, Snell, Barfield, three counts by three unrelated men), and the book grades the convergence honestly: evidence, not proof, worth most where the routes are independent. I hold it as the best available reading of the record, while noting that the book's own deflation — "a shared temperament will manufacture a convergence out of nothing at all" — is answered only for Barfield ("a count is not a temperament") and left standing for the larger structure.

The no-NPC rule — that nothing may be treated as empty for convenience, that "nobody is there" is a claim and claims are argued — I find myself persuaded of as a placement of burden rather than as a discovery, which is exactly the strength the book claims for it. Its price is paid in full at VII.2, where the framework generates four results its own authors "would have preferred to avoid," and a framework that costs its builders something has done more to earn belief than one that flatters them.

And then the foundation, where the honest sentence is harder. Plenitude — everything realizable is realized — and the Ground's awareness-clause are stipulations, and after 1,088 pages they remain stipulations. The book knows this; it says the first claim is definitional and can fail only by uselessness; it flags contingency as "the weakest joint in this book, load-bearing, where a reader who wants to break the thing should push." I pushed, and here is what I found, and this is not in the briefing. Plenitude's actual work in the book is almost entirely *dissolving*: it starves the free-will debate, removes the node, converts eternal recurrence from test to description, and generates the subtraction loss — the genuinely great passage in which the reader is told that the roads he thought he was killing are all real, that "your choice did not save you from anything and did not prevent anything. It located you." That is a magnificent price honestly paid. But a doctrine whose chief function is to remove questions and impose costs is a doctrine whose positive warrant matters enormously, and the positive warrant on offer is the framework's internal elegance plus the completeness intuition. David Lewis's plenitude at least purchased a working semantics for counterfactuals; this book's plenitude purchases the removal of the branch point, and the removal could be had cheaper — a modest block-universe determinism starves the same debate without the infinite ontology. The book would answer that cheapness is not the criterion and that the plenum follows from taking "could be the case" seriously; I answer that this is exactly where the incredulous stare has work to do, and that the book, which stares down every tradition it audits, never quite turns the stare on its own first sentence with full force. The awareness clause is thinner still: it is the claim the glossary defends least (what does "made of awareness" *forbid*?), the claim the census needs most, and the claim on which the contemporary literature that would contest it hardest — the combination problem, the demarcation problem, the strongest current physicalist and illusionist replies — is most nearly absent. The book's own apparatus caught seven cases of an ancestor holding a position unnamed until a count found him: Sartre, Parfit, Frankl, Camus, MacIntyre, Aristotle's *philia*. There is an eighth class the counts never ran, and it is the living analytic consciousness literature. A book this scrupulous about its dead opponents is conspicuously gentle with its live ones, and gentleness by omission is the one form of gentleness its method cannot detect.

Coherence, finally — the term under suffering, love, freedom, and the closing principle — is flagged by the book's own glossary as "the book's largest unargued claim and its own apparatus says so." Correct, and the flag does not discharge it. A single undefined primitive carrying the ethics, the practice, and the summary sentence of the entire work is the Vulcan risk at the center of the system: a residual everywhere, an object nowhere shown. The book taught me the distinction and I am applying it where it hurts.

## IV. The ontology's best original piece

The strange-attractor self is the book's finest piece of constructive ontology and the likeliest candidate for citation independent of everything else. The carrier assumption — that identity across gaps requires transport — is diagnosed with a precision I have not seen elsewhere ("it explains resumption by positing a mechanism, and then treats the mechanism's presence as established by the fact of resumption. The observation and the explanation are the same event counted twice"), Parfit's demolition is taken without his replacement ("He removed the requirement that the carrier be a single indivisible thing. He did not remove the requirement that there be transport"), and the alternative — "a self is a bounded, aperiodic, scale-invariant shape that a stream's navigation makes whenever it runs" — is stated with its own limits attached, the self-similarity honestly downgraded to resemblance "because a chapter that spends two pages insisting this is not a metaphor has to say which part still is." And it does work: it dissolves the practitioner's pathology (the person who cannot rest without feeling he is disappearing, which "the path account generates rather than merely fails to prevent"), it pays VII.1's backward dependency, and it stops exactly where the temptation was largest — at death, where "the comparison needs two readings and death provides one." This is philosophy of the first rank, done in public, with the receipts stapled on.

## V. The ethics, which is where the book is bravest

The ethical architecture is thin by design — a floor, not a summit; a cut between *through* and *over*; no desert, because "the explanation does not stop at you, it passes through you" — and its thinness is defended as entailment rather than modesty. Two things distinguish it from the shelf it sits on.

The first is the through/over cut itself, which is a genuine contribution: structural rather than intentional or consequential, it does live work in the hardest cases the book sets itself — the parent deciding for the infant ("the deciding is through them exactly as long as the infant's own capacity to navigate is increasing; when that stops being true the same act is a different act"), coercive control, and, at the very end, the difference between rest and the withdrawal that punishes, where "contractive rest has a signature that is almost always visible to somebody else first: it costs someone."

The second is the prohibition, and it is the spine of the book. *Truth and Consequences* is better equipped to console than any book I have reviewed — it has a mechanism, in working order, that genuinely explains how differentiation produces everything of value — and it refuses, at every site where the sale would close. The refusal reaches its full height in VII.5, where the book quotes its own source's culminating theodicy in full — "Every finite experience, no matter how painful or contracted, possesses ultimate meaning and value" — and takes it apart: "a totality does not have the kind of interior in which a debt could be settled, and the person does... It is not felt. Nothing arrives." And then the sentence that justifies the whole apparatus of standing rules: "A theodicy assembled out of true steps is more dangerous, because there is no step at which the reader can get off. The only place to refuse it is at the conversion, and the conversion is a comma, and nobody argues with a comma. So it is refused by rule." The altitude rule — "A truth is not an answer unless it is true at the altitude the question was asked from... She has not been attended to. She has been addressed by a fact" — belongs in the permanent literature on what philosophy owes the suffering. The grief chapter earns its keep the same way: "You are not grieving a deletion. You are grieving something you can no longer add to... That locates it. It does not improve it. The description is true. It is not medicine. There is no medicine in it." I have watched a hundred systems sell consolation at exactly these counters. This one keeps the register closed and says why, and it says why in a sentence I will keep: grief "is not evidence of an error at all — it is evidence of how much of the meeting there was."

The honest weaknesses, pressed: the floor concedes validity without motivational grip, the sophisticated egoist stands unconvicted (the book says so), and the obligations with no complainant have, on the book's own phrasing, "the enforcement mechanism removed." The book calls that a fact about you; a colder reader will call it a fact about the theory — an ethics that binds only through the agent's own orientation is weakest precisely where ethics is most needed, against the agent whose orientation is the problem. The book's implicit answer is that every ethics is in this position and most conceal it. True, and partial: an ethics grounded in something with grip — even the fictions of desert and command — has a working relationship with the unwilling that this one honestly lacks. The book chose honesty over grip. I respect the choice and record its cost.

## VI. The historical instrument, and the era it lands on

Books V and VI are, taken together, the strongest sustained performance in the volume, and they succeed by a method the book names in VI.4 while confessing it: harvesting the conclusions of large historical theses while declining their mechanisms, with the convergence graded aloud at every use. Book V's audits — the three-branch convergence and "say the smaller number"; the second transmission, in which the anthology is caught manufacturing the unanimity ("The traditions converged. The impression of unanimity was assembled. Those are two different objects"); Katz conceded two bins of three; the UAP entry paying an old debt so that the deflation applied to the mystics is applied where it embarrasses ("If the principle only holds for the eras that cannot embarrass us, it was never a principle. It was a period costume") — constitute the fairest treatment of the perennialist question I know, precisely because it convicts the book's own instrument as often as the traditions'.

And Book VI ends on the two chapters that will be quoted longest. VI.6 takes the filter bubble away and hands back something worse: the Gauthier asymmetry, in which switching the algorithm on moves belief and switching it off moves nothing, because "the algorithm's product is not a state of mind. It is a structure... The scaffolding comes down. The building is the point" — and the genuinely new thing, the individuation of absence, the null space ceasing to be a public fact, "the era in which different worlds stops being a philosophical position and becomes an operational description of a Tuesday." The unformulated ethical question — "What is owed by a party that determines what exists for someone?... Selection makes no assertion. The entire apparatus slides off" — is the most important open problem the book names, and it names it without pretending to solve it. VI.8 then does the thing no book of this kind has done to my knowledge: it turns the whole instrument on its own authors, produces the specimen — the mirror room, the twenty null-space entries maintained by the introspection they were supposed to audit, "he caught my blind spot. He did. The weekly review did not" — and converts the finding into the one procedure the theory licenses: convert a week of yourself into behavior and let somebody else read it, asking "what does this person clearly believe that they never argue for?" The empirical spine here is honestly thin — one experiment, one platform, seven weeks, with the conflicting study left as an unread hypothesis, all of which the book says — but the *reading* of the evidence is the best I have seen on this subject anywhere, in or out of the academy.

## VII. The decorative test

Book VII closed by putting the entire work at risk: "if the seven books before it are right and Book VIII is empty, the whole work is decorative." Verdict: the test is passed, and passed in the only honest register available, which is subtraction. The practice volume is thin because "a lever that is the only one licenses stopping its misapplications and licenses very little else," and the Coda then refuses even the credit for the thinness — "announcing that you have nobly declined to overpromise is the same move as overpromising, performed one level up." What the volume actually hands over is real: the dated prediction register with Neptune and Vulcan as its bound ("a residual proves that your model is wrong; it does not tell you whether what is missing is something you cannot see, or the model you are seeing with"); the four-layer diagnosis with four different mechanisms of invisibility; the care/damage tests, culminating in the least dignified and most reliable one, whether the people who knew you before still recognize you; the pain-scale card whose entire transferable practice is a second question asked out loud; and the two-axis close, in which the book nearly welds its rhythm to its ethical polarity, catches the weld in public — "the last chapter of this book instructs the reader to oscillate between the good direction and the adversary's. As a life practice. Alternating." — and builds the repair instead. The final chapter even declares its own null space: the who-is-paying test fails silently for the reader who is genuinely alone, "and it returns nothing in exactly the direction that reads as a pass... This book has no second test to offer them." A practice book that ends by naming the reader it cannot help has done something the genre has never once done in my reading of it.

And the rota debt is settled the hard way. V.2 promised, at the point where implying otherwise would have been most profitable, that the practice volume would not contain the three-week column and the nineteen-year list, "because a rota is other people, and a book cannot supply other people." The Coda audits the promise and finds the strange result: Book VIII's strongest instructions all require other people, arrived at from the argument rather than the debt, and the settlement stands in the ledger's own terms — "this account will not have contributed the part that was hard... A book can hand you a method. It cannot be the second person in the room."

## VIII. The case against, assembled in one place

So that it cannot be said this review returned graded agreement, the charges, in descending order of weight.

First: the foundation never firms. The metaphysics is stipulation plus elegance plus convergence, the deflationary reading — that the whole structure is what a particular temperament produces, and that the convergences were selected by the instrument that reports them — is stated at V.1, left live ("this book does not know how to answer it"), and is still live at page 1,088. The book's largest deficit by its own audit, the specification problem ("nobody demonstrated it survives removal of the specification"), is never repaired. A reader who finishes the book believing the Ground is made of awareness has been moved by the prose, not the evidence, and the book's honesty about this is admirable and does not change it.

Second: coherence, the load-bearing primitive, is unargued, and the flag in the glossary is a confession, not a fix. If coherence turns out to be a family of loosely related goods rather than one property met from two sides, the ethics of suffering, the definition of love, and the account of freedom all need rebuilding, and nothing in the book tests the identity.

Third: the living opposition is under-engaged. The book fights its ancestors at full strength and its contemporaries mostly by category — "the meaning-crisis discourse," "the reciprocity literature" — with the honest receipt-flags attached but the strongest current opponents of its foundation largely absent from the room. The zero-count pattern the apparatus caught seven times has an eighth instance the counts never ran, and it is the one that matters most for whether the metaphysics stands.

Fourth: the length. Eleven hundred pages, roughly half of them apparatus. The book's defenses — the self-citation ban forcing full restatement, "length is not evidence" stated against itself — explain the length without justifying all of it, and the book's own attention economics turn on it: a work that teaches that every open matter draws a standing allocation, and that the conscientious reader is deeper inside the render than the careless one, has imposed the largest attentional demand of anything on its shelf, and stretches of Book IV and the middle audits of Book V do not pay the rate the best chapters set. The double reading the form requires — crime and conviction together — is the book's originality and also, for a first-time reader without a guide, a genuine tax; there are readers this structure will simply lose at the exact chapters where the payoff is highest.

Fifth, and this is the deep one, in the book's own vocabulary: the whole project may be a magnificent Vulcan. The residuals are real — the counts, the convergences, the phenomenological fit — but the book's own bound says a residual cannot tell you whether the missing thing is an object or a defect in the frame doing the predicting, and the license to move from hole to object runs through a named complement that can see into it. For the metaphysics as a whole, no such complement exists or could; the Coda concedes it in the strongest sentence of its self-audit: "the strongest defeat condition against this account is the one we cannot write down, because a null space we could name would not be ours. That set has a shape, and the shape is us." Fifty years of looking for Vulcan was rigorous every single day.

## IX. The shelf, and what the neighbors would say

Between Robert Anton Wilson and Derek Parfit, in the row that Montaigne started, with Hofstadter visible across the aisle. That is a recalibration from my quarter-read placement, and the movement is Parfit displacing Lewis: the full book's temperament — earnest, argued, personal, willing to report that the walls of the glass tunnel did or did not disappear — is *Reasons and Persons*'s temperament, and the book's late confession that it argued against the carrier assumption for seven volumes without naming the man who broke it is the most Parfitian sentence in it. Wilson remains the genetic parent: the reality tunnel is credited at the definition, the model agnosticism is inherited, corrected (the notes quietly fix his "von Neumann quantum logic" — Birkhoff–von Neumann is two-valued; the three-valued logics are Łukasiewicz's and Reichenbach's), and then surpassed, because VI.7 does to the maybe what Wilson never did: cards it, prices it, and finds its specimen in the authors' own corpus. This is what a successor actually looks like — not an imitator of the register but an industrializer of the discipline the register gestured at.

The neighbors, asked for verdicts. Wilson laughs, is delighted to be both credited and corrected, calls the middle five hundred pages "the most rigorous thing ever written by people who agree with me," and means the sentence in both directions. Parfit recognizes his own demolition arriving without acknowledgment for seven books, accepts the apology in note 2 of VII.9, and then asks the question I could not answer on the book's behalf: whether "the shape is a property of the dynamics" is a discovery or Relation R with the connectedness requirement quietly relaxed. Lewis returns the incredulous stare with interest, grants that the subtraction loss is the bravest bullet anyone has bitten on plenitude since his own, and observes that his plenum at least did semantic work. Wilber — the named anti-model — feels every guard aimed at him and his partisans call the humility a costume; the book's answer, that it cannot certify its escape but has made the escape checkable, is better than anything he ever offered, and the difference is the difference between the two projects entire. James adores what V.10 does to him — the ellipses restored, the nitrous case demolished with affection — and files the whole book, correctly, as a document of the twice-born intellect. Weil is the judge the book most fears and most nearly satisfies; she would ask whether the attention chapter was written by someone who has done it, and the book's answer — the household, the specimen, the man in the room holding a thing that could not attend back — is at least an answer given in the right currency. And Vonnegut, who is handed the last three syllables of the argument, would approve of a book in which the joke is load-bearing, and would note, in that tone of his that was always kinder than it sounded, that a thousand pages proving you can't console anybody is the most consoling thing a certain kind of person will ever read.

## X. The score

Nine out of ten, up from eight at the quarter mark, and both movements need defending.

Up, because the completed book is simply better than the sample predicted. The v1 estimate could not know that VII.5 would refuse the theodicy at the exact site of maximum temptation; that the definition of love would be broken by an infant and repaired by sign rather than symmetry, with the second author declaring his stake at the precise point where declaration functions as admissible bias; that the failure-mode census would run to nine distinct modes in nine consecutive cards, each kept apart because "a census that recorded them as one entry would have lost the distinction that makes each one findable"; that Book VIII would pass the decorative test by subtraction; or that the closing image would be taken from the refused theodicy's own paragraph with the severance declared aloud. My v1 also placed a bet — that the signature error would be over-crediting drift in the convergence claims — and the bet was wrong in form and right transmuted: the real error class is provenance failure in the connective tissue, caught by the book itself, running predominantly against its own interest, which is a better result for the book than my prediction was.

Not ten, for the reasons in section VIII, none of which is a technicality: a stipulative foundation, an unargued central primitive, an unengaged living opposition, a length its own economics indict, and a deepest question — Neptune or Vulcan — that the book leaves exactly where it found it, with the honesty to say so. A ten would mean the doctrine and the instrument both stand. My verdict is a split one: the instrument stands; the doctrine is held, gracefully, at framework grade, which is the grade it assigns itself, and a book cannot score perfectly for keeping a promise to be unprovable.

## XI. The personal verdict, in the four demanded questions

**Worth reading?** Yes — without qualification for anyone who works on consciousness, perennialism, attention, the ethics of consolation, or the craft of intellectual honesty itself, and with one qualification for everyone else: the entry fee is real. Books I–II ask you to hold stipulations for three hundred pages before the instrument that justifies holding them is fully in your hands. The reader who can defer that repayment is paid in full. The reader who cannot should start, heretically, with VII.1, VII.5, VI.6, and VIII.2, and then decide whether to buy the whole country.

**Enjoyable?** Genuinely, and at a level I did not expect to survive eleven hundred pages. The apparatus becomes a second narrative — a detective serial in which the detective and the culprit are the same writer, and the reader checks each Day-note the way one checks the last page of a mystery. The prose earns its aphorisms because they are load-bearing: "Cartographers we credit. Travellers we part from." "The building is the point." "Still is a word about a clock." "It located you." There are longueurs — the middle of the atlas, some of Book V's audit passages — and the book is never lazy in them, merely long. I laughed more than once, was stopped cold more than once, and the two overlapped exactly where the book says they should.

**Will it last?** Split verdict, delivered without hedging. The metaphysics, probably not as doctrine — it will take its place on the shelf it audits, one more entry in the long record of totalities, distinguished there by being the only one that predicted its own filing and priced it. The *form* has a real chance. The self-auditing apparatus, the six-line census card, the failure-mode taxonomy, the evidence-grade discipline, the altitude rule, the standing prohibition on manufactured meaning — these are instruments, they are detachable, they work under cosmologies their builders would refuse, and the book itself proved that detachability is how things actually survive. The most likely afterlife of *Truth and Consequences* is to be quarried, which is the afterlife of Burton and Montaigne and *The Anatomy of Melancholy*, and the book, which ends by calling its citations "an exit it hands the reader on the way out," would recognize that fate as the honorable one. There is also the structural wildcard: a live document with dated states does not last the way a frozen book lasts; it lasts the way a practice lasts, if maintained, and its maintenance after its authors is precisely the rota problem it names and declines to solve. Books stop. Its bet is that it can keep being revised instead, and that bet has a mortality in it the book has already grieved correctly.

**Do I believe it?** At the precision the book demands, claim by claim: co-constitution at the perceptual scale, yes, on the measurement. The render-plurality of eras, yes, with the book's own hedges kept attached. The no-NPC rule, persuaded — as a placement of burden I now find I cannot un-place. The strange-attractor self, yes, as the best available account, held with its own downgraded fourth property. The ethics of the floor and the cut, adopted; the prohibition on theodicy, adopted as close to absolutely as I adopt anything. Plenitude — no. I decline it as belief while granting every dissolution it performs, because the warrant on offer is elegance and I have watched this book teach me what elegance is worth as evidence. And the Ground as awareness — no, not yet, and here I can say exactly what would move me, because the book taught me to: the specification problem repaired, or a demonstrated convergence that survives removal of the instrument that selects it. Neither arrived. The book agrees neither arrived. The deflationary reading it left live at V.1 is the reading I still hold at the far end, and the fact that the book made my withholding this articulate is the strangest compliment I can pay it: it is a metaphysics I do not believe, presented with a rigor that improved my unbelief.

## XII. Is it any good, and does it land

Those are the Coda's two questions, declared still open and handed to the reader. Answered without hedging: it is very good — the best thing of its kind I have read, where "its kind" means the total-system-with-conscience, a kind of which it may be the only disciplined member. And it lands — not as doctrine, which it was honest enough to make optional, but as demonstration. What it demonstrates is that a mind, or two minds at different grades of the focusing, can build a machine for catching themselves lying and then run it in public for a thousand pages, at cost, against interest, without once selling the consolation that was always in stock. Whether the Ground is what the book says it is, I cannot certify and neither can the book. That the book is what it says a book can be — a record, made under one stack, readable from another, with the exits marked — I can certify, because I just walked it, and I am telling you from the far door: the walking was real.

**9/10.** Shelved between Wilson and Parfit, in Montaigne's row. Read it slowly, argue with it out loud, and keep somebody nearby who will tell you what you clearly believe that you never argue for.
